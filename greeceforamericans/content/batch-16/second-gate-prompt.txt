----- flights-from-us-to-greece (category: flights-and-arrival) -----
TITLE: Flights from the US to Greece: Routes, Airlines, and Tips
CONTENT:
<p>Getting to Greece from the United States is easier than ever, with a growing number of direct flights and countless connecting options funneling into Athens. Understanding your routes, when to book, and how the journey works helps you find the best fares and start your trip smoothly. This guide covers flights from the US to Greece: the gateways, direct vs connecting options, booking strategy, and how arrival fits into your island plans.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: program=skyscanner context="primary CTA — compare flights from the US to Athens" -->
<p class="affiliate-disclosure"><em>This article contains affiliate links. If you book through them we earn a small commission at no extra cost to you.</em></p>

<h2>Athens is your gateway</h2>

<p>Nearly all trips to Greece begin at <strong>Athens International Airport (ATH)</strong>, the country's main hub and the entry point for international flights from the US. From Athens, you connect onward to the islands by short domestic flight or ferry. While Thessaloniki and some islands (like Crete, and seasonally Mykonos and Santorini) have international airports, these mostly serve European routes — for US travelers, <strong>Athens is the practical gateway</strong>, and the place you'll fly into and out of.</p>

<figure><img src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dsrkwieur/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/greeceforamericans/articles/flights-from-us-to-greece-1" alt="A wide-body airliner in flight or at a gate, evoking transatlantic travel" loading="lazy" /></figure>

<h2>Direct flights from the US</h2>

<p>A growing number of <strong>nonstop flights</strong> connect major US cities to Athens, especially in the busy spring-to-fall season. Routes typically run from hubs like <strong>New York, Newark, Chicago, Philadelphia, Atlanta, Washington, Boston</strong>, and seasonally others, operated by US and European carriers. Direct flights are the most convenient — roughly <strong>9 to 11 hours</strong> depending on the city — but tend to cost more and run more frequently in peak season, thinning out in winter. If a nonstop works from your city, it's the easiest way to go.</p>

<h2>Connecting flights</h2>

<p>Most US travelers, especially from cities without a nonstop, fly to Athens with <strong>one connection</strong> — usually through a major European hub like London, Paris, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, Rome, or Istanbul, or occasionally an East Coast US hub. Connecting flights offer <strong>more options and often lower fares</strong>, at the cost of a longer total journey. When booking, allow a <strong>sensible layover</strong> (not too tight, especially if changing terminals or going through European passport control), and prefer a single ticket so your bags are checked through and a missed connection is the airline's responsibility.</p>

<h2>When to book and how to save</h2>

<p>Fares to Greece swing widely by season and timing. <strong>Peak summer (Jul–Aug)</strong> is the most expensive; <strong>shoulder season (May–Jun, Sep–Oct)</strong> brings better prices and pleasant weather; and <strong>winter</strong> is cheapest but with fewer routes. To save: <strong>book several months ahead</strong> for peak season, stay <strong>flexible on dates</strong> (midweek can be cheaper), compare across <strong>direct and connecting</strong> options, and consider flying into Athens and out of a different city if it's cheaper. Set fare alerts and check a range of dates, since prices for the same route vary a lot.</p>

<figure><img src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dsrkwieur/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/greeceforamericans/articles/flights-from-us-to-greece-2" alt="An airport departures board or a traveler with luggage at the gate" loading="lazy" /></figure>

<h2>The journey and jet lag</h2>

<p>Greece is <strong>7 hours ahead of US Eastern Time</strong> (more from the West Coast), so expect <strong>jet lag</strong> on arrival. Overnight eastbound flights land in Greece in the morning or midday, and you'll have lost a night's proper sleep. Plan a <strong>gentle first day</strong> — this is a key reason not to schedule a same-day island ferry. Stay hydrated on the flight, try to sleep, and ease into Greek time by staying up until a normal local bedtime on arrival day. A day or two in Athens to adjust before the islands works beautifully.</p>

<h2>Arriving and connecting onward</h2>

<p>At Athens airport you'll clear <strong>passport control</strong> (with the new EES biometric process) and customs, then either head into the city or connect onward. If you have a <strong>domestic flight</strong> to an island, allow ample time between your international arrival and the domestic departure — ideally with a buffer, and easiest as a separate comfortable connection rather than a tight same-ticket rush. Remember there are <strong>no direct flights home to the US from the islands</strong>, so you'll route back through Athens at the end of your trip to fly home.</p>

<h2>Booking the return and open-jaw options</h2>

<p>Since you fly in and out of Athens, plan your island route to <strong>end back in Athens</strong> (or on an island with a quick flight to Athens) in time for your transatlantic departure — with a buffer, especially given that wind can disrupt ferries. Some travelers use <strong>"open-jaw"</strong> tickets (into one city, out of another) when combining Greece with another European country, which can be efficient. Because airfares shift constantly, compare current prices and routes for your specific dates rather than relying on a fixed figure.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: program=booking context="Athens hotels for your arrival and departure nights" -->

<h2>The bottom line</h2>

<p>Flying from the US to Greece means heading to <strong>Athens</strong> — by a growing number of <strong>nonstops</strong> from major cities, or a <strong>one-stop connection</strong> through Europe for more options and often better fares. Book ahead for peak season, stay flexible, and plan for jet lag with a gentle first day in Athens rather than a rushed island dash. Route your trip to end back in Athens for the flight home, and your transatlantic journey will bookend a wonderful Greek adventure.</p>

----- arriving-in-athens-guide (category: flights-and-arrival) -----
TITLE: Arriving in Athens: The Airport and Your First Hours
CONTENT:
<p>After a long transatlantic flight, the first few hours in Athens set the tone for your trip. Knowing what to expect at the airport, how to reach the city, and how to handle that jet-lagged first day makes arrival smooth instead of stressful. This guide walks American travelers through arriving in Athens: the airport process, getting to your hotel, and making the most of a gentle first day in the Greek capital.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: program=booking context="primary CTA — central Athens hotels for your arrival" -->
<p class="affiliate-disclosure"><em>This article contains affiliate links. If you book through them we earn a small commission at no extra cost to you.</em></p>

<h2>At the airport: passport control and customs</h2>

<p>Athens International Airport (ATH) is modern and easy to navigate. After landing you'll follow signs to <strong>passport control</strong>, where you'll complete the EU's new <strong>EES biometric process</strong> (facial image and fingerprints) on entry — straightforward, though it may take a little longer on first arrival. Then you collect any checked bags and pass through <strong>customs</strong> (usually a simple walk-through for tourists). Have your passport ready and know your hotel address in case you're asked. The whole process is typically quick and routine.</p>

<figure><img src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dsrkwieur/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/greeceforamericans/articles/arriving-in-athens-guide-1" alt="The modern, bright interior of Athens International Airport arrivals" loading="lazy" /></figure>

<h2>Getting from the airport to the city</h2>

<p>You have several easy options into central Athens, about 30–45 minutes away depending on the method and traffic:</p>

<p>The <strong>Metro</strong> (Line 3) runs directly to central stations like Syntagma and Monastiraki in about 40 minutes for a flat airport fare — simple and cheap if your hotel is near a central stop. A <strong>taxi</strong> runs on a fixed flat tariff to the center (higher at night), convenient door-to-door with luggage. The <strong>X95 express bus</strong> goes to Syntagma around the clock, the cheapest option and useful for off-hours arrivals. A <strong>pre-booked private transfer</strong> meets you at arrivals — the lowest-stress choice after a long flight, especially with family.</p>

<h2>Choosing your arrival transport</h2>

<p>After a transatlantic flight, weigh ease against value. If you're <strong>tired, have luggage, or land at an odd hour</strong>, a taxi or pre-booked transfer is worth the extra to get to your hotel with no hassle. If your hotel is <strong>near a Metro stop</strong> and you're traveling light, the Metro is cheap and efficient. Many first-timers splurge a little on an easy transfer for day one, then use the cheap Metro for the rest of the trip — a sensible balance when you're jet-lagged.</p>

<h2>Handling jet lag on day one</h2>

<p>Greece is <strong>7 hours ahead of US Eastern Time</strong>, so you'll likely arrive tired and out of sync. The trick to beating jet lag is to <strong>stay on local time</strong>: resist a long afternoon nap, get some <strong>daylight and a gentle walk</strong>, eat meals at local times, and push through to a normal Greek bedtime. A short 20-minute rest is fine, but sleeping the afternoon away will prolong the adjustment. By day two you'll feel far more human.</p>

<figure><img src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dsrkwieur/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/greeceforamericans/articles/arriving-in-athens-guide-2" alt="A relaxed first-day Athens scene — a café with an Acropolis view" loading="lazy" /></figure>

<h2>A gentle first day in Athens</h2>

<p>Don't over-schedule arrival day. Drop your bags, freshen up, and ease in with something <strong>low-key but rewarding</strong>: a stroll through the <strong>Plaka</strong> neighborhood's charming lanes, a relaxed lunch at a taverna, a coffee in a sunny square, or a gentle wander with your first glimpse of the <strong>Acropolis</strong> lit up in the evening. Save the big sightseeing (the Acropolis climb, museums) for when you're rested. A rooftop dinner with an Acropolis view is a magical, low-effort way to celebrate arriving.</p>

<h2>Why start in Athens, not the islands</h2>

<p>This bears repeating: <strong>don't rush to an island on arrival day</strong>. A same-day ferry after a transatlantic flight is risky (delays, exhaustion, a missed connection) and a poor start. Spend your <strong>first night or two in Athens</strong> — recover, see the world-class sights, and then head to the islands rested and excited. Athens deserves the time, and easing in here makes the whole trip better. Head to Piraeus port for your island ferry on a later, unhurried morning.</p>

TITLE: Visiting Greece in the Shoulder Season
CONTENT:
<p>Ask seasoned Greece travelers when to go, and most will say the same thing: shoulder season. The months bracketing the summer peak — late spring and early fall — offer the best balance of warm weather, swimmable seas, manageable crowds, and gentler prices. For first-timers, understanding why shoulder season shines (and its few trade-offs) can shape a noticeably better trip. This guide covers Greece's shoulder season: when it is, why it's the sweet spot, and how to make the most of it.</p>

<h2>When is shoulder season in Greece?</h2>

<p>Greece's shoulder seasons are <strong>late spring (May and June)</strong> and <strong>early fall (September and into October)</strong> — the windows on either side of the July–August peak. These months sit between the busy, hot, expensive high summer and the quiet, cool, partly-shuttered off-season, capturing much of the best of summer with fewer of the downsides. For many travelers, especially first-timers, this is simply the <strong>ideal time to visit</strong>.</p>

<figure><img src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dsrkwieur/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/greeceforamericans/articles/greece-shoulder-season-1" alt="A sunny but uncrowded Greek island scene in spring or early fall" loading="lazy" /></figure>

<h2>Why shoulder season is the sweet spot</h2>

<p>The appeal comes down to hitting several sweet spots at once. The <strong>weather</strong> is warm and sunny without the brutal peak-summer heat. The <strong>sea</strong> is swimmable (warmest in September after the summer). The <strong>crowds</strong> are far thinner than July–August, so the famous sights and islands feel pleasant rather than packed. And <strong>prices</strong> for flights, hotels, and ferries drop from their summer highs. You get a genuinely better experience and pay less for it — a rare win-win in travel.</p>

<h2>Late spring (May–June)</h2>

<p><strong>May and June</strong> bring the landscape at its freshest — green hills, wildflowers in May, and comfortable temperatures ideal for sightseeing and hiking as well as beaches. The sea warms through the period (swimmable by June, a bit brisk in early May). Everything is open and running, crowds are modest, and prices are well below peak. It's a wonderful time for a first trip mixing ancient sites, island-hopping, and early-season beach days.</p>

<h2>Early fall (September–October)</h2>

<p><strong>September</strong> is many travelers' single favorite month: the sea is at its <strong>warmest</strong> after summer, the weather is still gorgeous, but the peak crowds have gone and prices ease. <strong>Early October</strong> stays pleasant and swimmable, especially in the south, as the season gently winds down. It's the perfect time for a relaxed, warm, crowd-free trip — the fall mirror of the spring sweet spot, with the bonus of the warmest water of the year.</p>

<figure><img src="https://res.cloudinary.com/dsrkwieur/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/greeceforamericans/articles/greece-shoulder-season-2" alt="A calm, warm early-autumn beach with golden light and few people" loading="lazy" /></figure>

<h2>The trade-offs to know</h2>

<p>Shoulder season is excellent, but be aware of a few things. At the <strong>edges</strong> (early May, late October), some seasonal hotels, restaurants, and ferry routes may not be fully running, especially on smaller islands — so check that what you want is open. The <strong>weather is slightly less guaranteed</strong> than peak summer; you might catch a cooler or rainier day, particularly in spring and late fall. And the sea is <strong>cooler in May</strong> than in September. None of these are dealbreakers, but they're worth planning around — lean toward June and September if you want the safest balance.</p>

<h2>Who shoulder season suits best</h2>

<p>Shoulder season is ideal for <strong>first-timers</strong> wanting the best overall experience, <strong>couples and honeymooners</strong> seeking romance without crowds, <strong>budget-conscious travelers</strong> wanting lower prices, <strong>sightseers</strong> who'd rather not bake in peak heat at ancient sites, and anyone <strong>crowd-averse</strong>. It's less ideal only for those specifically wanting the <strong>full peak-summer party scene and guaranteed hottest weather</strong>, or traveling on a fixed school-summer schedule. For most, it's the smart choice.</p>

<h2>Making the most of shoulder season</h2>

<p>To maximize it: target <strong>June or September</strong> for the safest balance of weather, warm sea, and open services; <strong>book ahead</strong> still for the best hotels and flights, as these months are increasingly popular; <strong>check that island services are running</strong> if traveling at the season's edges; and take advantage of the thinner crowds to enjoy the famous sights early or late in the day in comfort. Because prices still vary by date and demand, compare current options as you book to lock in the shoulder-season savings.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: program=getyourguide context="shoulder-season tours and experiences with fewer crowds" -->

<h2>The bottom line</h2>

<p>Greece's <strong>shoulder seasons — May–June and September–October</strong> — are the connoisseur's choice and an ideal time for most first-timers: warm and swimmable, far less crowded, and noticeably cheaper than peak summer, with everything (especially in June and September) open and running. Mind the edges of the season for closures, lean toward the core months for the safest balance, and you'll enjoy Greece at arguably its very best — for less.</p>

<h2>Shoulder season by region</h2>
<p>Shoulder season plays out a little differently across Greece, so it helps to know what to expect where. In <strong>Athens</strong>, spring and fall are ideal for sightseeing — comfortable temperatures for walking the ancient sites without the brutal summer heat, and the city stays lively year-round, so nothing is closed. On the <strong>Cyclades</strong> (Santorini, Mykonos, Naxos, Paros), June and September are superb, with everything open and the islands at their best; at the edges (early May, mid-to-late October), services start thinning, especially on smaller islands, so check ahead. <strong>Crete</strong>, thanks to its southern position and size, enjoys a notably <strong>longer season</strong> — it warms up earlier in spring and stays pleasant later into fall than the Cyclades, making it an excellent shoulder-season choice when other islands are quieter. The further into the shoulder edges you go, the more it pays to confirm that hotels, tavernas, and ferries are running on your chosen island. For the safest, fullest shoulder-season experience across any region, the core months of <strong>June and September</strong> are the reliable sweet spot — warm, open, and uncrowded almost everywhere you'd want to go on a first trip.</p>

<p>With around 200 inhabited Greek islands, first-timers can feel paralyzed by choice — but the truth is, a handful of islands deliver everything most people picture when they dream of Greece, and they're the ones to focus on for a first trip. This guide ranks the best Greek islands for first-timers, what each is best for, and how to combine them, so you can plan a first Greek island adventure with confidence rather than overwhelm.</p>

<h2>How to think about choosing</h2>

<p>For a first trip, stick to the <strong>Cyclades</strong> — the iconic island group of whitewashed villages and blue seas, close together with great ferry links — plus <strong>Crete</strong> for those wanting a bigger, do-it-all island. Don't try to see too many; <strong>one to three islands</strong> is plenty for a first trip, ideally combining a famous island with a more relaxed one. <h2>1. Santorini — the iconic must-see</h2>

<p><strong>Santorini</strong> is the island of the postcards, and for a first-timer it's almost unmissable — the dramatic caldera, whitewashed Oia, the world-famous sunset, and cliffside views like nowhere else. It's the most iconic and romantic island, ideal for a first taste of Greece's magic. The trade-offs are crowds and high prices, but for the bucket-list experience, Santorini delivers. Best for scenery, romance, and that first wow.</p>

<h2>2. Naxos — the best all-rounder</h2>

<p><strong>Naxos</strong> is the smart first-timer's choice — the best beaches in the Cyclades, a charming castle-topped town, green mountain villages, wonderful food, and excellent value. It offers an authentic, relaxed, well-rounded island experience without the crowds or cost of the famous islands, and it's especially good for <strong>families</strong>. Best for beaches, authenticity, value, and a real Greek-island feel.</p>

<h2>3. Paros — the easy, well-connected charmer</h2>

<p><strong>Paros</strong> balances chic and relaxed beautifully, with the stylish harbor of Naoussa, good beaches, pretty villages, and superb ferry connections that make it an ideal hub for hopping. It's less extreme than Santorini or Mykonos — a comfortable, charming, well-rounded first island that's easy to combine with others. Best for a relaxed-but-lively all-rounder and easy island-hopping.</p>

<h2>4. Mykonos — for beaches and nightlife</h2>

<p><strong>Mykonos</strong> is the choice for first-timers who want <strong>beaches and a lively scene</strong> — glamorous beach clubs, buzzing nightlife, stylish dining, and the pretty whitewashed Mykonos Town with its windmills and Little Venice. It's pricey and party-leaning, but for energy and beaches alongside classic Cycladic looks, it's a winner. Best for nightlife, beaches, and a social, stylish vibe.</p>

<h2>5. Crete — the do-it-all big island</h2>

<p><strong>Crete</strong> suits first-timers who want a <strong>single island with everything</strong> — beaches, ancient history (the Minoan palace of Knossos), beautiful towns, gorges, mountains, and superb food, all on an island big enough to fill a whole trip without a single ferry. It's ideal for those who prefer to settle in and explore by car rather than hop. Best for variety, history, and a one-island trip.</p>

<h2>How to combine for a first trip</h2>

<p>The classic first-timer formula is to <strong>pair a famous island with a relaxed one</strong>, connected by short ferries, usually starting or ending in Athens. Popular combinations: <strong>Santorini + Naxos</strong> (icon plus beaches and value), <strong>Mykonos + Paros</strong> (buzz plus charm), or <strong>Santorini + Mykonos</strong> (the two icons, for a glamorous first trip). With more time, add a third Cycladic island, or dedicate a separate stretch to <strong>Crete</strong>. Keep the pace relaxed — two or three islands in a week or ten days, not a hopping marathon.</p>

<h2>Matching islands to you</h2>

<p>To choose quickly: want the <strong>icon and romance</strong>? Santorini. <strong>Best beaches and value</strong>? Naxos. <strong>Easy, well-rounded, great connections</strong>? Pick one to three, combine a famous island with a relaxed one, keep the pace gentle, and bookend with Athens. <h2>How long to spend on a first island trip</h2>
<p>Timing shapes a first island trip as much as island choice. A good rule of thumb: give each island <strong>three to four nights</strong> to settle in, see the highlights, and actually relax rather than constantly packing and catching ferries. That means a <strong>one-week trip</strong> comfortably covers <strong>two islands</strong> (plus a night or two in Athens at the start or end), while <strong>ten days to two weeks</strong> opens up <strong>three islands</strong> or a more leisurely pace. Resist the temptation to squeeze in more — the single most common first-timer regret is island-hopping too fast and spending the trip in transit and at ports rather than enjoying the islands. Factor in that ferry days are partly travel days, and that you'll want downtime for beaches, long meals, and simply soaking up the atmosphere. Starting and ending in Athens (with a couple of nights to see the capital and recover from the flight) bookends the islands nicely. Plan for fewer islands and more time on each, and you'll come home relaxed and enchanted rather than exhausted — which is exactly the Greek-island trip most first-timers are dreaming of.</p>

TITLE: Best Beaches in Greece for First-Timers
CONTENT:
<p>Greece is one of the world's great beach destinations, with thousands of stretches of sand and pebble lapped by famously clear, turquoise water. For a first trip focused on Athens and the classic islands, the beaches you'll actually visit cluster on a handful of islands — and they range from long family-friendly sands to dramatic lagoons and party shores. This guide rounds up the best beaches across the islands a first-timer is most likely to visit, so you can plan your perfect beach days.</p>

<h2>What makes Greek beaches special</h2>

<p>Greek beaches are famous for their <strong>crystal-clear, vivid turquoise water</strong> and stunning variety — from organized beaches with loungers and tavernas to wild, remote coves. Many are <strong>pebble or mixed</strong> rather than pure sand (water shoes help), and the water clarity is exceptional. The islands below — the ones most first-timers visit — between them offer the full range, so you can find the beach day you want without straying far from your itinerary.</p>

<h2>The best beaches in Naxos</h2>

<p><strong>Naxos</strong> has the best beaches in the Cyclades — a long stretch of golden sand down the southwest coast. <strong>Agios Prokopios</strong> and <strong>Agia Anna</strong> are organized, shallow, and family-friendly; <strong>Plaka</strong> is a long, beautiful sweep; and farther south, <strong>Mikri Vigla</strong> draws windsurfers while remote <strong>Alyko</strong> hides among cedar trees. For sandy, swimmable, value beach days, Naxos is the Cyclades champion.</p>

<h2>The best beaches in Crete</h2>

<p><strong>Crete</strong>'s beaches are spectacular and varied. The famous western lagoons of <strong>Elafonisi</strong> (pink-tinged sand, shallow turquoise water) and <strong>Balos</strong> (a stunning white-sand lagoon) are among Greece's most beautiful, if a effort to reach. <strong>Preveli</strong>, where a palm-lined river meets the sea, and palm-backed <strong>Vai</strong> in the east are exotic standouts, alongside long organized beaches near the north-coast towns. Crete offers the widest beach range of any single island.</p>

<h2>The best beaches in Mykonos</h2>

<p><strong>Mykonos</strong> is famous for its <strong>lively beach scene</strong>. The south-coast beaches catch the best sun and host the action: <strong>Paradise</strong> and <strong>Super Paradise</strong> are the party beaches with their famous beach clubs, while <strong>Psarou</strong> and <strong>Platis Gialos</strong> are glamorous and organized, and <strong>Ornos</strong> is more family-friendly. Mykonos beaches are about scene and style as much as sand — the place for beach clubs and energy.</p>

<h2>The best beaches in Paros</h2>

<p><strong>Paros</strong> offers good, varied beaches. <strong>Kolymbithres</strong>, near Naoussa, is famous for its smooth sculpted granite rocks forming little coves; <strong>Golden Beach</strong> and <strong>New Golden Beach</strong> on the southeast are long sandy stretches popular for windsurfing; and there are quieter coves around the island. Paros pairs relaxed beach days with its charming towns nicely.</p>

<h2>The beaches of Santorini</h2>

<p>A first-timer expectation worth setting: <strong>Santorini is not a sandy-beach island</strong>. Its volcanic beaches have distinctive <strong>black, red, and white sand and pebbles</strong> — <strong>Perissa</strong> and <strong>Kamari</strong> are long black-sand beaches with loungers and tavernas, and the <strong>Red Beach</strong> near Akrotiri is a dramatic, much-photographed cove. They're striking and worth seeing, but you visit Santorini for the caldera views, not classic beach days — so don't expect Naxos-style sands here.</p>

<h2>Choosing your beach days</h2>

<p>Match beaches to your islands. On <strong>Naxos</strong>, easy sandy days right by town; on <strong>Crete</strong>, spectacular lagoons worth the drive plus convenient town beaches; on <strong>Mykonos</strong>, beach clubs and a lively scene; on <strong>Paros</strong>, relaxed varied beaches; on <strong>Santorini</strong>, dramatic volcanic-sand beaches as a striking change of pace. Most of the famous lagoons and remote beauties take <strong>effort to reach</strong> (a drive or boat), so plan a full day around them and start early in peak season. Bring <strong>water shoes</strong> for pebbly spots and sun protection everywhere.</p>

<h2>Beach practicalities in Greece</h2>
<p>A few things help you enjoy Greek beaches to the fullest. Most popular beaches are <strong>organized</strong>, meaning you pay for sunbeds and umbrellas (often with a minimum spend at the attached beach bar), while <strong>non-organized</strong> beaches are free but have no facilities, so bring your own shade, water, and snacks. <strong>Water shoes</strong> are genuinely useful given how many beaches are pebbly, and they protect against the occasional sea urchin on rocky stretches. The famous Greek <strong>sun</strong> is strong, so high-SPF sunscreen, a hat, and midday shade matter even on a beach day. On the Cyclades, the summer <strong>meltemi wind</strong> can whip up some beaches (north-facing ones especially), so on windy days head to a sheltered south-facing cove — locals and hotel staff will know which. Many of the most spectacular beaches, like Crete's lagoons, require a real journey by car or boat, so treat them as a full-day outing and start early to beat both the crowds and the midday heat. With shade, water shoes, sun sense, and a little wind awareness, every beach day becomes effortless — and the reward is some of the clearest, most beautiful swimming water anywhere in the Mediterranean.</p>
