# Ruth Calloway reads Rome, Solved. — 2026-10-25, build 87 pages (+ 1-page Field Sheet)
Reader: 34B — American, 30–65, first trip to Rome, six weeks out, flight booked, **hotel not booked**. Paid **$14.99** on Etsy (LISTING.md). Reads on a phone in bed and a laptop at lunch; prints the wallet card at home in black and white. Thinks in dollars, miles, °F, 12-hour clock. No Italian. Has heard "skip the line" and "ZTL", can define neither. Afraid of being ripped off, losing a day, missing the must-see, looking like a tourist.            **Verdict: SHIP AFTER BLOCKERS**

*Rome, Solved.* is what it says: nine decisions a first-timer has to make, each with the call, the reasoning, and when to overrule it. They all come from one good idea, that the Rome you came to see is a district you can walk. For $14.99 the book is worth it on three decisions alone: the door-to-door airport math (D03), "two lines" at the Vatican (D05), and Ostia over Pompeii (D09). Any one of them saves a reader more than the price. The biggest problem is the other half of the purchase. The book defers every live number to the Field Sheet at least seven times: room rates, pass prices, the fare cap, booking fees, opening hours, train times, the exchange rate. The Field Sheet I was handed holds four numbers. The rest says "confirm" plus a web address, and the rows are misaligned, so some values sit beside the wrong labels. The second problem is the pictures. About a dozen show the wrong place, contradict the caption, or show the thing the text tells me to avoid. Three of them mislead on the exact number or place the decision turns on. None of this needs the argument rewritten. It needs the Field Sheet built and the pictures made to answer for themselves.

Scores (1–5): promise **2** · clarity **3** · logic **3** · pictures **2** · value **4** · trust **3** · usability **3**

- promise 2: the "figures you need on the ground" sheet is mostly empty, and the in-book promise of "what closes, what strikes" is not kept.
- logic 3: the calls are sound and consistent, but five figures disagree with their own text.
- pictures 2: 3 mislead, about 9 confuse, about 13 decorate. Only about 6 photos do a job the reader can name.
- value 4: the decision spine is real synthesis.
- trust 3: the marks system is excellent. Repeated charts, broken references and an empty sheet undo some of it.
- usability 3: the contents page, wallet card, p85 and p87 are excellent. Fifteen pages pass before the first decision, and the closers repeat their posters.

## What I would not change

- **The nine-decision spine with FOLLOW THIS IF / OVERRULE IT IF on every call.** That is the product, and it is honest. It tells me where my own situation should win.
- **Decision 03's door-to-door re-pricing.** The free web never makes this connection, and it is the one I'd quote to a friend. (Fix the chart's unit; keep the argument.)
- **Decision 05, "TWO LINES."** The poster passes in two seconds, and the ticket-line / security-line distinction will save real money at St Peter's.
- **Decision 09, Ostia over Pompeii.** The book's most valuable non-obvious call.
- **Fig. 1**, the walk-time ring. It is the thesis in one picture, and it convinced me on p11.
- **The four marks (● ◇ ▲ ◆)** and the line "a mark means this specific claim is checkable; its absence means this is our read." That's fact-checking made visible.
- **p40's table, the wallet card (p80), p85 "decide these first", p86 "what to ask before you pay", p87 "when the plan does not survive the day".** These are the pages 34B will actually use in Rome.
- **"What this book won't tell you" (p82).** The refusal to name restaurants and hotels, with reasons. It earns trust.
- **Day zero's permission to "do almost nothing well".** That's the right advice, and nobody else gives it.
- **The p53 taxi-rank photo and the p68 Forum photo.** These are pictures that prove something. The rest should be held to their standard.
- **The D07 poster (EUR / USD terminal)**: the clearest page in the book.
