Rome, Solved.
Six weeks before a trip to Rome you are not short of facts. You have
forty browser tabs, a dozen contradictory “best of” lists, and a Reddit
thread for every question you can think to ask. What you are short of is
the thing no single tab gives you: a competent voice that holds the
whole picture at once and says here is what actually matters, here is
the call, and here is how you’ll know if you disagree and are still right.
That is the whole of what this book is. It is not a guidebook: it will not
walk you through every church or rank the gelato. It is a manual of
decisions: the fifteen or so choices that decide whether a first Roman
trip feels effortless or exhausting, each one resolved with its reasoning
shown, so you can follow the call or overrule it on purpose. The facts
underneath are free and everywhere. The connections between them,
which is where nearly every expensive mistake actually hides, are what
we’ve done for you.
Everything here traces to a single organizing fact, and the book is
really that fact worked out to its consequences. We’ll state it plainly in a
moment, defend it, and then let it decide where you sleep, whether you
buy a transit pass, how you sequence the two biggest sights, and
where you eat. The promise is not that you’ll agree with every verdict.
It’s that you’ll never be guessing, and that when you close the last tab,
the trip is already decided.

=== p5 ===
A word on what you’re holding, so the shape makes sense. Each
decision is posed as a real question a first-timer asks, answered with
the reasoning laid bare: the cross-article synthesis, the losable verdict,
the case for overruling us. The chapters between them give the ground
the decisions stand on: the one fact, the rhythm of the week, the
texture of the quarters you’ll walk. And a short companion sheet
carries the figures that change, so this book can hold only what lasts.
Read it once, cover to cover, in an evening. Then carry the wallet card
and the Field Sheet, and let the forty tabs stay closed.

How to read this book
This is a book of judgment, so it separates two kinds of statement
carefully. When we assert a hard fact (a fare, a rule, a distance), we
mark it, so you can see it is sourced and how solidly. When we reason
across those facts to reach a call, we don’t mark it, because that syn‐
thesis is ours and you are free to argue with it. Most paragraphs carry
no mark at all. A mark means “this specific claim is checkable”; its
absence means “this is our read.”
The marks are four. ● ESTABLISHED is structural: true for years,
unlikely to move. ◇ OFFICIAL is a regulated fare, a published rule, a
government advisory. ▲ SURVEYED is our own dated observation,
with the month attached. And ◆ VOLATILE is a live figure (a price, an
opening hour, a booking window), which is why you will rarely see it in
these pages: volatile numbers belong in the second document.
Because a book that prints today’s prices is wrong by spring, this
product is two documents by design. The Book, what you are reading,
holds the analysis that ages slowly: the decisions, the reasoning, the
geography, the rules that outlive any list. The Field Sheet is a dated,
single-page companion carrying only the volatile figures (the current
fares, the booking windows, the exchange reference) refreshed on its
own schedule. When the two ever disagree, the Field Sheet is the
newer truth and wins. Read the Book once before you go; carry the
Field Sheet in the trip week.

One more habit will serve you. Where a decision hands you a verdict, it
also hands you the case for overruling it: a short “choose this instead
if…” beside each call. That is not hedging; it is the point. A manual that
only worked for the average traveler would fail the specific one, and
you are specific: you may burn out on your feet, or travel with children,
or care more about one long dinner than three museums. Read the
verdict as the default and the exceptions as the fitting: the book is built
to be argued with, and it tells you exactly where your own
circumstances should win.

The ground: a city you
can walk across
Here is the fact the rest of this book is built on. Rome’s historic cen‐
ter, the connected core that holds almost every sight a first-timer
comes for, is far smaller than it looks on a map. The Pantheon, the
Trevi Fountain, Piazza Navona, the Spanish Steps, Campo de’ Fiori,
the Forum and the Colosseum all sit inside a pedestrian district you
can cross on foot in well under an hour. ● From its center near Piazza
Venezia, the farthest of those sights (the Colosseum, the Spanish
Steps, the edge of Trastevere) are about twenty minutes’ walk. The
dense heart of it, the Pantheon and the piazzas, is closer to ten.
Two structural facts make walking not merely possible but the only
sensible way around. The metro deliberately skirts the historic center,
you cannot tunnel freely under two millennia of ruins, so the core has
only patchy line coverage, improved somewhat by Line C’s new
Colosseo and Porta Metronia stations (December 2025), and cars are
restricted or unwelcome in the tangle of pedestrian lanes. ● On foot is
genuinely the fastest way through the center, and it is also the richest:
walk from the Pantheon to the Trevi and you pass fountains, churches
with Caravaggios hung in the gloom, artisan workshops and tiny piaz‐
zas you’d never have planned to see. In Rome the walking is not the
overhead between sights. It is the sightseeing.
Internalize that one measurement and the daunting city becomes a
small one, and half the trip’s decisions answer themselves. You sleep
in the core because the core is walkable (Decision 01). You skip the
transit pass because you won’t ride enough to justify it (Decision 02).
You take the fixed-fare taxi from the airport because it delivers you into
that walkable heart (Decision 03). You eat two streets off the monu‐
ments because the core is small enough that two streets is a different
city (Decision 08). Every one of those calls is the same kilometer-and-
a-half fact, pointed at a different question. The one real exception (the
Vatican, across the river, a genuine ride or a long walk away) is exactly
why it gets its own half-day rather than sharing the ancient core’s
(Decision 04).

It’s worth meeting the obvious objection head-on, because it’s a fair
one: isn’t Rome an enormous city? It is: a sprawling modern
metropolis of nearly three million people, with suburbs and ring roads
and a second airport. But the Rome a first-timer comes to see is not
that city; it’s the compact historic core the tourist map actually covers,
and that core is the kilometer and a half we’ve measured. The
confusion between the two, planning for the metropolis when you’ll
only ever touch the core, is precisely what produces the Termini base,
the transit pass and the metro-map anxiety this book spends its pages
undoing. Rome the metropolis is large. Rome the trip is small, and you
walk it. Hold those two apart and the rest of the book follows.

Why the obvious tool
doesn’t help
Afirst instinct in any big European city is to master its metro, and
Rome punishes that instinct gently. The network is real and useful for a
few specific journeys (out to the Vatican, in from a far-flung station,
across to a day-trip railhead), but its two main lines, A and B, cross at
Termini and then run to the city’s edges, and the historic heart sits in
the gap between them. ● There is still no line under the
Pantheon/Piazza Navona itself, though Line C now reaches the
Colosseum/Forum edge of the core (December 2025) — check cur‐
rent coverage for your route. The single most photographed square
kilometer of the city is, to the metro, a hole.
This is why we don’t reprint a metro map, though it’s free at every sta‐
tion: for the first-timer’s core trip it would mislead more than it helps,
implying the network reaches sights it structurally cannot. The honest
picture is the negative space: the walkable center the lines curve
around. For the handful of rides you will take, tap a contactless card
and let the daily cap handle the fare (Decision 02); for everything
inside the core, the fastest route is your feet.

The rides that are genuinely worth taking are few and specific, and
worth naming so you know the network isn’t useless: only misapplied.
Line A carries you out to the Vatican (the Ottaviano stop) when you
don’t fancy the long walk across the river, and back in from the airport
train at Termini; Line B drops you at the Colosseo stop beside the
ancient core, and runs to the Ostiense and Piramide interchanges for
the day-trip lines out to Ostia. Buses and trams fill the gaps the metro
leaves (the tram to Trastevere, buses across the center), but for a first-
timer sleeping in the core, the honest tally is a Vatican hop, an airport
connection and a day-trip line. Everything else is the walk you came to
take. Carry the network as a tool for the three journeys that leave the
core, not as the way you’ll get around inside it.

Where should you
actually sleep?
The most consequential booking you make, and the instinct that
ruins it is treating a metro address as an upgrade.
Rome’s historic core is roughly a kilometer and a half across, and its
outer edges (the Colosseum, Trastevere, the Spanish Steps) sit about
twenty minutes’ walk from the center. ● The classic sights a first-timer
comes for are not scattered across a metropolis; they are one con‐
nected pedestrian district you cross on foot in well under an hour. That
single fact should decide where you sleep — and it quietly overturns
the booking logic most Americans arrive with.
The instinct is reasonable and wrong. You are told, correctly, that the
streets around Termini station hold Rome’s densest concentration of
hotels, at the city’s best prices, with both metro lines and the airport
train on the doorstep. ● So you book there to save money and “stay
near the transport.” The flaw is that the transport you are buying
access to may not reach the sights you came for. Rome’s metro cov‐
erage of the historic core is patchy — the Pantheon/Navona area has
no nearby station, though the Colosseum/Forum edge is served by
Colosseo (lines B/C) — so check your actual route rather than assum‐
ing transit is out. ● A Termini base puts you next to the one tool that
doesn’t serve the place you’re going.
Do the walk instead of the math. From a hotel by Termini, the Pantheon
is a thirty-five-minute walk and Piazza Navona closer to forty; from
Centro Storico or Monti, the center of the core is about thirteen. The
€30 you saved on the room buys you an hour of daily commuting — by
metro to a station that still leaves you a walk short, or on foot across
the least interesting part of the center. You did not save money. You
spent it in the currency the trip is actually short of, which is unhurried
time inside the walls.

Do you need transit
tickets at all?
The Roma Pass sells itself on a commute you already decided
not to make.
Rome’s two tourist passes (the Roma Pass and the pricier, Vatican-
inclusive Omnia Card) promise to save you money and time, and the
marketing makes them sound like obvious buys. ● The honest answer
is that they pay off for a specific traveler with a specific itinerary and
waste money for everyone else. What almost no one tells you is that
Decision 01 has already answered this one.
The passes bundle two things: unlimited public transport for the peri‐
od, and free or discounted entry to a handful of attractions. ● The
standard advice, sound as far as it goes, is to “run the math”: total the
entry prices you’d pay anyway, add your transport, and compare to the
pass. But that math is always computed in a vacuum, as if your trans‐
port spend were an unknown. It isn’t. If you took Decision 01 and slept
in the core, you will walk to nearly everything, and your real transport
bill for three days is close to zero.
Collapse the transport line and the pass’s case collapses with it. Strip
out the rides you won’t take, and a pass has to justify itself on attrac‐
tion savings alone — a much narrower margin, and one the first
Sunday of the month can erase entirely, when state museums includ‐
ing the Colosseum admit everyone free. ◇ For the walk-everywhere
first-timer, the pass is selling a convenience — grab-any-bus, skip-the-
ticket-line — priced for a traveler who is criss-crossing the city by
metro. You are not that traveler by design.

Train, or the fixed-fare taxi from
the airport?
The cheap option is only cheap for a hotel you were told not
to book.
Nearly every transatlantic flight lands at Fiumicino, and the first
decision of the trip (made jet-lagged, hauling luggage) is how to reach
the city. The received wisdom is that the Leonardo Express train is the
smart, cheap choice and the taxi is the lazy splurge. ● At face value it’s
true: the train is about €14, the fixed airport taxi €55. ◇ But that com‐
parison quietly assumes something Decision 01 told you not to do.
The Leonardo Express runs non-stop to Termini in about thirty-two
minutes, roughly every fifteen minutes through the day. ◇ It is fast,
frequent and traffic-proof — and it deposits you at Termini, which, as
Decision 01 established, is exactly where you are not sleeping. From
there you still need a metro hop or a taxi or a fifteen-to-twenty-five-
minute walk with your bags to a central hotel. The train’s €14 is only the
first leg of your actual journey door-to-door.
Re-price it honestly and the gap narrows to almost nothing. For two
adults, Leonardo Express tickets run about €28; add the hop from
Termini (about €3 for two metro rides, or about €9–13 by taxi) and the
pair of you pay about €31–41 door to door. The fixed taxi is €55 flat for
the party, up to four people, bags included, door to door, no matter the
traffic or hour. ◇ For a couple with suitcases after an overnight flight,
the gap narrows once the onward hop is added to the train total, and
the taxi’s single flat price, rather than a station decoded and a second
connection while exhausted, is not obviously the wrong spend. The
train wins outright only if you’re near Termini or a metro stop, the very
address the book steers you away from.

“Skip the line” is the most-marketed and least-understood phrase in

different lines, and they are not the same thing — a distinction no

tour you may not want.

Day zero: the arrival
The trip begins the moment the wheels touch at Fiumicino, jet-
lagged and hauling luggage — not the ideal state for decoding fixed
fares and train platforms, which is exactly why the airport transfer is the
first place a little settled judgment pays off. Take the fixed-fare official
taxi to a central hotel if you’re two-to-four people with suitcases; take
the Leonardo Express if you’re light or sleeping near Termini (Decision
03). Either way, walk past anyone who approaches you offering a ride
and go to the marked official rank. ●
Then do almost nothing. The single best first-day plan in Rome is to
drop the bags, walk out the door of your central hotel, and let the walk‐
able core do the work — no tickets, no schedule, no sight you’ve com‐
mitted to. Within a few blocks of a Centro Storico or Monti base you will
pass a two-thousand-year-old temple, a Bernini fountain and a good
espresso bar without trying, which is the whole argument for sleeping
there. The Pantheon now charges admission, though you buy at the
door and need no booking; the piazzas around it still cost nothing at all.
Save the booked, ticketed, sequenced sights for tomorrow, when
you’ve slept.
There is a real wonder in this first aimless evening, and it is part of what
you paid for. The light goes gold on the travertine, the day-trippers thin
out, and a city that read as a logistical problem on your laptop
becomes, abruptly, the reason you came. Let day zero be that. The
decisions in this book exist precisely so the rest of the trip needs no
anxious improvising — which frees the first night to be spent,
not planned.

=== p54 ===
Two practical notes keep day zero from souring. First, aim your arrival
transfer at your hotel’s neighborhood, not at a landmark — give the
driver the street address, and if you’re on the Leonardo Express, know
your onward hop from Termini before you land rather than working it
out on the platform with bags. Second, if you arrive too early to check
in, most central hotels will hold your luggage (drop the bags, don’t drag
them), and the walkable core means you’re never more than a few
minutes from a first espresso and a place to sit while the room is
readied. The single thing that reliably ruins a first day is trying to
sightsee seriously on no sleep; the single thing that saves it is
permission to do almost nothing well. Take the permission.

The rhythm of the week
Rome’s week has a texture worth knowing before it surprises you.
Many kitchens and smaller sights keep their own rhythms — the
Jewish Ghetto’s kosher restaurants and the Boccione bakery close for
Shabbat and Jewish holidays, with shortened Friday hours ● — and
the city’s free state-museum Sunday, first of each month, is the single
busiest sightseeing day there is. Treat “free” as “crowded” and plan
around it (Decision 06).
Three closures cost a first-timer a whole day, and no ticket page warns
you. The Vatican Museums close on Sundays, except the last Sunday
of the month, when entry is free from 9 a.m. to 2 p.m. (last entry 12:30
p.m.) and the crowd is the month’s largest; they also close on about a
dozen church holidays, among them 29 June, 14–15 August, 1
November and 8, 25 and 26 December. ◇ So the Vatican day is never
a Sunday. Ostia Antica, the fourth-day ruin (Decision 09), and the
Borghese Gallery both close on Mondays, ◇ which makes Monday
the natural day for the ancient core. And winter shortens the afternoon:
from late October to the end of February the Colosseum closes at 4:30
p.m., with last entry at 3:30, ◇ so the ancient half-day is a morning.
The dates for your trip are on the Field Sheet.
Strikes are the other rhythm, and they are announced, not sprung:
Italian transport strikes are listed in advance on the transport ministry’s
strike calendar. ◇ On a strike day Rome’s buses, trams and metro run
only in guaranteed bands, until 8:30 a.m. and from 5 to 8 p.m.; ◇
trains run to a list of guaranteed services that Trenitalia posts for each
strike, so check it for the airport train. What a strike means for you is
simple: it costs rides, not sights. Walk the core, which you were doing
anyway; take the fixed-fare taxi to or from the airport; and move a
Vatican or Ostia ride into the guaranteed hours, or to another day.
Season is the larger rhythm, and for a first trip it’s a mild decision rather
than a fraught one. Late April into early June, and September into
October, are the ideal windows (warm, lively, and beautiful) at the cost
of peak crowds and higher prices. ● July and August are hot and busy;
the ancient sites, unshaded, are genuinely punishing on stone at mid‐
day, which turns the early or late Colosseum slot from a nicety into self-
defense. Winter is quiet, cool and cheap, with short queues and a city
returned to Romans. None of this reshapes the trip the way the walk-
time fact does; it adjusts the edges.

=== p57 ===
The daily rhythm matters more than the season, because it decides
when the same sight is a pleasure or a scrum. The ancient core
rewards the early start twice over, cool stone and thin crowds before
the tour buses arrive, and the Vatican Museums reward it a third time,
since a booked early slot walks in past a line that will run for hours by
late morning. Midday, counter-intuitively, is when to eat and rest rather
than sightsee: the sun is hardest on the unshaded ruins and the sights
are at their fullest. Romans themselves shift the day late (dinner rarely
before eight, the pre-dinner aperitivo bridging the gold-lit hours
between), and a first-timer who moves with that rhythm rather than
against it sees more and queues less. The single scheduling instinct
worth keeping is to front-load the day and leave the afternoon soft.

Walking the quarters
The walk-everywhere thesis only holds up because the walking is a
pleasure, and it’s a pleasure because the core isn’t one uniform tourist
zone but a string of quarters with distinct characters, each a short stroll
from the next. The Centro Storico is the dense magical heart — the
square mile where the Pantheon, Piazza Navona and the Trevi sit
within a few minutes of each other, and where you genuinely don’t
need a plan: step out and stumble onto a two-thousand-year-old tem‐
ple, a Bernini fountain and the perfect espresso bar within a single
block. ● This is the Rome most first-timers picture, and the argument
for sleeping inside it.
Cross a few streets and the register changes completely. Monti, tucked
between the Colosseum and Termini, is a village inside the city —
cobbled lanes, vintage boutiques, wine bars and artisan workshops, a
five-minute walk from the Forum and once ancient Rome’s roughest
district, the Suburra where Caesar reportedly grew up. ● Campo de’
Fiori shows Rome’s split personality in a single square: a centuries-old
open-air market of fruit and flowers by morning, one of the city’s liveli‐
est social hubs by night, a brooding statue of a burned philosopher
over it all. ● And the Jewish Ghetto, tucked between the Tiber and
Campo de’ Fiori, layers two thousand years into a few compact
streets — one of the oldest Jewish communities in the world, a grand
synagogue, ancient ruins, and a distinctive Roman-Jewish cuisine you
can taste nowhere else. ●

=== p63 ===
Two quarters reward a deliberate trip rather than a stumble.
Trastevere, across the Tiber, is the Rome of postcards — a maze of
cobbled lanes and ivy-draped façades that’s wonderful by day and
genuinely magical after dark, when the lantern-lit lanes fill with a slow
tide drifting from aperitivo to dinner to gelato; crossing the river as the
sun goes down is the one evening every first-timer should keep. ● And
Testaccio, south of the center and off the tourist trail, is where Romans
actually eat — a working-class quarter built around the old
slaughterhouse, which made it the heartland of the four classic pastas
and the bold “fifth-quarter” offal cooking, with a brilliant food market
and barely a tourist in sight. ●

The set pieces
The Pantheon is the one that catches people twice — once with
wonder, once with the ticket. Completed under Hadrian around AD 126
and preserved because it was never abandoned but continuously
used, it survives not as a ruin but as a building: you walk in and stand
under the same coffered dome the ancient Romans did, lit by the same
open oculus. ● What’s changed recently is that it now charges admis‐
sion, which blindsides first-timers who remember it as the free marvel
it long was. ◇ Booking is optional and tickets are sold on site, so buy
at the door; the visit itself rarely takes more than half an hour and
lingers far longer than that.
There is a threshold moment here worth slowing down for, because
anticipation is part of what you’re buying. You step out of an ordinary
Roman street, through the great bronze doors, and the space opens
overhead into a perfect hemisphere with a hole of pure sky at its
apex — rain falling straight through it onto the marble on a wet day, a
shaft of hard sun tracking the floor on a bright one. Two thousand
years of engineering resolve into a single quiet awe. Then you’re back
on the street with a gelato, and the city has done to you what it does.

=== p66 ===
The Vatican’s two vertical set pieces reward knowing the logistics in
advance. St. Peter’s dome is a genuine climb — 551 steps in total, with
a lift that covers only the first stretch to the roof terrace and leaves the
final narrow, leaning spiral to your legs ● — and it sits behind the
basilica’s security and its dress code: “Shoulders and knees must
always be covered.” ◇ Pack a scarf and long pants or a skirt for
Vatican day. It pairs with, rather than interrupts, a St. Peter’s visit timed
early (Decision 05). And Castel Sant’Angelo, a short walk along the
Tiber from St. Peter’s, is the natural afternoon companion to the
Vatican morning: Hadrian’s tomb turned fortress, papal refuge, prison
and now a museum crowned by one of the best rooftop views in Rome,
linked to the Vatican by the elevated corridor popes once fled along. ●
Many visitors do the Vatican in the morning and the castle in the
afternoon — which is exactly the half-day pairing the ancient core
forbids with the Vatican, and invites here.

The plan, as operations
Afirst Roman trip has two unavoidable heavy half-days (the ancient

is that they never share a day (Decision 04). Everything else arranges
itself around that constraint. If you slept in the core, the ancient cluster

the sequence writes itself.
A clean three-day shape: the first day is the walkable core on foot
(Pantheon, piazzas, Trevi, Campo) with nothing booked, easing off the
flight (Day Zero). The second is the ancient half-day, Forum first at
opening, Colosseum on a midday slot, and a Roman dinner two
streets off the monuments (Decisions 04, 08). The third is the Vatican,
never on a Sunday — Museums on a booked early slot, St. Peter’s by

(Decision 09).

museum in Rome is a morning in itself; and the walk home through the
lit core at night is the part you’ll remember. Leave the gaps. A first
Roman trip fails far more often from cramming than from missing a
sight — and the sight you skip is always there for the next visit the city

The principle from Decision 09 generalizes: a day trip is worth it
when the reward per hour-on-the-train is high, and Italy makes that
easy because the trains are fast, frequent, and drop you in the town
center — you almost never need a car. Once you’ve done the core, the
Vatican and the piazzas, here is the honest ranking of what a spare day
buys, cheapest-in-time first.
Ostia Antica
~30 MIN EACH WAY, METRO  TICKET
The best short trip, covered in full at Decision 09: an entire ancient
port (streets, mosaics, everyday Roman life) for half a day and a
metro fare. If you have one spare half-day, this is it.
Castelli Romani
~40 – 60 MIN, REGIONAL  TRAIN
The Romans’ own countryside escape — volcanic hills, two crater
lakes, hill towns and the crisp Frascati wine poured all over the city.
A relaxed day of wine and lake views for travelers who’ve had their
fill of monuments, and refreshingly untouristed.
Orvieto
~1 HR, DIRECT  TRAIN
A golden-stoned Umbrian hill town on a plug of volcanic rock,
crowned by one of Italy’s most spectacular Gothic cathedrals, with
an underground world of caves and a namesake white wine.
Compact, walkable and easy — the perfect low-effort antidote to a
few intense days in the city.
Tivoli
~1 HR, TRAIN OR  BUS
Two spectacular and utterly different villas in a day: Hadrian’s vast
ruined country estate and the Renaissance fountain-garden of
Villa d’Este. One of the best escapes near Rome — it just needs a
little planning, since the two sites sit apart and each deserves
real time.
Pompeii
~2 hr each way, ~12 hr  door-to-door
The greater site at the greater cost, dissected at Decision 09.
Extraordinary, and a whole demanding day; right for the trip that
can spare one, wrong as a squeeze on a short visit.
Florence
~1.5 HR EACH WAY,  HIGH-SPEED
Possible, and rarely ideal. High-speed rail makes a day trip
genuinely doable and tempting for travelers who won’t otherwise
reach Tuscany — but Florence rewards far more than a rushed
day, and doing it from Rome means a great city seen at a sprint.
Go if it’s your only chance; otherwise save it for its own trip.

=== p72 ===
The central portal of Orvieto's cathedral, an hour out by direct train: the near

The pattern rewards reading. The nearer, lower-key places (Ostia, the
Castelli, Orvieto, Tivoli) give a genuinely restful day and a real taste of
Italy beyond the capital for a modest slice of time. The famous, farther
names (Pompeii, Florence) are extraordinary and expensive in exactly
the currency a short trip is short of. On a first visit of three or four days,
the honest advice is to resist the famous day-trip and take a near one,
or none at all: Rome itself is not close to exhausted in four days, and
the countryside will still be there for the trip the city has already
convinced you to take.

Eating: the rule that outlives
the list
Roman food is not Italy’s fanciest, and that is the point. This is
cucina povera — “poor kitchen” cooking built on a handful of humble
ingredients used brilliantly: pasta, cured pork cheek, sheep’s cheese,
black pepper, artichokes, offal. ● The dishes are centuries old and
Romans are fiercely protective of them, which is why the ninety-
second rule of Decision 08 works so reliably: a kitchen two streets off
the monument survives on locals who would notice a wrong car‐
bonara, while the photo-menu on the piazza survives on tourists who
won’t return. Knowing what to order is the difference between tasting
the actual city and eating a tourist-trap approximation of it.
Learn four pastas and you understand the cuisine. The Roman canon
shares a common DNA (pecorino romano, guanciale and black pep‐
per) across cacio e pepe, carbonara, amatriciana and gricia. ● A real
carbonara has no cream and no bacon; it’s egg, pecorino, guanciale
and pepper, and it is the dish Americans most often meet in a wrong
version at home. Ordering these by name, in a room full of Romans, is
both the better meal and the quiet signal that you’ve done
your reading.

=== p75 ===
Coffee and the pre-dinner hour are the two rituals that most quickly
stop you reading as a tourist. Roman coffee is a small strong espresso
taken standing at a marble bar in thirty seconds for about a euro, often
paid for first at the register ● — no venti oat-milk latte on a park bench,
and a cappuccino strictly before mid-morning. And aperitivo, roughly
six to eight in the evening as the stone turns gold, is the civilized bridge
into Rome’s late dinner: a spritz or a glass of wine with a few bites, as
much social pause as drink. ● Both are cheap, both are pleasures,
and both are the everyday texture the monuments can’t give you.

81
What this book won’t
tell you
This book sells you reality: the real model behind the naive one, the
connections the free web leaves scattered. A few things on offer
everywhere else are not reality, they are false certainty that rots in a
season, and printing them would quietly discredit everything else here.
So we refuse them on purpose, and tell you why.
We won’t name specific restaurants.
A named restaurant is a guess with a byline, wrong by spring and
free anywhere. What outlasts it is the rule for spotting the right
one — walk ninety seconds off the sight, look for a short Italian
menu and locals after 8:30 — which we do give you (Decision 08).
The rule works in a neighborhood we’ve never named.
We won’t list hotels.
A hotel pick is stale in a season and we haven’t slept in it. The
decision that lasts is which area, and why — and that one we
make, in full, on measured walk-times (Decision 01). Pick your own
room inside the base we argue for.
We won’t print current prices.
A price in a book is a lie with a publication date. Every live figure —
fares, booking windows, the exchange reference — lives on the
dated Field Sheet, so it can be right when you read it rather than
right when we wrote it. When the two disagree, the Sheet wins.
We won’t republish anyone’s ratings.
Per-place scores are legally and editorially radioactive, and they
encode a crowd’s taste, not a method you can carry. We give you
the method instead — the filters and the geography — so you can
judge a room or a plate the city has never rated.

82
After the last tab
There is a moment, usually the second evening, when Rome stops
being a problem to solve. You’ve done the ancient morning and eaten
well two streets off the Forum, and you’re walking back through the lit
core with nowhere you have to be. A fountain you didn’t plan to see is
running in an empty square. The city that lived in forty browser tabs is
simply around you, and it is better than the tabs promised.
That evening is what all the deciding was for. The point of resolving the
fifteen calls in advance (where to sleep, what to book, how to
sequence, where to eat) was never the calls themselves. It was to buy
back the attention those decisions would otherwise have eaten on the
trip, and spend it here, on the thing you actually came for. Close the
last tab. Rome’s core sights sit within a kilometer and a half of Piazza
Venezia, and now you know how to walk it.

=== p83 ===
What you carry out of this book is not a list of sights — the sights were
never the hard part, and they’re free to look up. What you carry is a way
of seeing the city that makes it small: the knowledge that the core is
walkable, that the metro is a distraction, that the traps cluster where
the frontage is dear, that the famous day-trip is the wrong default, that
the expensive “skip the line” skips a line you could beat with an alarm
clock. Those aren’t facts to memorize; they’re a lens. Put it on, and
Rome stops being forty tabs of contradictory advice and becomes
what it is — a kilometer and a half of the most rewarding walking in the
world, already yours to cross.
