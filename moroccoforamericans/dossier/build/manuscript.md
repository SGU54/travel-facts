## The contract

Most Morocco guides answer where should I go? This one answers how should I think?

That distinction is the whole product. The ninety free articles behind this book will tell you what to see, what to eat, and how to get from Marrakech to Fez. What no free article does, because each lives on its own page, is connect the handful of decisions that actually determine whether your trip runs smoothly or bleeds money and days. This book makes those connections, and it makes them so you can disagree with us and still come out ahead. Every verdict here shows its arithmetic. If your priorities differ from the ones we assumed, a sharp first-time American, a week to spend, a mid-range budget, you can see exactly which number to change and re-run the decision yourself.

There is a particular kind of traveller this is written for. You have forty browser tabs open. You have read that Morocco is wonderful and also that everyone gets ripped off, and you cannot tell which of those to believe or how to be the first without being the second. You are not looking for a list of riads. You are looking for the shape of the thing: what actually matters, what is noise, and where the expensive mistakes hide.

We are not trying to be your itinerary. We are trying to make you ungullible.

## How to Read This

The two documents. This is a book and a field sheet, and the split is deliberate. The book holds the analysis that stays true for years: how the closed currency works, why the payment-direction test sorts guides from touts, what the desert actually costs in days. The field sheet holds the figures that move, a taxi fare, a meal, the day's exchange rate. When a price changes, only the field sheet goes stale; the reasoning doesn't. That is why you will never find a live price in the book's body, and why the field sheet carries a date.

The marks. Five symbols tell you how we know a thing:

● ESTABLISHED — a structural fact, unlikely to change within the life of this edition.

◇ OFFICIAL — a published rule or regulated figure. Every ◇ claim is listed with its source and the date we last checked it, at the back.

▲ SURVEYED — a dated observation from the ground, ours or from our own articles.

◈ OUR CALL — a judgment, not a finding. Contestable on purpose, and always shown with the reasoning that produced it.

◆ VOLATILE — it moves; it lives on the field sheet, not here.

◈ matters most. A guide that presents its opinions in the same voice as its facts is asking you to trust the wrong things. Where this book says the desert is worth three days rather than two, that is a ◈, and you may weigh it differently. Where it says the dirham cannot be bought abroad, that is a ●, and your weighing it differently will cost you money.

The shape of the book. Part one is what you settle before you fly. Part two is the eight decisions that set the trip's shape, in the order you actually face them. Part three is the places themselves, treated as set pieces with an order of operations rather than lists. Part four is the ground: the first hour, the customs, and what to book when.

Using it on the trip. Read part one and part two before you book anything. Carry the field sheet. Re-check anything marked ◆ the week you travel.

## What’s inside, and where

## The one fact that reorganises the trip

Everything in this book follows from a single organising fact: in Morocco, no price is simply the price.

Not because the country is dishonest. Because a series of small, legal, avoidable mechanisms each move a little of your money before you have decided to spend it. The currency is closed, so you cannot arrive already holding dirham and already knowing the rate. The card terminal offers, politely, to bill you in dollars at a padded number. The helpful young man at the medina gate is paid by the shop he walks you into, not by you. The first figure named in a souk is an opening bid dressed as a price tag. Individually, footnotes. Together, the single most expensive feature of an unprepared first trip.

Once you see the trip through this lens, its shape changes. The money question stops being a chapter and becomes the spine that runs through every other decision. Guides stop being a safety worry and become a payment-direction question. "Is it safe?" resolves into the sharper and more useful "who is trying to extract from me here, and by what mechanism?" Even the itinerary bends to it: the reason the open-jaw ticket matters is that a wasted backtracking day is the most expensive purchase in the whole trip, and nobody sells it to you as a purchase.

This is not a book about spending less. Plenty of people have a cheap, miserable time in Morocco. It is a book about never being the person who answers four questions they did not know they were being asked, and who therefore pays a tax that has nothing to do with what they got.

A caution about the caution. This book is organised around what costs you money, so it spends its pages on the small number of interactions that are transactional and says almost nothing about the overwhelming majority that are not. That imbalance is a property of the book, not of the country. Most of what happens to you in Morocco is somebody being hospitable for reasons unconnected to money: the tea you did not ask for, the directions walked rather than pointed, the shopkeeper who sends you to a competitor because he does not have your size. Read what follows as a small and learnable tax, and not as a description of the people charging it.

## What to settle, and by when

The naive assumption is that Morocco requires paperwork. For Americans it requires remarkably little, which creates its own failure mode: because there is no visa to force a deadline, people arrive having thought about none of it.

Entry is the easy part. For US citizens Morocco is one of the simplest countries in the world to enter: no visa, no application, no fee. You arrive with a valid passport and are admitted for up to ninety days ◇. The same visa-free entry applies to UK, EU, Canadian and Australian citizens and dozens of other nationalities. You book your flights, turn up, and get an entry stamp at passport control.

Two details do trip people. The first is passport validity, and it is worth stating precisely because the sources genuinely diverge. The official minimum varies by nationality and is shorter than most travellers assume; the practical minimum is set by airlines, which routinely refuse boarding on a passport with under six months left ◇. Treat six months beyond your arrival date as the working rule and the divergence stops mattering. The second is an electronic-authorization system, the AEVM, which exists and which Morocco has signalled it may extend. As things stand it applies to certain nationalities and does not currently apply to US, UK or EU visa-free travellers ◇. Americans do not need one today. Morocco has said it may widen the system in future, which is exactly why the responsible habit for any border crossing is a quick check close to your travel date.

Health is a conversation, not a requirement. Beyond routine vaccinations, the recommendations for Morocco are things to weigh with a doctor, not conditions of entry ●. The country carries a low-level health notice and is a low-risk destination for serious tropical disease. Hepatitis B is advised for many travellers; rabies is suggested mainly for long stays or those likely to be around animals. A travel clinic can tailor this to your itinerary, since a short city trip is a different exposure profile from long rural travel. The higher-yield preparation is duller: a small medical kit, and sensible food and water habits.

Arrive connected. This matters more in Morocco than in most places, and the reason is structural. Because the currency is closed, your first act on landing is usually finding an ATM rather than a phone shop ●. If you land already online, you can check a map, message your riad, and confirm your transfer without first hunting for airport Wi-Fi while jet-lagged. An eSIM does that at the cost of a slightly higher price per gigabyte. A local SIM is cheaper per gigabyte and gives you a Moroccan number, but it costs you twenty minutes in the arrivals hall and requires your passport. Either beats the international day-pass your US carrier will happily sell you.

Tell your bank, and bring the right cards. The card that declines at an airport ATM in a closed-currency country is a genuinely bad start. Two cards from two different networks, travel notifications filed, and a rough idea of your own withdrawal fees is fifteen minutes of preparation that removes an entire category of first-day trouble.

Pack for cold. The most common packing error is not cultural, it is thermal. Travellers picture Morocco as uniformly hot, pack accordingly, and then freeze on a desert night or a winter evening in Marrakech ●. Desert nights are cold, even after hot days. Riads are stone and often unheated. Bring a layer you would take to a coastal city in autumn, plus a light scarf, which does double duty for sun, dust, and covering up near religious sites.

## The shape of the year

Morocco has four genuinely distinct seasons, and the country is big enough that the right answer differs by region ●. That is the part most calendars miss: "best time to visit Morocco" has no single answer, but "best time to visit Morocco on the Marrakech–desert–Fez loop" does.

The short answer is spring and fall. For most first-timers the ideal months are April, May, October and November ●. Spring and fall bring warm, comfortable days and cool evenings across the cities, and they are pleasant even in the desert. These shoulder seasons balance good weather, manageable crowds, and reasonable prices, which is the sweet spot for exactly the loop this book is built around.

Summer is a regional question, not a national one. Inland is very hot: Marrakech, Fez and the Sahara can exceed 100°F (38°C), which makes midday sightseeing genuinely tough ●. That does not rule out a summer trip, but it changes its shape: early mornings, late evenings, and real reliance on shaded riad courtyards and pools in the middle of the day. The coast behaves differently. Essaouira stays mild and breezy, which is precisely why Moroccans use it as a summer refuge.

Winter is underrated and misunderstood. The cities are mild and pleasant by day and cold at night. The desert is cold at night in a way that surprises people who have only pictured dunes in sunlight. The Atlas gets snow. A winter trip is a good trip, provided you pack for the temperature that actually occurs rather than the one in the brochure.

Ramadan changes the rhythm, not the sights. Because Morocco follows the lunar calendar, Ramadan moves roughly eleven days earlier each year, so its dates shift and are worth confirming for any specific travel window ●. Travelling during the holy month changes the shape of a day far more than it changes what you can see. Tourist sites generally stay open, sometimes with adjusted hours, so the sightseeing itself is rarely blocked; the logistics around it need more planning. Daytime is quieter. The hour just before sunset empties the streets almost completely as everyone heads home for iftar, which is either an inconvenience or one of the most striking things you will witness, depending on whether you planned around it. Evenings become extraordinary.

As a non-Muslim visitor you are not expected to fast, and tourist-oriented restaurants, hotels and riads continue to serve meals ●. The courtesy is discretion rather than abstinence: not eating, drinking or smoking conspicuously in the street during fasting hours. Ramadan is neither a reason to avoid Morocco nor a month to travel through unaware. It rewards a little preparation and respect, and it gives back an atmosphere no ordinary month offers.

## The web, and why no single price is the price

The naive model a sharp first-timer arrives with is "I'll get some dirham and watch my spending." That model assumes the price you are shown is the price. In Morocco it rarely is, not because the country is dishonest, but because four separate, legal, avoidable mechanisms each move a little of your money before you have decided to spend it. Read alone, each is a footnote. Read together, they are the single most expensive thing about a first trip, and no free article joins them, because each lives in its own post.

WHERE IT LEAKS · THE MOVE

Take them in order, because they compound. The closed currency is the one that surprises Americans most: dirham cannot legally be bought and sold freely outside Morocco, so you cannot land already holding local cash at a rate you chose. You arrive with foreign notes or a card, and the first exchange happens on someone else's terms, in an airport, while you are tired. That is not a scam; it is a market structure. But it means the first hour of your trip is the hour you are least equipped to negotiate and most likely to be transacting.

Dynamic currency conversion is the purest of the four because it is a question with a right answer. When a terminal asks whether you would like to be charged in dollars rather than dirham, the polite-sounding offer is a worse exchange rate with a spread taken by the processor. Declining costs nothing and takes one word. Yet the offer is designed to feel like a convenience, and travellers accept it precisely because they are unsure and the screen is waiting.

The commission chain is the one with the most disguises. It appears as a free guide, a "cooperative," a friend who happens to know a place, a detour on a tour. In every version the person leading you is compensated by the destination rather than by you, which quietly inverts whose interests are being served. And the anchor price is the mechanism most travellers already half-know and still lose to. Learning to bargain, and to agree fares before rather than after, is the single biggest money-saver available to a first-timer ●. The reason it works on people is that Americans are culturally trained to treat a stated number as information rather than as an opening move. It is an opening move.

None of these is a scam in the legal sense, and that is exactly why they work. Each is a default you must actively refuse. Treat every one as a question with a rehearsed answer, and Morocco's "expensive" reputation largely dissolves. The tourist who overpays isn't careless; they're answering four questions they didn't know they were being asked. That is the whole trip in miniature, and preparing for the web is what the rest of this book does.

This is the connection the free articles never make. Everything else here is a consequence of it.

## Marrakech, Fez, or both?

The default plan is "we'll stay in Marrakech." It is the famous one, the cheap flight, the name everyone knows. That is fine for a long weekend. But the moment the desert or a second city enters the trip, treating Marrakech as the sole base quietly forces a backtrack that eats a day. The base decision isn't about which city is nicer; it's about which city you end in, because that is what the open-jaw ticket turns on.

WHAT IT ANCHORS · THE COST

A riad is a traditional Moroccan house or palace built inward around a central courtyard or garden, often with a fountain at its heart ●. The plain door on the lane opens onto a calm interior world, and that inversion is the whole architectural idea: the house turns its back on the street and makes its own sky. Ask anyone who has been to Morocco where you should stay and the answer comes back fast, a riad, but "riad" is a word most Americans arrive not quite understanding, and the difference between a riad and a hotel is not a matter of style.

It is a matter of position. Staying inside the medina puts you within the thing you came to see, on foot, at the hours when it is at its best, early and late, when the day-trippers are elsewhere. A modern hotel on the ring road is more comfortable in the conventional sense and structurally further from the trip. The trade is real: medina riads are often unreachable by car, which is precisely why arrival logistics matter (decision 05 and the Day Zero chapter), and why the airport transfer that folds that problem into a fixed price is worth its small premium.

The two cities are not interchangeable, either. Marrakech is Morocco's showman: compact, theatrical, relentless, and easy to love in two days. Fez is its scholar, older and deeper, home to the world's largest car-free urban area, and the most bewildering to navigate ●. Many travellers end up loving Fez more, and almost all of them needed help on the first morning to get there.

For a week, don't pick a base, pick an axis: Marrakech to Fez, desert in the middle, sleeping in a riad inside each city's medina rather than a modern hotel outside it. The riad functions here as an orientation rather than a luxury, and we rate the first-day licensed guide in Fez as the highest-value purchase of the trip ◈. If your trip is shorter than five days, invert this entirely: one city, done properly, beats two done at a run.

a single base is right → you're staying three or four days and never leaving Marrakech's orbit.

don't split → mobility stresses you more than backtracking; then base in Marrakech and treat Fez as a fast train day, accepting you'll only taste it.

No field-sheet entry — this is structure, not price.

## How many days, and in what order?

Almost every first-timer arrives with a day count and no shape, "we have a week." Our reporting is blunt about what actually governs the trip: how many days you need depends almost entirely on one question, do you want the desert? The desert is not a side trip you slot in; it is a two-to-three day commitment that reorganises everything around it. Decide the desert first, and the day count and the order fall out of it.

SHAPE · WHAT IT BUYS

Morocco's distances are long and the roads slow, so fewer places at a humane pace beats a frantic tour ●. This is our most repeated piece of advice and the most consistently ignored, because a map of Morocco looks compact and a list of its highlights looks achievable. It is not the mileage that defeats people, it is the combination of mileage and mountain road. The travellers who most enjoy Morocco are, on our own reading, the ones who did less and did it well ▲.

The single structural move that most improves a week is the one most people miss. Most desert tours can end in Fez rather than doubling back to Marrakech ●, which means the natural shape of the trip is a line, not a loop: arrive at Marrakech (RAK), depart from Fez (FEZ), with the desert in the middle. Booked as one open-jaw ticket, this saves an entire day of backtracking. That day is the difference between a rushed week and an unhurried one, and it costs nothing but knowing to ask for it at the point of booking, when it is trivially easy, rather than discovering it in month two of planning, when your flights are already paid for.

The corollary is a warning. Pick two cities and one great excursion for a week, and let the trip breathe ●. Three cities plus the desert in seven days is a schedule, not a holiday, and Moroccan road time will not cooperate with it.

For a first trip, seven days on the Marrakech–desert–Fez line is the sweet spot our reporting keeps returning to, and the open-jaw ticket is what makes it work. Book the flights that way before anything else; every other decision in this book is easier once the shape is a line.

the short version is right → you're on a stopover or want only Marrakech's energy without the long southern drives.

the loop is too much → you have four days, not seven; then it's Marrakech plus one deliberate excursion, not a compressed loop.

No field-sheet entry — the shapes are structural.

## Merzouga or Zagora?

It looks like a distance problem, and a reasonable one: Zagora is closer, so it's the sensible desert if time is tight. This is the trap. "Closer" buys you a shorter drive to a smaller, rockier desert, not the sweeping dune sea that made you want the Sahara in the first place. The decision isn't distance; it's whether you want the desert you pictured, and whether your itinerary can absorb the night it costs.

DRIVE (each way) · WHAT YOU GET

The drive itself is worth understanding before you price the options, because it is not dead time. The route south from Marrakech crosses the High Atlas by mountain pass and drops through the Draa valley past kasbahs and palm oases; a good share of what people remember from the desert leg happens through a car window. That cuts both ways. It means a two-day dash is not simply "less desert," it is a day and a half of driving with the interesting parts taken at speed. And it means the extra night that Merzouga demands buys you the drive done properly as well as the dunes done properly.

What separates the two destinations is what the sand does. Scale in the abstract tells you nothing. Erg Chebbi is a dune sea: tall, shifting, golden, the thing in the photographs. Zagora's desert is thinner and rockier, closer to scrubland with dunes than to the Sahara of the imagination. Travellers who choose Zagora for the arithmetic and expected Lawrence of Arabia are the reliably disappointed ones, and the disappointment is not a matter of taste. They bought a different product.

There is also a third option the arithmetic usually hides, which is not going. A rushed desert is worse than a well-spent city, and Morocco has two of the great old cities in the world within a day of each other.

If you have three days, go to Merzouga; the extra night is the entire point. If you genuinely have only two, our call ◈ is not Zagora but skipping the desert on this trip, give the time to a second city, and come back for Erg Chebbi properly. The one itinerary we would refuse outright ◈ is the two-day Merzouga dash: eighteen to twenty hours of driving for a few hours on the dunes.

Zagora earns its place → you have exactly two days, you've seen dunes before, and the drive itself (Draa Valley, kasbahs) is the draw.

Merzouga is wrong → you have four+ days and want the Atlas and the desert both; then a private driver open-jaw (decision 05) beats the round-trip tour.

## Train, coach, or private driver?

Arriving from Europe, you import an assumption without noticing: there'll be a train. There is, and ONCF is comfortable and punctual, but the network reaches only part of the country, and the part it doesn't reach includes the desert. So the real decision isn't "which is best"; it's "which tool for which leg," and getting it wrong means a nine-hour coach on a route a driver would have made scenic, or a taxi haggle on a route the train already owned.

WHERE IT WINS · THE CATCH

The mistake isn't picking the wrong single tool; it's assuming one tool covers the whole country. The train is a fixed skeleton, fast and calm on the Casablanca–Fez–Marrakech corridor and absent everywhere south of it. First class is a small premium that buys a guaranteed seat with room for luggage, which on a busy corridor is the difference between a pleasant three hours and standing.

The coach fills the gaps the rail line leaves. CTM and Supratours are the two reliable intercity operators, and they are how most travellers reach Chefchaouen or Essaouira. Two practical details matter more than they should: hold luggage carries a small separate fee paid at the station rather than folded into the ticket, and CTM's terminal is sometimes not the same building as the main bus or train station, which is a bad thing to discover twenty minutes before departure.

The private driver is the one people either over-buy or dismiss entirely. It is genuinely the most expensive option per day, and genuinely the right one for exactly one part of a standard first trip: the desert loop, where the drive is content rather than transit, where luggage moves door to door, and where hiring the car and driver replaces both a rigid group tour and the backtracking day. Used for a single city-to-city leg the train already serves well, it is money spent on nothing.

Use the train as the dependable spine wherever it runs. Take the coach where the train doesn't go and you're not in a hurry. Hire the driver for the desert and the open-jaw specifically. Match the tool to the leg and the country opens up; force one tool onto every leg and one of them will cost you a day.

the driver earns it → the desert loop, four+ days, luggage, and you value the kasbah stops on the Draa road.

skip the driver → your trip is city-to-city on the rail line; then the train is faster, calmer, and a fraction of the cost.

## Who's paying the person showing you around?

This is the money-web at its sharpest, and it inverts the naive instinct. The first-timer's instinct is to refuse all guides, having read about the "faux guide" who latches on and steers you to his cousin's shop. Correct to refuse him. But the same instinct then refuses the licensed guide, who is one of the highest-value purchases of the entire trip. The distinction isn't whether you use a guide; it's the direction of payment.

WHO PAYS THEM · WHOSE INTEREST THEY SERVE

An official guide is licensed by the state, carries a badge, and is paid directly by you at a rate agreed in advance ●. That structure is the whole argument. His incentive is to show you the city well, because that is the transaction, and a good one will read your interests within an hour and shape the morning around them.

The tell is simple and worth rehearsing, because it works even when everything else is ambiguous. Ask to see the badge: licensed guides carry an official identification card, and a genuine one will show it without hesitation ●. A guide who insists on visiting workshops is quietly reintroducing the commission model you hired an official guide to avoid ●. One workshop, offered and declinable, is hospitality. Three is a sales route.

Booking removes the problem upstream. The reliable routes are through your riad or hotel, which can arrange a vetted licensed guide, or through a reputable tour operator ●. What you are buying with that small amount of friction is the elimination of the medina-gate encounter entirely: you never have to evaluate a stranger's credentials while jet-lagged and lost, because someone with a reputation to protect has already done it.

Where this pays for itself most obviously is Fez. Fès el-Bali is the world's largest car-free urban area, a living medieval city and the most bewildering in Morocco to navigate ●. Hiring a licensed local guide for your first morning there is money exceptionally well spent: they orient you, get you past the faux-guide hustle, and open doors that a self-guided wander does not find. After that morning you can walk it yourself, which is the point.

The tell is a single question: who pays this person? If it's you, their incentive aligns with yours. If it's a shop, it doesn't, and everything they show you bends toward a purchase. Hire a licensed guide for the first day in a dense old city, book through your riad, ask for the badge, and refuse anyone who approaches you first.

hire the guide → your first day is in Fez's medina, or any dense old town where you'd otherwise lose hours.

skip it → you're confident with a map in Marrakech's more legible core and prefer to wander; then just decline the approachers cleanly.

## How do you eat well without eating like a tourist?

First-timers fixate on the wrong worry. They obsess over whether the food is safe, and mostly it is, while walking straight into the trap that actually costs them: eating badly and expensively in the obvious places. Knowing a handful of dishes before you arrive turns a bewildering menu into a plan, and one rule outlives every list of restaurant names a blog could give you.

THE TRAP · THE RULE

Busy food stalls with high turnover are a safer bet than a quiet one where food sits ●, and this single heuristic does two jobs at once. It handles the hygiene question, because turnover means nothing waits around, and the value question, because locals vote with their money. The queue functions as a review that cannot be bought, which is more than most online ratings manage.

The dishes are worth learning in advance. This has nothing to do with authenticity points: a menu you can read is a menu you can order from without defaulting to the safest, blandest, most tourist-priced option on it. Tagine is the workhorse, slow-cooked in the conical clay pot that gives it its name and varying by region and household. Couscous is traditionally the Friday dish. There is a soup you will meet everywhere, and a street-food staple in every city that locals eat standing up. Four things is enough to navigate anywhere in the country.

Mint tea deserves a paragraph of its own, because Americans consistently misread it. It is poured from a height for aeration and theatre, it is offered constantly, and it is a genuine social ritual across the Maghreb rather than a performance staged for you. Accepting a glass is not a purchase and does not create an obligation, with one exception worth knowing: in a shop, tea offered as the opening move of a long negotiation is hospitality doing double duty, and you are free to enjoy it and still walk away.

Learn four dishes and you can read any menu in the country. Then apply the rule we would keep above all others ◈: eat where locals queue, and confirm prices at tourist-heavy stalls before ordering, not after. That defends against both ways Morocco separates eaters from money, and costs nothing but a moment's attention.

chase the regional specialities → you have time on the coast (Essaouira's seafood) and a curious palate.

keep it simple → you're moving fast; then tagine and street food alone will not let you down.

## How worried should you actually be?

Every first-timer asks this more than any other question, and the honest answer reorganises the trip: yes, Morocco is safe for the vast majority of travellers, but "safe" doesn't mean "hassle-free," and knowing the difference is what keeps a first trip relaxed instead of tense ●. The energy you'd spend fearing violent crime is better spent recognising the extraction you'll actually meet.

THE FEAR · THE REALITY

Most "safety" issues in Morocco are really scams aimed at your wallet, and they follow predictable scripts ●. That predictability is the good news, because a script can be learned. The unsolicited helper who appears exactly when you look lost. The sight that is "closed today," with an alternative conveniently at hand. The tannery terrace with an aggressive "fee." The henna applied to your hand before you agreed to it. None is a threat; each works by creating a small obligation you did not consent to ●. Keep your hands to yourself, decline anything placed on you, and agree any price before rather than after, and the entire category collapses.

The crowd itself is the other half. Dense medinas and packed squares are pickpocket environments in the same way that any dense tourist environment anywhere is, and they call for the same ordinary discipline rather than special fear. Being lost in Fez feels alarming the first time and is not: it is normal and rarely unsafe, just disorienting ●.

The advisory level is worth reading rather than reacting to. Level 2 means "exercise increased caution," it cites terrorism as the primary concern, and it is the same level carried by a number of European countries Americans visit without a second thought ◇. It is a routine designation, not a warning to stay away, and it changes, which is why it lives on the field sheet rather than in this paragraph as a permanent fact.

Solo women travellers get a sharper version of the same picture: the risk profile is persistent, wearying attention rather than violence, and our reporting treats it as its own subject with its own playbook rather than a footnote here.

Recalibrate. The thing to manage is persistence, not danger. Keep normal medina awareness for pickpockets, treat the touts as noise rather than threat, and check the current advisory before you fly. The traveller who arrives braced for danger is braced for the wrong thing; brace for the web instead.

extra caution is warranted → you're a solo woman traveller and want our specific playbook on that.

don't over-worry → you have city instincts already; then Morocco asks less of you than the reputation suggests.

## The set pieces, in the right order

Marrakech packs an enormous amount into a compact old city, and a first-timer can fill two or three days without repeating a thing ●. That compactness is the reason it works as a first stop and the reason it overwhelms: everything is close, so everything happens at once. What follows is an order of operations rather than a list of attractions, because in Marrakech the sequence matters more than the selection.

THE PIECE · THE MOVE

Jemaa el-Fnaa is the city's beating heart and the thing to do first ●. By day it is a wide, sun-baked square with juice stalls and snake charmers. At sunset it transforms into a carnival of food stalls, musicians and storytellers, and the transformation is the event. The right way to experience it is to arrive around sunset, walk in slowly, and let the energy build around you ●.

The specific move worth stealing: watch it fill from a rooftop terrace café with a mint tea, then descend into the crowd ●. Seeing it from above and then from within is the ideal sequence for a first night, and the price of the tea buys the panorama. Down in the square, the same rule from decision 07 applies without modification, eat at a busy stall, and decision 08 governs the rest.

The ticketed monuments deserve their own morning rather than being scattered through the week. Beyond the sensory rush of the souks, Marrakech holds a cluster of palaces, tombs and a madrasa that reward a calm half-day ●, and they sit close enough together in the southern medina that a single walking loop covers most of them. The Bahia Palace is the one most first-timers remember and the best place to see Moroccan craftsmanship at palace scale; give it a proper hour to wander rooms and gardens rather than rushing ●. The Saadian Tombs are small and bottleneck at the famous main mausoleum, so arrive early to see it without a queue ●. Doing these as a loop in one unhurried morning is the difference between three monuments and three separate expeditions across a crowded medina.

The souks are not a set piece so much as a medium you move through, and they are where the anchor-price mechanism from decision 01 is most concentrated. Going in daylight, entering from a known side, and treating the whole thing as a walk rather than a shopping trip removes most of the pressure. Buy on the second day, not the first.

Two full days in Marrakech is right for a first trip, ordered as: arrival evening at Jemaa el-Fnaa from a rooftop then within; a full day for the souks and the monument loop; and a half-day for Majorelle or the new city, which exists in this itinerary specifically as relief from the medina's intensity. Three days if you want a hammam and a slow afternoon, which is a good use of a third day.

No field-sheet entry — ticket prices and hours move; the order does not.

## The scholar's city, and the first morning

If Marrakech is Morocco's showman, Fez is its scholar: older, deeper, and more traditional ●. It is home to the world's largest car-free urban area, a genuinely living medieval city that many travellers end up loving even more than Marrakech, and it is also the most bewildering to navigate, so a little preparation goes a long way ●.

THE PIECE · THE MOVE

The case for the guided first morning is made in decision 06 and it is strongest here, in the one city where self-guided arrival reliably costs hours. What the guide buys is orientation: a mental map with two or three fixed points on it, after which the medina stops being an undifferentiated maze and becomes a place with directions.

The tanneries are the set piece and also the clearest small lesson in how the commission model works. The famous view is from a terrace, and the "entry" is really access to a shop's balcony, which is why so much of what happens around the tannery is about steering you into a purchase ●. Knowing that changes your posture entirely. You do not need a guide to find the tanneries; walk to the area yourself and step into any leather shop that offers a terrace view ●. A few dirham for the terrace is reasonable and buys goodwill; a large "fee" demanded aggressively is not, and you can decline and use a different shop ●. You are never obliged to buy the jackets and bags, however warmly they are presented ●. And the mint sprig handed to you at the top is not theatre: the tanning process uses natural methods that smell genuinely strong ●.

Fez also sits differently in the country's geography, which matters for decision 03. It pairs naturally with Chefchaouen, about four hours north by bus ●, and on a typical first-timer loop many travellers arrive in Fez after the desert and either leave from there or continue north ●. That is precisely the open-jaw shape, and Fez is the city that makes it work.

Two days in Fez, opened by a licensed guide on the first morning and closed by an unguided wander on the second, is the pattern we would recommend ◈, and the one behind most travellers who end up preferring Fez to Marrakech. Walk to the tanneries yourself. Refuse the aggressive terrace fee and use the next shop along.

## Chefchaouen, Essaouira, or the Atlas?

Once the loop is set, most travellers have room for exactly one more thing, and the three candidates are genuinely different products rather than variations on a theme. This is a decision about what your trip is missing, not about which place is best.

WHAT IT ADDS · WHAT IT COSTS

Chefchaouen is unlike anywhere else in the country ●. Tucked into the Rif Mountains, its medina is washed in every shade of blue, from powder to cobalt, spilling down the hillside ●. What makes it valuable in an itinerary is the register, not the colour that photographs have already sold you: it is small, relaxed and refreshingly low-hassle, the gentle counterpoint to the intensity of Marrakech and Fez ●. Travellers who add it after the desert and Fez arrive tired of being sold to and find a town that mostly is not selling.

The catch is access. Chefchaouen has no airport and no train station, so you arrive by road ●, which in practice means a CTM or Supratours coach or a grand taxi, about four hours from Fez or two from Tangier ●. It is most often visited as part of a northern leg, either after Fez heading north, or on the way to or from Tangier ●. Slotting it into a seven-day Marrakech–desert–Fez week means adding days, not rearranging them.

Essaouira solves a different problem. It is the coast: mild, breezy, and the reason Moroccans go there in summer when inland cities exceed 100°F ●. If your trip is in June, July or August, Essaouira stops being optional and starts being the thermal escape valve. It is also the seafood answer to a week of tagine.

The Atlas is the cheapest addition in time. Foothill day trips run from Marrakech and give you mountains, Berber villages and a change of air inside a single day, which is why decision 03 lists it as the natural companion to a Marrakech-only trip.

There is a fourth answer worth naming, which is nothing. A seven-day trip that already contains two great cities and a desert night does not have a hole in it. The impulse to add a third place usually comes from the map rather than from the trip, and our reporting is consistent on this point: the travellers who most enjoy Morocco are almost always the ones who did less, but did it well ●. Adding Chefchaouen to a week means either cutting the desert short or arriving home unrested, and neither trade is good.

If you have seven days, take the Atlas day trip and save the rest for a second visit. If you have ten, add Chefchaouen after Fez, because the loop already puts you within reach and the change of pace at that point in the trip is worth more than another medina. Take Essaouira instead if you are travelling in high summer or if the coast is what you were missing.

## The first three hours

Everything in decision 01 arrives at once, in an airport, while you are tired. This chapter is that hour written out in order, because the money web is at its most effective against people who are solving four problems simultaneously with luggage in their hands.

THE MOMENT · THE MOVE

The distance from the airport to the medina is trivial; the fare is where first-timers most reliably overpay ●. That sentence is worth reading twice, because it locates the problem precisely. This is not a long or difficult journey. It is a short journey conducted at the exact moment your defences are lowest, which is why our reporting ranks the options by how much hassle each removes rather than by price.

The lowest-friction choice is to arrange an airport pickup through your riad or hotel in advance, at a fixed price agreed beforehand ●. For a first arrival the small premium over a haggled taxi is usually worth every dirham ●, and the reason is not laziness. Medina riads are often unreachable by car, so arrival involves meeting someone and walking in through lanes you do not know ●. A prearranged transfer folds that problem into the price, which is exactly what you want when you are jet-lagged and it is dark ●.

The cash sequence has an order too, and it is the reverse of what people do instinctively. Withdraw from an ATM on arrival, carry small notes, and spend down your cash before you fly ●. Small notes matter more than the total: taxis, tips, terrace fees and stalls all run on them, and breaking a large note is a recurring small friction you can simply avoid by asking for a mixture at the start. The export ceiling of 2,000 MAD ◇ is the reason for the "spend down before you fly" half of the rule, and it is why leftover dirham is a problem best solved in the last two days rather than at the gate.

Arriving already online is the multiplier on all of this. With data running you can check the map, message the riad, and confirm the transfer without hunting for airport Wi-Fi ●, which means the three problems above get solved in sequence rather than all at once.

Book the transfer with the riad, arrive with an eSIM live, draw cash from a bank ATM in the hall and ask for small notes, and decline the dollar-billing offer at every terminal. Understanding how the money works before you land removes a whole category of first-day fumbling ●.

## And the hammam

Morocco is a welcoming, Muslim country used to visitors, so the aim is respect rather than performance ●. That framing is worth holding onto, because anxious travellers tend to overcorrect into a costume, which nobody asked for and which reads as strangely as underdressing.

Dressing modestly, shoulders and knees covered, looser rather than tight, is the single most useful habit, showing respect and reducing unwanted attention ●. It applies more in rural areas and religious sites than on a city rooftop, and you will see a range on other travellers, but erring modest is always the safe, easy call ●. The scarf is the most useful object in the bag for exactly this reason: it converts one outfit into two contexts.

The hand convention is small and easy and noticed: use your right hand for eating, giving and receiving, since the left is traditionally considered unclean ●. Nobody will correct you, which is precisely why getting it right registers.

The hammam deserves more space than etiquette guides give it, because it is the one cultural practice most travellers skip out of uncertainty and most regret skipping. It is a ritual of deep cleansing and relaxation practised across the Maghreb, and in Morocco it is woven into ordinary life, with many locals going weekly ●. The result is a level of clean and relaxed you rarely reach otherwise, and it is genuinely worth doing at least once ●.

There are two very different experiences under the same name ●. The public neighbourhood hammam is cheap, authentic and local: you bring your own kit, share the steam rooms with locals, and either scrub yourself or pay an attendant ●. The tourist or spa hammam is more expensive, private or semi-private, and includes the scrub, oils and pampering in a polished setting ●. Neither is the "real" one in any useful sense; they are different products, and the honest advice is that a first-timer who wants the ritual without the uncertainty should take the spa version, while a traveller who wants the thing itself should take the public one and accept twenty minutes of not knowing quite what happens next.

Dress modestly by default and carry a scarf; use your right hand; ask before photographing. Do the hammam once, and choose the version by how much uncertainty you want, not by which is more authentic. Both are.

## What to book, and when

Almost nothing in Morocco requires booking far ahead, which is why the few things that do catch people out. This is the whole booking calendar on one page, ordered by how early it needs to happen.

HOW FAR OUT · WHAT

The open-jaw ticket sits at the top of the list not because airlines sell out but because it is the decision that everything else inherits. Booked as a loop, your trip has a backtracking day baked into it that no amount of later cleverness removes. Booked as a line, the desert and Fez fall into place. That is why decision 03 exists before the rest.

Riads are the genuine scarcity in the system. A riad is a house, not a hotel, which means the appealing ones have six or eight rooms rather than sixty, and in April, May, October and November ● those rooms are gone early. This is the one accommodation category where booking two or three months out is not anxiety, it is arithmetic.

The desert leg wants a month or two because you are booking a vehicle and a camp rather than a seat, and because the good operators are running finite trips. The high-speed rail is the small surprise: Al Boraq is booked separately from the ordinary network and fills faster ●, so a corridor journey you assumed you could take on the day may already be full at the time you want.

Everything else genuinely can wait. Ordinary trains, coaches, monuments and a licensed guide arranged through your riad are all same-week decisions, and treating them as such is what leaves the trip room to breathe.

Book the open-jaw flights first and the riads second, both further out than feels necessary. Book the desert and the driver a month or two ahead. Leave the rest alone until you are there; the flexibility is worth more than the certainty.

## Closing

Morocco rewards the traveller who arrives unafraid and unhurried.

The dunes at Erg Chebbi are worth every hour of the drive. The first morning with a real guide in the Fez medina turns a thousand-year maze into a place you can read. Jemaa el-Fnaa seen from a rooftop and then from within, in that order, on your first evening, is one of the great arrivals in travel. The mint tea is poured from a height for the theatre of it, and the theatre is the point.

None of the traps in this book are the country's true face. They are the friction at the edge, the tax on arriving unprepared. Pay it once, learn it, and it never troubles you again. Everything that made you want to come is still there, undiminished, waiting on the other side of a little preparation.
