----- morocco-atm-fees-and-limits -----
TITLE: ATMs in Morocco: Fees, Limits, and Which Banks to Use

 Because you can only get dirham once you arrive, the ATM is not an occasional errand in Morocco — it is the single source of nearly all the money you will spend. That makes three things worth understanding before your first withdrawal: what each machine charges, how much it will let you take at once, and the screen near the end that quietly offers you a worse exchange rate. Get these right and cash costs you almost nothing extra. 

 

 The two fees on every withdrawal 
 A Moroccan ATM withdrawal can carry two separate charges: one from the local bank operating the machine, levied per withdrawal regardless of amount, and one from your own US bank, either a flat foreign-ATM fee or a percentage on the conversion. The local charge is the reason to withdraw larger amounts less often, since it is the same whether you take out a little or a lot. 
 Check your own card first 
 Your American bank's terms decide half of this before you leave home. Some checking accounts refund foreign ATM fees entirely; many travel-oriented cards charge no foreign transaction fee at all. Knowing which category your card falls into is worth ten minutes on your bank's website, because it changes the right withdrawal size and can save more than any tactic at the machine. 

 Which banks' machines to prefer 
 The major Moroccan banks — Attijariwafa, Bank of Africa, Banque Populaire and CIH — operate reliable ATMs across cities, towns and both main airports, and they are the ones to use. Their machines are well maintained, dispense reliably, and sit inside or beside branches where help exists if a card is retained. The ones to be wary of are the standalone, brightly branded machines aimed squarely at tourists, which tend to charge more and push conversion hardest. 
 Use machines attached to branches 
 Withdrawing from a machine physically attached to a bank branch, during opening hours, is the safest habit. If the machine keeps your card — rare, but it happens — you can walk inside immediately rather than losing it to a wall on a Sunday. It also puts you somewhere lit and staffed, which is the ordinary sensible choice for handling cash anywhere. 

 Withdrawal limits and empty machines 
 Moroccan ATMs cap each withdrawal, and the cap is often lower than an American traveller expects, so a single transaction may not cover a full desert leg. You can sometimes withdraw twice, but each withdrawal triggers the local fee again. Machines also run dry, especially on weekends and in smaller towns, so treat a working, well-stocked ATM in a decent-sized town as an opportunity rather than assuming the next one will serve you. 

 The screen that costs you: "convert to dollars?" 
 Near the end of the withdrawal, many machines ask whether you want to be charged in dirham or in your home currency, presenting the second option as a helpful convenience. Always choose dirham. Choosing dollars hands the exchange rate to the ATM's operator, a practice called dynamic currency conversion, and the rate they apply is consistently worse than the one your own card network would have used. The convenience is the trap. 
 Why it looks like a favour 
 The offer is framed to reassure — "we'll show you the amount in your own money" — which sounds like transparency and is actually a markup of a few per cent, applied every single time you accept it. Over a two-week trip of regular withdrawals, always accepting it is a meaningful sum handed over for nothing. The rule is simple and never varies: pay in dirham, let your bank convert. 

 Blocked cards and how to avoid them 
 A US card that stops working at a Moroccan machine is a common and preventable problem, usually triggered by your own bank's automated fraud systems flagging a first foreign withdrawal. Set a travel notice before you leave, and carry a second card from a different bank, kept in a separate place. The reserve card is worth more than any fee saved, because it is the difference between an inconvenience and an emergency. 
 If a machine eats your card 
 Should a machine retain your card, using one attached to a bank branch during opening hours means you walk straight inside to recover it. Outside hours, note the bank and location, call your card issuer to freeze the card, and rely on the backup. This is the specific scenario the branch-attached rule and the second card exist to defuse. 

 A sensible withdrawal rhythm 
 Put the pieces together and a pattern falls out: use a major-bank ATM attached to a branch, withdraw a larger amount every few days rather than small daily sums, always decline the dollar conversion, and top up fully before any desert or rural leg. Carry a second card from a different bank in a separate pocket in case one is blocked or eaten. That is the whole discipline, and it makes the machine a non-event. 

 

 The ATM's fees, its cap, and that conversion screen are small in isolation and compound across a trip — and they connect to what you carry, where you can spend it, and how far you can get from a bank. Those links are worked through in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For how much to hold at any one time, see  how much cash to carry ; for the dollar-conversion screen in full, our  dirhams-not-dollars guide  goes deeper.

----- morocco-pay-in-dirhams-dcc -----
TITLE: “Dollars or Dirhams?” The Card Screen That Costs You in Morocco

 Somewhere in nearly every ATM withdrawal and card payment in Morocco, a screen asks whether you would like to be charged in dirham or in US dollars. The dollar option is presented as a courtesy, and it is the one moment where a Moroccan machine will reliably cost you money for nothing. The answer is always dirham, and understanding why makes the choice automatic for the rest of the trip. 

 

 What the screen is actually offering 
 The dollar option is a service called dynamic currency conversion, in which the Moroccan machine or terminal — not your bank — performs the currency conversion and picks the exchange rate. It looks like a convenience because it shows the amount in money you recognise. What it hides is that the rate applied is set by the merchant's payment processor, and it is built to be worse than the rate your own card network would use. 
 Who pockets the difference 
 The gap between that inflated rate and the real one is revenue, split between the terminal operator and the processor behind it. It is typically a few per cent, small enough on a single coffee to ignore and large enough across a fortnight of daily payments to matter. Nothing about it is illegal or hidden in the fine print — you are simply being asked to opt into a bad rate, politely. 

 Why "dirham" is always right 
 Choosing to be charged in dirham sends the transaction to your own bank in local currency, and your card network then converts it at the interbank rate, the genuine wholesale rate that is essentially always better than the terminal's. Your bank may still add its own foreign-transaction fee, but that fee applies either way — accepting dollars does not remove it, it just adds a second markup on top. 

 The same trick with a human face 
 A softer version happens without any screen: a shop or hotel simply offers to charge your card "in dollars, easier for you", framing it as a kindness. It is the same dynamic currency conversion, chosen on your behalf, and it carries the same inflated rate. Decline it exactly as you would the machine's prompt, and ask to be charged in dirham instead. 
 It is not the vendor cheating you 
 The individual shopkeeper rarely sets or even understands the rate — the markup lives in the payment processor behind the terminal. This matters because there is nothing to argue about and nobody to be annoyed at: you simply choose the local currency and the whole thing resolves. Politeness costs you nothing and the correct answer is always available. 

 Where you'll meet it 
 The choice appears in three places: at ATMs during a withdrawal, at card terminals in shops and restaurants that take cards, and occasionally on hotel bills at checkout. The wording varies — "with conversion", "in your home currency", "guaranteed in USD" — but the tell is always the same: if a screen shows you a dollar figure and asks you to confirm, it is offering dynamic currency conversion. Decline and choose the local amount. 
 When the choice is made for you 
 Occasionally a terminal is set to convert automatically, and a receipt shows a dollar total you never agreed to. You can ask for the transaction to be voided and re-run in dirham, and a reputable business will do it. It is worth a polite word for a large amount, though rarely worth the friction for a small one. 

 The one-sentence rule 
 You do not need to track exchange rates or do mental maths to get this right. Whenever a Moroccan machine or terminal offers you a choice of currency, pick dirham, every time, without exception. That single habit captures the entire benefit and removes the only decision the screen was trying to make complicated. 

 

 This screen is one instance of a pattern that runs through a Moroccan trip: a cost that arrives dressed as a convenience, paid by you to someone you never see. Where else that shape appears, and how to spot it before you confirm, is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the machine itself, see our  ATM fees and limits guide ; for what still has to be paid in cash,  how much to carry  covers the rest. 

 
   Dollars or dirhams: FAQ 
    What is dynamic currency conversion?   A service where the Moroccan machine converts the charge to your home currency at a rate it sets — consistently worse than your own bank's rate.   
    So which should I pick?   Dirham, always. It sends the conversion to your own card network at the interbank rate.   
    Does paying in dirham avoid all fees?   No — your bank's own foreign-transaction fee may still apply, but that fee applies either way. Choosing dollars only adds a second markup on top.   
    What if the receipt already shows dollars?   Ask for it to be voided and re-run in dirham. A reputable business will do it; it is worth the effort on a large amount.   
    How much does accepting dollars cost?   Usually a few percent per transaction — trivial once, meaningful across two weeks of daily payments.   
 


----- tipping-in-morocco -----
TITLE: Tipping in Morocco: Who, How Much, and When

 Tipping in Morocco is genuinely expected but modest, and it is the daily habit that quietly determines whether you always have the right change in your pocket. Nobody is looking for American-sized percentages; what people appreciate is a small, prompt tip in cash for a service done. The whole practice runs on small notes and coins, which is why tipping and the scramble for change are really the same subject. 

 

 The general rule 
 Moroccan tipping is expected but modest across the board: think small amounts, given in cash, at the moment of the service. In restaurants without a service charge, rounding up to roughly ten per cent is generous; for most other services a handful of dirham is right. This is a culture of frequent small gratuities rather than occasional large ones, and getting the scale right is more about frequency than size. 
 Always cash, always small notes 
 Tips are paid in physical cash, never added to a card payment, so a tip you cannot make change for is a tip you cannot give. This is the practical reason the guides on this site keep pushing you to break large notes and hold a float of small ones. A pocket of five, ten and twenty dirham notes is the infrastructure that makes every situation below easy. 

 Restaurants and cafes 
 In a sit-down restaurant with no service charge, rounding the bill up by around ten per cent is a warm tip and more than acceptable. At a casual cafe, leaving the coins from your change, or a few dirham, is normal. Check the bill first: some tourist-facing places already add a service charge, in which case an extra tip is optional rather than expected. 

 Guides, drivers, and porters 
 For guides, porters and hammam staff, the site's working range is  5 to 20 dirham  for a small service, scaling up with time and effort. A local guide who has given you a good half-day in the medina merits more than the porter who carried a bag up two flights, and a multi-day desert driver more again. For taxis, simply rounding the fare up is the norm rather than a set percentage. 
 Multi-day guides and drivers 
 On a multi-day tour, a driver or guide who has looked after you for two or three days is tipped at the end, in a lump, and more substantially than any single daily service. There is no fixed figure, but it should feel proportional to the days and the care. Agreeing nothing in advance and giving a genuine tip at the end is the normal, comfortable way this works. 

 Hammams, hotels, and small services 
 At a public hammam or a spa treatment, a small tip for the attendant is customary. In hotels, a few dirham for the porter and for housekeeping is appreciated though not obligatory. Parking attendants — the men in high-visibility vests who watch cars on the street — expect a couple of dirham, and it is worth having coins ready for them since you will meet them constantly. 

 A rough mental model for the day 
 Rather than memorising figures, carry one idea: small and often, in cash, at the moment. A coffee gets loose change, a helpful porter a couple of small notes, a good half-day guide a real handful, a multi-day driver a proper lump at the end. Scaled to time and care, that instinct covers almost every situation you will meet without a table to consult. 
 Keep a tipping stash separate 
 Some travellers keep a dedicated pocket of small notes purely for tips, replenished whenever they break a large bill. It removes the awkward moment of digging for change in front of the person you are tipping, and it guarantees you are never caught out at exactly the wrong time. It is a tiny habit that makes the whole practice feel effortless. 

 When not to tip 
 Tipping is not universal, and forcing it where it does not belong marks you as a target. You are not obliged to add anything when a service charge already appears on the bill, and shop purchases are simply not tipped. Rounding up a taxi is a tip; adding to a card total is not how it works here. 
 One demand is not a tip at all. When someone provides an unrequested service and then asks for money, that is a transaction to decline, and handing money over only encourages a repeat. Politely refusing is the right move, not negotiating an amount. 

 

 Tipping looks trivial and is really the daily edge of a bigger habit: Morocco runs on small cash, and holding the right notes is what keeps every small transaction smooth.     Can I add a tip to my card payment?   No. Tips are paid in cash, which is why keeping small notes and coins on you matters.

----- merzouga-vs-zagora -----
TITLE: Merzouga vs. Zagora: Which Sahara Trip to Take

 Merzouga and Zagora are the two Sahara trips sold from Marrakech, and choosing between them looks like a question about scenery when it is really a question about time. Merzouga has the tall, cinematic dunes and costs you more driving; Zagora is closer and quicker with smaller, stonier dunes. Which is right depends almost entirely on how many days you can hand to the desert. 

 

 The honest difference: dunes and driving 
 Merzouga sits beside Erg Chebbi, the tall golden dune sea that is the postcard Sahara, and reaching it means roughly a 9 to 10 hour drive from Marrakech, so realistically a 3-day, 2-night trip. Zagora is closer, doable as a shorter 2-day, 1-night trip, but its desert is smaller and rockier rather than the sweeping sand most people picture. That trade — bigger dunes for more driving — is the whole decision. 
 Why most first-timers choose Merzouga 
 For a traveller chasing the classic desert image, Merzouga is worth the extra day, because Erg Chebbi delivers the dramatic landscape that Zagora simply does not have. The longer drive is broken by genuine highlights — the Tizi n'Tichka pass, Aït Benhaddou, the Todra Gorge — so the journey earns its length rather than merely enduring it. If the Sahara is a headline reason you came to Morocco, this is the one. 

 When Zagora is the right call 
 Zagora makes sense when time is the binding constraint rather than the scenery. If you have only a spare two days, a Zagora overnight still gives you a camel ride, a night under desert stars and a camp dinner without writing off three days of a short trip. It is the sensible answer for a week that is already full, not a lesser version of the same experience. 

 What both trips share 
 Whichever you choose, the shape is the same: a long drive out with scenic stops, a camel ride into the dunes toward sunset, a night in a camp with a communal tagine dinner and stargazing, and a sunrise before the drive back. There is no airport or train to the dunes — Errachidia exists near Merzouga but is rarely used by tourists — so both are reached overland, by tour or private driver. Most first-timers take a tour with a driver rather than self-driving. 
 The camp is where the money splits 
 Both routes offer standard and luxury camps, and this is where prices diverge more than the destination does. A standard camp is a genuine tented night with shared facilities; a luxury camp adds private bathrooms, better bedding and a quieter setting. The choice of camp changes the cost of the trip as much as the choice of Merzouga over Zagora, and it is a separate decision worth making deliberately. 

 From Marrakech or from Fez 
 Both dune fields can also be reached from Fez, and many Merzouga tours run one-way, starting in one city and ending in the other. This is genuinely useful on a classic loop, because it saves you repeating the long southern drive in reverse. A one-way Marrakech-to-Fez desert trip threads the Sahara into the middle of the itinerary instead of dangling it off one end. 
 Errachidia and the no-fly reality 
 There is a small airport at Errachidia near Merzouga, but tourists almost never use it, and there is no rail anywhere near the dunes. In practice the desert is always an overland journey, which is exactly why the driving time is the real cost of the trip and why the choice between the two dune fields is a choice about days. Plan the transport before you fall in love with a photograph of Erg Chebbi. 

 How the desert leg reshapes the rest 
 The desert is not a side excursion you slot in; it is the piece the itinerary bends around. A Merzouga trip removes three days from a week and dictates that you leave from and often return to Marrakech, while a Zagora trip frees a day for a city or the coast. Decide the desert first, then build the rest of the route around the days it leaves you.

----- solo-female-travel-morocco -----
TITLE: Solo Female Travel in Morocco: An Honest Guide

 Morocco is a well-travelled and rewarding destination for women on their own, and the honest picture is one of persistent attention rather than genuine danger. Violent crime against tourists is rare; what a solo woman actually manages is unwanted comments, being approached in the street, and the assumption that she needs help she did not ask for. Knowing that in advance turns most of it from unsettling into merely tedious. 

 

 What to actually expect 
 The most common experience for a solo woman in Morocco is verbal attention — comments, greetings, persistent offers to guide or sell — rather than anything physical. It is frequent, it can be wearing over a long day, and it is very rarely threatening. Treating it as background noise to move through, rather than a series of encounters to resolve, is the mindset most experienced solo travellers describe settling into. 
 The faux guide dynamic, amplified 
 The unofficial-guide approach that every visitor meets tends to land harder on a woman alone, because a solo traveller reads as an easier mark. The response is the same as for anyone: a firm, friendly refusal while continuing to walk, repeated once without slowing. Engaging, explaining or apologising extends the interaction; brief and unbothered ends it. 

 Dress and how it changes the day 
 Morocco is a Muslim country, and dressing modestly — shoulders and knees covered, looser rather than tight — noticeably reduces attention while showing respect for local norms. This is not about a strict code for visitors, and you will see a range on other tourists, but the practical payoff is real: covered clothing simply draws less comment. A scarf in the bag is useful for mosques' surroundings and for cooler desert evenings. 

 Where and when to be more deliberate 
 Daytime in the medinas and main areas is straightforward with ordinary awareness. After dark, quiet medina lanes are worth avoiding alone in favour of busier streets and taxis, exactly as you would treat an unfamiliar city anywhere. A petit taxi is cheap and removes the walk home through empty lanes; it is money well spent for peace of mind at night. 
 Riads as a base 
 Staying in a riad rather than a large hotel tends to suit solo travellers, because the small scale means staff who recognise you, help with directions, and arrange trusted taxis and guides. That personal layer is genuinely reassuring on a first solo trip, and it is one more reason the medina riad is the common first-timer recommendation. 

 Guided or independent, as a solo woman 
 The guided-versus-independent question has an extra dimension for a woman travelling alone: a reputable multi-day tour or a licensed local guide provides company and a buffer against constant approaches, which some solo travellers value highly. Others find the cities perfectly manageable alone and prefer the freedom. Neither is safer in any dramatic sense; it is a comfort choice, and a hybrid — independent cities, guided desert — suits many first solo trips. 
 Meeting other travellers 
 Morocco has a well-worn traveller trail, and riads, group desert tours and cooking classes are natural places to fall in with others for a stretch if you want company. A solo trip here rarely means solitary unless you want it to. Group day tours in particular are an easy, low-commitment way to share the more remote parts of the country. 

 Practical habits that help 
 A few small habits smooth the trip: wear a wedding-style ring if the "are you married?" line wears thin, keep your phone loaded with offline maps so you never stand lost in a lane, learn a couple of firm Arabic or French phrases for declining, and trust the instinct that says leave a situation. None of these is about fear; they are the same street sense you would use in any big city, tuned slightly for the context. 

 

 The realistic challenges of travelling Morocco alone are mostly about reading intentions — who is being friendly, who is working, who is paid by whom — which is the same thread that runs through the country's whole commercial texture. ----- what-to-pack-for-morocco -----
TITLE: What to Pack for Morocco

 Packing for Morocco trips people up because a single trip spans genuinely different conditions: hot city days, cold desert nights, modest-dress expectations, and miles of walking on uneven medina ground. Pack for only the heat and the desert night ambushes you; pack heavy and the medina stairs punish you. The goal is a light bag that covers a wide range, and that comes down to a handful of deliberate choices. 

 

 Dress modestly, and layer for it 
 Morocco is a Muslim country where covered shoulders and knees are the respectful norm, so the core wardrobe is loose, lightweight clothing that covers without overheating. Breathable trousers, long skirts and tops with sleeves handle both the modesty expectation and the sun. A light scarf is the single most useful item, working for sun, mosque surroundings, cool evenings and dusty desert roads all at once. 
 Why loose beats light 
 In the heat, loose covered clothing is genuinely more comfortable than shorts and a tank top, because it keeps the sun off your skin while letting air move. It also removes the constant low-level attention that more revealing clothing draws. This is the rare case where the respectful choice and the comfortable choice are the same choice. 

 Warm layers for the desert and mountains 
 The Sahara and the High Atlas get genuinely cold after dark, even when the cities are hot, and this catches out travellers who packed only for a warm holiday. A warm fleece or jacket, plus something long-sleeved to sleep in at a desert camp, is essential rather than optional. Nights under the dunes drop far more than the daytime heat suggests. 

 Shoes for uneven ground 
 You will walk a great deal on uneven, sometimes slippery medina stones and packed earth, so closed, comfortable, broken-in walking shoes matter more than any style consideration. Sandals are fine for downtime but poor for a full day in the souks. If a desert trip involves any walking on dunes, know that footwear fills with sand instantly — many people simply go barefoot for that part. 

 Sun, water and small practicalities 
 Strong sun means a hat, sunglasses and high-factor sunscreen earn their space year-round. A refillable water bottle is useful, though stick to bottled or filtered water to drink. Pack any medications you rely on, since specific brands can be hard to find, and bring a small supply of hand sanitiser and tissues — public facilities do not always provide either. 
 Power and connectivity 
 Morocco uses European-style two-pin plugs, so bring the right adapter, and a power bank is worth carrying for long days out and desert nights with no sockets. Download offline maps before rural legs where signal disappears. These are small items that prevent specific, predictable annoyances rather than general ones. 

 A small daypack for the cities 
 A light daypack earns its place for city days and the desert, carrying water, a scarf, sunscreen, a power bank and whatever you buy along the way. Worn on the front in dense souk crowds, it also sidesteps the pickpocketing that thrives on distraction. Choose one that closes properly rather than an open tote, and it doubles as your desert-camp overnight bag when the main luggage stays behind. 
 Packing for the desert overnight 
 Most desert trips let you leave the main bag at a Marrakech riad or the camp's base kasbah and take only an overnight bag into the dunes. Pack that small bag with the warm layer, something to sleep in, a toothbrush, water and a charged power bank, since camps often have no power. Travelling into the dunes light, with the bulk left behind, is far more comfortable than dragging a full case across sand. 

 What you don't need to bring 
 Skip the dirham — you cannot buy it before you arrive anyway — and skip overpacking clothes, since laundry is cheap and easy and a scarf or two restyles an outfit. Leave expensive jewellery and anything you would hate to lose at home. Morocco is a place to travel light through, and a bag you can carry up a riad staircase yourself is worth more than any single item you left behind. 

 

 Most of this packing list traces back to the country's real shape — the heat-and-cold spread, the modesty norm, the walking, the closed currency you cannot pre-buy.
