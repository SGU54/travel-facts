----- morocco-entry-requirements -----
TITLE: Do You Need a Visa for Morocco? US Citizen Rules

 For US citizens, Morocco is one of the easiest countries to enter: no visa, no application, and no fee — you arrive with a valid passport and are admitted for up to 90 days as a tourist. There is one electronic-authorization system worth understanding because it may expand in future, and a passport-validity rule that trips people up, but the headline is genuinely simple. Here is exactly what you need, and what you can ignore. 

 

 The short answer for Americans 
 US citizens do not need a visa to visit Morocco for tourism and may stay up to 90 days on a passport alone — there is nothing to apply for and nothing to pay in advance. The same visa-free entry applies to UK, EU, Canadian and Australian citizens and dozens of other nationalities. You simply book your flights, turn up, and get an entry stamp at passport control. For the standard first trip, that is the whole story. 
 Verify close to travel 
 Entry rules are exactly the kind of fact that can change, so confirm the current position against an official Moroccan consular source or the US State Department shortly before you fly, rather than relying on any single guide. This page reflects the rules as they stand, but a quick check near your travel date is the responsible habit for any border crossing. It takes minutes and removes the only real uncertainty. 

 The AEVM system: watch, don't worry 
 Morocco has been rolling out an Electronic Travel Authorization known as AEVM, and it is worth understanding precisely so you neither ignore a future change nor pay for something you do not need. As things stand, the AEVM applies to certain nationalities and does not currently apply to US, UK or EU visa-free travellers — Americans do not need one today. Morocco has signalled it may extend the system to visa-exempt countries in future, which is exactly why you verify close to travel. 
 Don't pay a third-party "visa" site 
 Because visa-free entry costs nothing, any website charging Americans a fee for a "Morocco tourist visa" is one to avoid — these third-party sites sell you a document you do not need. If a future AEVM requirement does arrive for US citizens, it will be handled through Morocco's official portal, not a middleman. Treat any fee-charging "visa service" for a visa-free trip as the red flag it is. 

 Passport validity: the real catch 
 The rule that actually catches people is passport validity, where sources differ: Morocco's official requirement is more lenient, but many airlines will deny boarding if your passport expires within six months of travel, so the safe standard is a passport valid at least six months beyond your arrival date. Make sure you also have at least one blank page for the entry stamp. Checking your passport's expiry date well before you book is the single most useful thing on this page. 

 Proof of onward travel 
 Airlines and occasionally immigration may ask for evidence of onward or return travel, so have your return or onward ticket accessible at check-in. It is rarely a problem for a normal round-trip, but travellers on open-ended or one-way itineraries should be ready to show a plan to leave. Keeping the booking on your phone is enough to satisfy the question quickly if it comes up. 

 What you don't need 
 You do not need a visa, an advance application, a paid authorization, or — for a standard trip — any vaccination certificate. The temptation is to over-prepare for a country that has made entry deliberately easy; resist it, and put the saved energy into checking your passport's expiry and confirming the rules close to departure. For a US tourist, Morocco's front door is about as open as they come. 

 

 Entry is the first of a short sequence that opens every Morocco trip — passport, then cash, then the ride into town — and getting it clear removes the only real friction from arrival. How that sequence fits together is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the stamp itself, see our  passport and entry-stamp guide ; for the arrival that follows, the  Casablanca airport guide  covers the first two hours.

----- morocco-passport-entry-stamp -----
TITLE: Morocco Entry Stamp and Passport Rules Explained

 The Moroccan entry stamp is a small thing that occasionally becomes a large one, and a few seconds of attention at passport control saves the rare but real hassle it can cause. Alongside it sit the passport-validity and blank-page rules that decide whether you board your flight at all. None of this is complicated, but all of it is worth getting right before you travel, because the fixes are trivial in advance and awkward after. Here is what to know. 

 

 Why the entry stamp matters 
 When you arrive, an immigration officer stamps your passport, and that stamp is your proof of legal entry and the date your 90-day stay began — it is what an officer checks when you leave. On busy arrival days a rushed official occasionally forgets to stamp, or stamps illegibly, and a missing entry stamp can turn your departure into a question: when and where did you actually enter Morocco? Catching it at the moment costs nothing. 
 Check it before you walk away 
 Take ten seconds at the counter to confirm your passport has a clear, legible entry stamp before you leave the booth. If it is missing or smudged, ask the officer to correct it there and then, while you are still in front of them. This is routine and no officer will mind; the alternative is explaining the gap on the way out, potentially to someone less inclined to be helpful. The habit is trivial and the payoff is real. 

 The six-month passport rule 
 Passport validity is where travellers most often come unstuck, and the sources genuinely differ: Morocco's official requirement is more lenient, but many airlines enforce a six-month rule and will deny boarding if your passport expires within six months of your travel dates. The safe standard, therefore, is a passport valid at least six months beyond your arrival. Check the expiry date the moment you start planning, because renewing takes time you may not have close to departure. 

 Blank pages 
 You also need at least one blank page in your passport for the entry stamp, and it is worth having a spare beyond that if your passport is filling up. Travellers near the end of a well-used passport sometimes arrive with no room for the stamp, which creates an unnecessary problem at the border. If your passport is close to full, add pages or renew before you go rather than gambling on the officer finding space. 

 Overstaying and the exit check 
 Your entry stamp starts the clock on a 90-day permitted stay, and overstaying it can mean fines or complications on departure, so note your entry date and keep within the window. For the standard trip of a week or two this is a non-issue, but travellers combining Morocco with a longer regional journey should track the date. If circumstances force a longer stay, address it with the authorities before the 90 days elapse rather than at the airport. 

 Photocopies and backups 
 Before you travel, photograph or scan the photo page of your passport and store it somewhere accessible offline on your phone and in your email, so a lost or stolen passport is a recoverable problem rather than a stranded one. Carrying a paper photocopy separately from the passport itself is the old-fashioned version and still works. Should the worst happen, a copy speeds up the replacement process at your embassy considerably. 
 Carrying it day to day 
 You rarely need the physical passport once you are in the country, so many travellers leave it in the riad safe and carry a photo or photocopy for daily use, reducing the chance of losing the real thing in a crowded souk. Some hotels ask to see it at check-in and note the details, which is normal. Keeping the original secure and a copy on you is the sensible balance between having ID and protecting the document. 

 Dual nationals, a quick note 
 If you hold both US and Moroccan nationality, the rules differ and it is worth checking with a Moroccan consulate before you travel, since dual nationals are generally treated as Moroccan citizens on entry and may face different documentation expectations. This does not affect single-nationality US travellers at all, but for dual nationals a short conversation with the consulate in advance heads off any surprise at the border. 

 

 The stamp and the passport rules are the fine print of the same arrival sequence the whole trip opens with — get them right and the border is a formality. How arrival, entry and the first hours fit together is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the visa position overall, see our  entry requirements guide ; for what happens after the stamp, the  Casablanca airport guide  covers arrival.

----- morocco-airport-money-exchange -----
TITLE: Exchanging Money at Moroccan Airports: A Reality Check

 Every American lands in Morocco with no local money, because the dirham is a closed currency you cannot buy before you fly — there is no way around getting your first cash on arrival. That makes the airport the site of your first financial decision, between the ATM and the exchange counter sitting a few metres apart in the arrivals hall. They are not equal, and the right choice in your first ten minutes saves you money and sets the tone for the trip. 

 

 Why you have no dirham yet 
 The dirham cannot legally leave Morocco freely and is not sold by US banks, so unlike euros or pounds you cannot arrive with a starter amount in your wallet. This closed-currency rule is the reason the airport cash decision exists at all, and it is worth understanding rather than being surprised by at the machine. Everyone around you in the arrivals hall is solving the same problem for the first time. 

 Use the ATM, not the counter 
 For almost every traveller, the bank ATM in the arrivals hall gives the better rate, and there are machines from the major Moroccan banks at both Casablanca and Marrakech airports. The exchange counter beside it is convenient, staffed and visible, and priced for people who feel they have no alternative at the end of a long flight. If your card works, walk past the counter to the machine. 
 Decline the dollar conversion 
 At the ATM, the machine may ask whether you want to be charged in dirham or in dollars — always choose dirham. The dollar option, dynamic currency conversion, lets the machine set a worse exchange rate than your own bank would, quietly costing you a few percent. This is the same prompt you will meet at card terminals all trip, and the answer never changes: pay in the local currency. 

 When the counter earns its keep 
 The exchange counter is not useless — it is the fallback if your card is blocked, damaged or simply not working, which happens often enough to plan for. In that case, change a small amount there to get into town and sort the rest properly the next day. This is exactly why carrying a couple of hundred dollars in clean, undamaged notes as backup is sensible: it turns a failed card from a crisis into a minor detour. 

 How much to take out 
 Withdraw enough for the first day or two rather than a token amount, since most US cards charge a flat fee per withdrawal and fewer, larger withdrawals cost less overall. Do not, however, take out a huge sum on arrival — you are tired, unfamiliar with the city, and can top up easily at bank machines in town. A sensible first withdrawal covers the airport transfer, the first meals and a cushion, and no more. 

 Which airport you land at 
 The two main arrival airports, Casablanca and Marrakech, both have bank ATMs in their arrivals halls, so the advice is the same wherever you land: use the machine, decline the dollar conversion, and take out enough for the first day or two. Casablanca, being the larger hub, has more machines and the train station beneath it; Marrakech drops you closer to where most first trips actually begin. Either way, the cash step is identical. 
 If the first machine fails 
 ATMs occasionally reject a card, run empty, or are out of service, so if the first machine does not work, try another from a different bank before assuming your card is the problem — the arrivals halls have several. Only after two machines fail is it worth changing a little at the counter as a stopgap. This small persistence saves the exchange-counter markup that a hasty traveller pays after one unlucky machine. 

 Before you leave the machine 
 Set a travel notice with your bank before you fly so a first foreign withdrawal is not flagged as fraud, and carry a second card from a different bank in a separate pocket in case the first fails. These two habits, decided at home, are what make the airport cash step a non-event. With dirham in hand and your phone connected, the rest of the arrival is simple logistics.

----- us-dollars-in-morocco -----
TITLE: Is US Currency Accepted in Morocco?

 Americans often ask whether they can spend US dollars in Morocco, and the honest answer is no — not for everyday purchases. Morocco runs on the dirham, a closed currency you cannot buy in advance, and the greengrocer, the taxi and the cafe all want local money. Dollars have a narrow, specific use as backup and for exchange, but the trip is paid in dirham. Here is where your dollars fit and, more importantly, where they do not. 

 

 The everyday answer: dirham only 
 For normal spending — food, taxis, souks, tickets, tips — you pay in dirham, and offering dollars will get you a blank look at a stall and a poor improvised rate at best anywhere else. The dirham is the currency of daily life, and there is no dollar economy running alongside it as there is in some heavily touristed countries. Plan to convert to dirham and spend dirham, full stop. 
 Why there's no dollar shortcut 
 Because the dirham is closed and tightly managed, Morocco does not have the informal dollar acceptance you find in some destinations, and trying to lean on dollars simply makes every transaction harder and more expensive. The efficient path is to withdraw dirham from an ATM on arrival and use it for everything. Fighting that with dollars is effort spent making the trip worse. 

 Where dollars do have a use 
 Dollars earn their place as backup and as exchange fuel, not as spending money. A couple of hundred dollars in clean, undamaged notes is sensible insurance if your card is blocked, lost or eaten by a machine: you can change them at a licensed bureau to get moving again. Some higher-end hotels or tour operators may also quote or accept dollars or euros for large bookings, but that is the exception, not daily life. 
 Bring clean, undamaged notes 
 If you do bring dollars as backup, their condition matters more than Americans expect: torn, marked or heavily worn bills are frequently refused at exchange bureaux, and older-series notes can be harder to change. Bring crisp, recent, undamaged bills, and keep them separate from your cards so a single lost bag cannot take both. Damaged dollars are backup that fails exactly when you need it. 

 What to do with leftover dollars 
 Any dollars you do not use as backup simply go home with you — unlike dirham, they are your own currency and lose nothing by returning unspent. This is precisely why dollars make good insurance: there is no penalty for carrying them there and back untouched. The asymmetry is worth remembering — hold dollars as a safety net, convert only what you need to dirham, and spend the dirham down to nothing before you fly. 

 Dollars versus euros as backup 
 If you are choosing a backup currency to carry, euros are marginally more widely recognised than dollars at Moroccan exchange bureaux, given Europe's proximity and tourism, though both change easily at a licensed bureau. For an American traveller, dollars are perfectly fine as backup and there is no need to acquire euros specially. The point is to carry some hard currency you can exchange if a card fails, and dollars serve that role without any fuss. 
 Keeping the backup safe 
 Wherever your backup cash comes from, split it from your cards and your everyday dirham so no single lost bag empties every source at once. A little in the room safe, a little on your person, and your cards elsewhere is the standard sensible spread. The whole value of backup dollars is that they survive whatever happened to your primary method, and that only holds if they are stored apart from it. 

 The practical takeaway 
 Treat dollars as a fire extinguisher, not a wallet: bring a modest amount of clean notes for emergencies, rely on your card and the ATM for actual dirham, and do your spending entirely in local currency. That division — dollars in reserve, dirham in hand — covers every money situation Morocco presents without ever needing a stall-holder to accept a currency they cannot use.

TITLE: Mobile Payments and Apps in Morocco

 Morocco is more digitally connected than first-timers expect, but the picture is uneven: contactless payments work where cards do, ride-hail and messaging apps genuinely smooth the trip, yet cash still rules the daily texture of souks, taxis and small cafes. Knowing which apps earn their place and where they simply do not reach saves you both hassle and the mistake of assuming your phone can replace a pocket of small notes. Here is what actually works. 

 

 Tap-to-pay, where cards work 
 Contactless and mobile wallet payments — Apple Pay, Google Pay — work at the same places physical cards do: hotels, riads, mid-range and upscale restaurants, modern shops and supermarkets. Where a terminal takes cards, it will usually take a tap. The limitation is identical to cards in general: the souk stall, the petit taxi and the small cafe remain cash-only, so mobile payment extends your card's reach but does not replace cash for daily life. 

 Ride-hail apps 
 Ride-hail apps are one of the genuinely useful digital tools in Morocco, because they sidestep the taxi meter argument entirely by fixing the fare in the app before you get in. Careem, Heetch and inDrive operate in some of the larger cities, though coverage is uneven and thinner outside the biggest centres. Where they work, they remove the negotiation and are especially valuable late at night, from the airport, or whenever your bearings are shaky. 
 Keep them as a fallback 
 Even if you mostly flag taxis on the street, having a ride-hail app installed is worth it as a fallback for the moments a street fare is least predictable. The fare is set, recorded and paid without a conversation, which turns a potentially stressful late-night pickup into a tap. Download and set one up before you travel rather than fumbling with registration when you actually need the ride. 

 WhatsApp runs everything 
 The single most important app in Morocco is WhatsApp, which locals and businesses use in place of phone calls and email: riads confirm arrivals on it, drivers send their location, and tour operators arrange everything through it. You will use it within a day of landing whether or not you planned to. Any data plan covers it, and having it set up means the practical business of the trip — confirmations, directions, bookings — flows smoothly. 

 Maps and staying oriented 
 Offline maps are essential rather than optional, because the medina lanes defeat GPS in places and rural legs lose signal entirely. Download offline map areas for your cities and any desert or mountain routes before you leave the last reliable signal, so you are never standing lost in a lane with a spinning icon. This is less a payment tool than a sanity tool, and it is the app habit travellers most often wish they had set up sooner. 

 Setting up before you fly 
 The apps that help are best installed and set up at home, on your own connection, rather than fumbled with in an arrivals hall: a ride-hail app registered, WhatsApp ready, offline maps downloaded, and your mobile wallet confirmed to work abroad. Ten minutes of preparation before you leave turns your phone into a genuinely useful travel tool the moment you land. Registration screens and verification codes are far easier to handle before you are tired and jet-lagged. 
 Data is the thing that powers it all 
 None of these apps helps without data, so an eSIM activated on arrival or a local SIM is the foundation the rest sits on. With data running, the maps, the messaging and the ride-hail all work; without it, you are back to cash and paper. Sorting connectivity first, in the arrivals hall or before, is what makes every other digital convenience on this page actually available when you need it. 

 What still needs cash 
 For all the digital convenience, the daily texture of Morocco remains cash: souk stalls, petit taxis, small cafes, tips, parking attendants, rural and desert stops. No app changes that, and a traveller who leans entirely on the phone will be caught out constantly by small, cash-only moments. The right setup is both — apps for rides, messaging, maps and the larger card-friendly payments, and a float of small dirham notes for everything the phone cannot touch.
