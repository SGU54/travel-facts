----- morocco-leftover-dirhams -----
TITLE: Leftover Dirhams: What to Do Before You Fly Home

 The closed currency that stopped you buying dirham before your trip creates a mirror problem at the end: leftover dirham is difficult to change back and mostly cannot leave the country, so the goal in your final days is to land near zero rather than carry a wad of notes you cannot use. A little planning in the last forty-eight hours turns this from a small loss into a non-issue. 

 

 Why dirham is hard to offload 
 Because the dirham is a closed currency, it is not freely traded outside Morocco, so exchange desks at home either refuse it or offer a poor rate, and the coins are worthless abroad entirely. What you carry out is effectively stranded value. This is the practical reason the advice throughout the trip is to withdraw in sensible amounts rather than stockpiling cash you might not spend. 

 The export limit 
 There is also a legal ceiling on taking dirham out of the country: no more than  2,000 MAD , and exceeding it is a customs violation rather than a mere formality. In practice you should be well under that simply by spending down, but it is worth knowing the rule exists, especially if a large final withdrawal left you holding more than you expected. 

 Time your last withdrawal 
 The cleanest approach is to stop withdrawing dirham a couple of days before departure and let card-friendly places absorb the rest of your spending. Estimate what you genuinely need for the final days — meals, taxis, tips, the airport — and take out only that. Running slightly short and topping up with a card beats ending the trip with notes you have to give away or abandon. 
 What still needs cash at the end 
 Keep enough small notes for the things that stay cash-only right to the last minute: the taxi or transfer to the airport, final tips for riad staff and porters, water, and any small purchases in the departures area. Everything else — a last dinner, a hotel bill — can usually go on a card. Holding back a modest, deliberate cash float for these avoids both a shortfall and a surplus. 

 Ways to spend it down painlessly 
 If you find yourself with more dirham than the last day can absorb, the good options are all pleasant: a final meal out, gifts and souvenirs from a fixed-price shop, a generous tip for a guide or driver who earned it, or topping up someone's service you were glad of. These turn leftover cash into something worthwhile rather than a rounding error handed to an airport exchange desk at a bad rate. 

 The end-of-trip cash plan 
 A simple plan makes the last days effortless: three or four days out, take a rough tally of what remains and what the final days require, then stop feeding the wallet from the ATM. Steer big remaining expenses — a hotel bill, a nice dinner — onto a card, and let the cash drain toward the essentials. The aim is to walk into the airport with pocket change, not a brick of notes. 
 Coins are the first to go 
 Coins are worth nothing outside Morocco and cannot even be exchanged at home, so spend them first and deliberately: water, tips, a coffee, small souvenirs. Notes are at least changeable, however poorly, but coins are pure stranded value once you leave. Emptying the coin pocket before the notes is a small habit that saves the most obviously wasted money. 

 If you do end up with extra 
 Should you leave with dirham despite your best planning, spend it before you clear security — airport shops and cafes inside Morocco still take it — or accept that changing a small amount back home will cost you. The airport exchange counter is the last resort it always was: convenient and poorly priced. Better to have handed the difference to a person who served you well than to a currency desk. 

 

 Leftover dirham is the closing bracket of the same fact that opened the trip: a currency you cannot buy in advance is also one you cannot easily carry away, so the whole journey is best paid in cash you draw down to nothing. How that arc runs from the arrival ATM to the last night is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the currency rules in full, see our  dirham guide ; for how much to hold day to day,  how much cash to carry  covers the rest.

----- shopping-in-morocco -----
TITLE: Shopping in Morocco: What to Buy and What Things Cost

 Shopping is one of the real pleasures of a Morocco trip, and doing it well means knowing what each craft is worth before the opening price tries to tell you. From rugs to spices, the souk runs on negotiation, so the useful knowledge is less a fixed price list — there isn't one — than a sense of what you are buying, where the number starts, and how to get it home. Here is the practical rundown. 

 

 The things worth buying 
 Morocco's standout buys are rugs and kilims, leather goods, metal and bone lamps, ceramics and tagines, argan oil, spices, and babouche slippers, each a genuine craft rather than a tourist trinket. The pleasure is in choosing something made well and carrying home an object with a story. Quality varies enormously within every category, so what you pay should track what you are actually getting, not the drama of the sale. 
 Rugs and leather: the big-ticket items 
 Rugs are the classic major purchase and the one where quality and price swing widest — hand-knotted wool with natural dyes sits far above machine-made blends, and the opening price is always a large multiple of the real one. Leather from the Fez tanneries is the other headline buy. For both, treat the first number as theatre, bargain calmly, and only start on something you would genuinely buy. 

 Argan oil, spices and edibles 
 Argan oil is a Moroccan speciality, sold in cosmetic and culinary grades, and a women's cooperative is the place to buy it both for quality and for where the money goes. Spices are gorgeous and cheap, but buy whole where you can and be wary of pre-mixed "ras el hanout" of unknown freshness. These smaller buys rarely need hard bargaining, and the gap between the tourist and fair price is usually small enough not to fight over. 

 Where the price is fixed 
 Not all Moroccan shopping is a negotiation: government-run artisan cooperatives such as the Ensemble Artisanal sell quality crafts at fixed, fair prices, and they are invaluable for benchmarking what things should cost before you brave the souk. Modern shops and supermarkets are fixed-price too. Starting at a cooperative to calibrate, then negotiating in the souk with that number in mind, is the shopper's winning sequence. 

 Bargaining, briefly 
 The souk expects negotiation, and the shape is familiar: the vendor opens high, you counter well below, and you meet in a middle you are both content with, kept warm and unhurried throughout. Only begin on something you mean to buy, and let the browsing you did elsewhere set your target. The  souk price sheet  explains the opening-price economy in depth. 

 Where the money goes 
 Some purchases do more good than others, and it is worth knowing which. Buying argan oil from a women's cooperative, crafts from a government artisan cooperative, or directly from the maker keeps more of your money with the people who made the thing. It also tends to mean fixed, fair prices and reliable quality. When the choice exists between a middleman's stall and the source, the source is usually both the better buy and the better outcome. 
 Spotting real craft from tourist filler 
 Every souk mixes genuine handwork with cheap imported filler sold at craft prices, and telling them apart takes a little attention: look for irregularities that signal handmade, ask what something is made of, and be wary of identical "handmade" items stacked in bulk. A cooperative visit early in the trip trains your eye for the rest of it. Paying a fair price for real craft is the goal; paying a craft price for a factory import is the thing to avoid. 

 Getting it home 
 Think about transport before you buy big: a rug you can roll and carry beats one you must ship, since shipping is where a purchase can go wrong after you have left the country. If you do ship, get the terms in writing and keep your receipt. For spices and liquids like argan oil, mind your airline's rules and pack them well. Buying what fits in your luggage is the simplest way to avoid the one part of Moroccan shopping that reliably causes regret. 

 

 Every purchase here sits on the same opening-price logic, and knowing an item's real range before the number is spoken is the whole defence. How shopping, taxis and guides share that one skill is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the bargaining itself, see  how to haggle in the souks ; for a carpet shop specifically,  what actually happens inside  walks through it.

    What's worth buying in Morocco?   Rugs and kilims, leather, lamps, ceramics and tagines, argan oil, spices, and babouche slippers — all genuine crafts.   
    Where do I buy argan oil?   From a women's cooperative, for both quality and where the money goes. It comes in cosmetic and culinary grades.   
    Is there anywhere with fixed prices?   Yes — government artisan cooperatives like the Ensemble Artisanal, plus modern shops and supermarkets. Use them to benchmark before the souk.

    How much should I bargain on small items?   Lightly. On spices and small crafts the tourist-to-fair gap is small; save real bargaining energy for rugs and leather.   
 


----- paying-by-card-in-morocco -----
TITLE: Where Cards Actually Work in Morocco

 Card acceptance in Morocco has grown, but it remains narrower than most Americans expect, and knowing the line between where plastic works and where only cash will do prevents the small, recurring awkwardness of reaching for a card that a stall cannot take. The pattern is consistent enough to plan around, and it mostly tracks the size and formality of the place you are paying. 

 

 Where cards work reliably 
 Cards are accepted dependably at hotels and riads, upscale and mid-range restaurants, modern shops and supermarkets, car rental desks, and established tour operators. In these places you can plan to pay by card, and Visa and Mastercard are both widely handled. This covers a real share of a trip's larger expenses, which is why you rarely need to carry huge amounts of cash at once — the big items take plastic. 

 Where it's cash only 
 The cash-only world is equally consistent: souk stalls, taxis, small local cafes and street food, medina riads that prefer cash, rural and desert areas, small-town everything, tips, and parking attendants. None of this is expensive individually, which is exactly why running out of small notes is the common friction rather than any large bill. Assume cash for anything small, informal, or off the main tourist track. 
 The rule of thumb 
 The reliable heuristic is that formality and size predict card acceptance: the larger and more modern the establishment, the more likely the card works; the smaller and more traditional, the more certainly it is cash. When in doubt, ask before you order or commit, and keep enough cash to cover the whole transaction either way. This one habit removes almost all card-related surprises. 

 Fees and the conversion trap 
 Two costs ride on card payments abroad. Your own bank may charge a foreign-transaction fee, which a good travel card avoids entirely, so it is worth knowing your card's terms before you go. The second is the "pay in dollars?" prompt at the terminal — dynamic currency conversion — which you should always decline in favour of dirham, exactly as at an ATM. Choosing dollars hands the rate to the merchant's processor at a markup. 

 Contactless, chip and the small realities 
 Where cards are accepted, chip-and-PIN is standard and contactless is increasingly common in modern shops and city restaurants, so your usual tap or insert works as expected. Magnetic-stripe-only cards are worth avoiding, and a card with no foreign-transaction fee saves a small percentage on every purchase across the trip. Notify your bank of the travel dates so a first Moroccan charge is not read as fraud and frozen. 
 When the terminal comes to you 
 In many restaurants the terminal is brought to your table rather than taken away with your card, which is good practice you can gently insist on, and it is where the "pay in dollars?" prompt appears in person. Watch for it, choose dirham, and keep your card in sight throughout. These are the same habits you would use anywhere; Morocco simply gives the currency prompt more often. 

 Practical card habits 
 Carry two cards from different banks in separate places, set a travel notice so a first foreign charge is not blocked, and keep a card back as the reserve if your primary is lost or frozen. Cards are best treated as the way you pay the large, formal expenses while cash handles the daily texture of the trip. Between a working card for hotels and tours and a float of small notes for everything else, you are covered in every situation Morocco presents.

TITLE: Renting a Car in Morocco: When It's Worth It

 Renting a car in Morocco buys real freedom for some trips and real stress for others, and the decision comes down to where you are going more than to your confidence behind the wheel. Cities are chaotic and parking is hard, so many first-timers hire a driver or take the train instead; a car earns its keep mainly on flexible routes through the countryside. Here is where each answer applies. 

 

 When a car makes sense 
 A rental car is genuinely worth it for flexible, scenic routes where public transport is thin: the Atlas Mountains, valleys and gorges, coastal drives, and reaching villages a coach never serves. If your trip is built around wandering the countryside at your own pace and stopping where you like, self-driving unlocks that in a way trains and tours cannot. This is the case the car was made for. 

 When to skip it 
 For a classic city-focused loop, a car is more burden than benefit. Morocco drives on the right, US licences are accepted for short stays with an International Driving Permit recommended, but the cities are chaotic, parking is scarce and stressful, and you would leave the car sitting unused while you walk the medinas. On the rail spine — Marrakech, Casablanca, Fez — the train is faster and calmer, and the long desert run is best left to a tour driver. 
 The driver alternative 
 Hiring a car with a driver is the middle path many first-timers choose, and it often costs less than it sounds while removing every downside of self-driving. You get door-to-door flexibility without navigating chaotic traffic, finding parking, or handling checkpoints, and a good driver doubles as an informal guide. For the desert and multi-stop country routes, this is frequently the sweet spot. 

 Driving realities to know 
 If you do drive, a few realities shape the experience: toll motorways connect the major cities and are smooth and fast, but secondary roads vary and mountain routes are slow and winding. Police checkpoints are common and routine — have your documents ready, be polite, and they pass without issue. Livestock, pedestrians and unlit vehicles make night driving genuinely risky, so plan to arrive before dark. 
 Parking, fuel and the extras 
 In towns, use guarded parking and tip the attendant a few dirham rather than leaving a car on the street. Fuel stations are plentiful on main routes and sparse in remote areas, so fill up before long empty stretches. When renting, check the insurance excess, inspect the car for existing damage before you drive off, and photograph it — the same sensible habits you would use anywhere, slightly more important here. 

 What renting actually costs 
 Beyond the daily rate, a Moroccan rental carries the usual extras worth budgeting for: insurance and the excess you are liable for, fuel, tolls on the motorways, and guarded parking in towns. The headline price is rarely the real one, so read what the insurance covers and what excess you would owe on damage before you sign. A cheap rate with a huge excess and thin cover is not the bargain it appears. 
 The one-way and cross-border catches 
 Dropping a car in a different city than you collected it usually carries a one-way fee, and taking a Moroccan rental across a border is generally not allowed at all — points to confirm before you plan a route around them. If your itinerary is genuinely one-way, price the fee into the comparison against train-plus-driver, which sometimes wins outright once the extras are counted. 

 The honest recommendation 
 For most first trips, the answer is not to self-drive: take the train between the imperial cities, use a tour or driver for the desert, and consider a rental only if a specific leg — the Atlas, the coast — genuinely calls for one. Save the car for the part of the trip where freedom of movement is the whole point, and let easier modes carry the rest.

TITLE: Getting to Chefchaouen: Every Route from Fez and Tangier

 Chefchaouen catches first-timers out because the blue city sits in the Rif mountains with no train line, so the smooth ONCF rail that carries you between the other cities simply stops short of it. Reaching it means a coach or a shared taxi, and knowing the routes in advance keeps this leg from becoming the awkward gap in an otherwise easy itinerary. Here is every way in and how it fits a trip. 

 

 Why there's no train 
 Chefchaouen is not on Morocco's rail network, and no line reaches the Rif mountains where it sits, so any plan that assumes a train there will fail. This is the single fact to absorb before routing a trip through the blue city: you arrive by road, from Fez or Tangier, by coach or grand taxi. Building the leg around that reality rather than discovering it late is the whole trick. 

 From Fez: the common route 
 The most common approach is the coach from Fez, a scenic mountain journey of roughly four hours run by reliable operators like CTM and Supratours. It is comfortable, inexpensive and the standard way first-timers arrive, pairing naturally with a Fez stay earlier in the loop. Book a day or two ahead in high season, since seats on the popular departures fill and the schedule is not frequent. 
 Booking the coach 
 CTM and Supratours sell tickets online and at their stations, and reserving ahead secures a seat on the timing you want rather than leaving you to the next departure hours later. Arrive with a little time in hand, keep the ticket for the journey, and treat the mountain scenery as part of the experience rather than dead transit. It is one of the prettier legs of a Moroccan trip. 

 From Tangier 
 Chefchaouen is also reachable from Tangier in a couple of hours by coach or grand taxi, which suits trips that start in the north or combine the blue city with the Tangier region. This routing works well if you are arriving by ferry from Spain or flying into the north, letting you take in Chefchaouen before heading south to Fez and beyond. The road is shorter than from Fez but the principle is the same: no rail, so coach or taxi. 

 The grand taxi option 
 Shared grand taxis run these routes too, leaving when full and priced per seat, and they are faster and more flexible than the coach if you do not mind the shared-sedan format. You can also charter a whole taxi for a private, immediate departure at roughly six times the seat fare — a reasonable choice for a group or a family wanting to leave on their own schedule. Agree the per-seat or whole-car price before you set off. 

 How long the journey really takes 
 The Fez route runs about four hours by coach and the Tangier route around two, and treating those as half-day commitments each way is the honest way to plan. Adding the wait for a scheduled departure and the transfer to your riad, a travel day to or from Chefchaouen is a travel day, not a morning. Planning it as such — arriving with the afternoon free rather than the whole day gone to logistics — keeps the leg from feeling like a slog. 
 Onward from the blue city 
 Leaving Chefchaouen follows the same rules in reverse: coach or grand taxi back to Fez or on to Tangier, with the same advice to book ahead in high season. If the blue city is your northern turnaround, plan the onward leg before you arrive so a relaxing stay does not end in a scramble for the last seat out. The mountains reward planning the exit as carefully as the entrance. 

 How it fits a loop 
 Chefchaouen slots most naturally between Fez and the north, added as a two-night detour rather than squeezed into a day, since the mountain journey each way eats real time. A common shape is Marrakech and the desert first, then Fez, then the coach up to Chefchaouen, then onward or back. Treating it as a restful pause in the mountains, not a rushed tick-box, is how the blue city rewards the effort of reaching it.

TITLE: Sahara Desert Camps: Standard, Deluxe, and What Changes

 The desert camp is where the price of a Sahara trip really splits, far more than the choice of dune field does, and knowing what separates a standard night from a luxury one lets you spend where it matters to you. The camel ride, the dunes and the stars are shared by every camp; what changes is the comfort of the night in between. Here is what each tier actually gives you. 

 

 What a standard camp includes 
 A standard desert camp is a genuine tented night: a bed in a shared or simple private tent, communal bathroom facilities, a group tagine dinner, and breakfast before the dawn ride out. It is comfortable enough, authentic, and the price most first-timers pay, delivering the whole point of the experience — a night in the dunes — without frills. For many travellers it is exactly right, and the simplicity is part of the appeal. 

 What luxury adds 
 A luxury or deluxe camp adds private, en-suite bathrooms, better bedding and larger tents, a quieter and more secluded setting, and often a more elaborate dinner and service. You are paying for comfort and privacy rather than a different view — the dunes and stars are the same. For a honeymoon, a family wanting private facilities, or anyone who sleeps poorly rough, the upgrade is genuinely worth it; for a hardy traveller, it is optional. 
 The bathroom is the real difference 
 If one thing decides the tier for most people, it is the bathroom: standard camps share facilities, luxury camps put a private en-suite in your tent. In the desert, that convenience carries more weight than it would in a hotel, and it is the single upgrade travellers most often say they were glad to have paid for. Everything else — bedding, tent size, dinner — is a matter of degree. 

 What stays the same either way 
 Every camp, standard or luxury, shares the elements that make the night unforgettable: the camel trek in over the dunes toward sunset, dinner under a vast sky, extraordinary stargazing far from any light, and a sunrise before the drive back. The core experience is remarkably consistent across price points, which is why the standard camp is never a lesser version of the trip — only a simpler bed beneath the same stars. 

 What to bring for the night 
 Whatever the tier, a desert night calls for a few specific things: a warm layer for the genuinely cold hours after dark, something long-sleeved to sleep in, a torch or headlamp, and a charged power bank since camps often have no electricity. Standard camps in particular may have limited lighting and no sockets, so arriving prepared makes the simpler night entirely comfortable rather than a test of endurance. 
 Managing expectations at a standard camp 
 A standard camp is basic by design, and travellers who expect a hotel in the sand are the ones disappointed, while those who expect a real desert night are delighted by the same camp. Shared facilities, simple food and a canvas tent are the authentic version of the experience, not a shortfall. Knowing that going in is what turns the standard camp from a compromise into exactly the night you came for. 

 Choosing your tier 
 Match the camp to who you are travelling as: standard suits budget-minded and adventurous travellers happy to rough it a little, while luxury suits families, couples on a special trip, and anyone who values a private bathroom and a quieter night. Decide this deliberately when you book the tour, since it moves the price as much as the destination does, and it is easy to upgrade at the booking stage and impossible once you are in the dunes.
