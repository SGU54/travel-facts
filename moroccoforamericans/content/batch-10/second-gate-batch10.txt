----- casablanca-airport-arrival -----
TITLE: Arriving at Casablanca Airport: Your First Two Hours

 Casablanca's Mohammed V airport is where most flights from the US touch down, and for the classic first-timer loop it is usually a gateway rather than a destination — the goal is to move smoothly through it and on to Marrakech or Fez. The single fact that makes that easy is the train station directly beneath the terminal. Here is how the first two hours run, in order. 

 

 Through immigration 
 US citizens need no advance visa, so passport control is a stamp rather than an application: have your passport valid for the required period and know the address of your first night's accommodation, which the form may ask for. Lines vary with how many flights have landed together, so build a little patience into the plan. Check that your entry stamp is clear before you walk away, since a missing stamp becomes a question on the way out. 

 Cash before anything else 
 Because you cannot buy dirham before arriving, the airport ATM is the first real stop after immigration, and there are bank machines in the arrivals area. Withdraw enough for the first day or two, decline the "pay in dollars" conversion prompt in favour of dirham, and skip the exchange counter unless your card fails. With cash in hand and your phone connected, the rest of the arrival is simple logistics. 
 Connect and confirm 
 If you have not already, this is the moment to switch on your eSIM or buy a local SIM, then message your onward accommodation so they know you have landed. A connected phone with a map and your booking details turns the next steps from guesswork into a short checklist. It is the small piece of preparation that removes most first-hour friction. 

 The train under the terminal 
 The key to Casablanca airport is the ONCF train station directly beneath it, from which trains run to Casa Voyageurs — the main station — and onward across the network. This is why Casablanca works as an entry point even when the city is not your destination: you can land and be on a train toward Marrakech or Fez without setting foot in Casablanca proper. Check the departure board and buy your ticket at the counter or machine. 
 Onward to Marrakech or Fez 
 For Marrakech, you typically take the airport train to Casa Voyageurs and change to a Marrakech service; for Fez, a similar connection heads north and east. First class buys a reserved seat for a small premium, worth it with luggage after a long flight. The whole move — land, cash, train — can be done in well under two hours when flights and schedules cooperate. 

 If you're staying in Casablanca first 
 Some travellers spend a first night in Casablanca to break the journey or see the Hassan II Mosque, and that is a reasonable soft landing after a transatlantic flight. A petit taxi or the train reaches the city centre, and a night's rest before the onward leg suits anyone badly jet-lagged. For the classic loop, though, most push straight on while the momentum of arrival carries them. 

 Timing your connection 
 How much buffer to leave depends on your energy and your onward booking: trains from the airport station run regularly, so a missed connection rarely costs more than the wait for the next, but after a transatlantic flight a little slack is kindness to yourself. If you have booked a specific onward train, allow time for immigration lines and the walk down to the platform. If you have not, buying on arrival is straightforward outside peak crush. 
 Late-night landings 
 Flights from the US often land in the evening, and a late arrival changes the calculus: onward trains thin out at night, so an airport-area hotel or a first night in Casablanca can beat arriving in Marrakech in the small hours. Weigh the momentum of pushing on against the comfort of a bed nearby. Neither is wrong; it depends on the hour and how you travel tired. 

 The calm-arrival checklist 
 The whole first two hours reduce to a short sequence: clear immigration, withdraw dirham and decline the dollar conversion, connect your phone and message your accommodation, then take the train beneath the terminal onward. Done in that order, nothing catches you out, and you reach your real first destination with cash, a working phone, and a seat booked rather than a scramble at the rank. 

 

 The arrival hour is where the trip's habits are set: cash before you commit to any fare, the conversion prompt declined, the onward leg planned. How arrival, money and the first move fit together is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For getting dirham on landing, see our  money-on-arrival guide ; for the rail network onward, the  trains guide  covers routes and classes.

----- atlas-mountains-guide -----
TITLE: The Atlas Mountains: Day Trips and Longer Stays

 The High Atlas rises barely an hour from Marrakech, which makes it the easiest escape from the city's heat and bustle and one of the most rewarding parts of a Morocco trip. It works as a half-day outing, a full day, or a multi-day base, depending on how much time you can give it, and the choice shapes what you see — from a valley lunch to a Toubkal summit. Here is what each version offers. 

 

 What fits a day trip 
 A day trip from Marrakech comfortably reaches the Ourika Valley, the village of Imlil at the foot of Toubkal, or the Berber villages and waterfalls of the foothills, all within easy driving distance. These outings trade the desert's drama for green valleys, running water and mountain air, and they are the perfect antidote to a few hot days in the medina. A driver or organised tour handles the winding roads and leaves you free to walk and look. 
 Ourika and Imlil 
 The Ourika Valley is the classic easy day trip — a river, waterfalls, and roadside cafes an hour from the city — while Imlil is the gateway village for Toubkal and a lovely destination in its own right for a valley walk and lunch. Either gives you a genuine taste of the mountains without an overnight, and both pair naturally with a Marrakech-based trip that wants one day of altitude and green. 

 What needs longer 
 Serious walking and the real high country need more than a day: trekking Mount Toubkal, North Africa's highest peak, is typically a two-day guided climb from Imlil, and multi-day village-to-village treks reward those who want to go deeper. Staying a night or two in a mountain guesthouse or a valley kasbah turns the Atlas from a glimpse into an experience, and the pace up here is the point rather than a limitation. 

 How to reach it 
 The mountains are reached by road from Marrakech, and most first-timers go with a driver or on an organised tour rather than self-driving the steep, winding routes. A hired car with a driver is flexible and affordable for a day, while trekking trips include transfers to the trailhead. The roads are scenic but demanding, which is exactly why handing the wheel to someone who knows them is the common and sensible choice. 

 What to wear and bring 
 The mountains run cooler than Marrakech year-round and cold at altitude, so a warm layer belongs in the daypack even on a summer day trip, along with sturdy shoes for uneven ground, sun protection, and water. Weather shifts quickly at height, and a valley that is warm at midday chills fast in shade or late afternoon. Packing for a range of conditions turns a day trip from uncomfortable into easy. 
 Combining the Atlas with the desert 
 Many Sahara tours from Marrakech cross the High Atlas on the way south, over the dramatic Tizi n'Tichka pass, so a desert trip already gives you a taste of the mountains in transit. If the Atlas is a headline reason you came, though, a dedicated day or overnight rewards it far better than the drive-through glimpse. Deciding which you want shapes how you build the southern part of the trip. 

 When to go 
 Timing matters more in the mountains than in the cities: spring and autumn are ideal, summer offers cool relief from the lowland heat, and winter brings snow to the high passes and peaks, with Toubkal becoming a technical winter climb. A valley day trip works most of the year; a serious trek should be matched to the season and, for the high routes in winter, to proper mountain conditions. 

 

 Fitting the Atlas into a trip — a day from Marrakech, or a longer base — is a sequencing choice that trades against the desert and the cities for your limited days. How those pieces balance is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the base you'd day-trip from, see our  Marrakech guide ; for reaching the mountains, the  car and driver guide  covers the drive. 

 
   Atlas Mountains: FAQ 
    Can I visit the Atlas on a day trip?   Yes — the Ourika Valley, Imlil, and foothill villages are all within an hour or so of Marrakech, ideal for a day of green and mountain air.   
    How do I climb Toubkal?   Typically a two-day guided climb from Imlil. It's North Africa's highest peak and a winter climb becomes technical.   
    Should I self-drive?   Most don't. The roads are steep and winding, so a driver or organised tour is the common, sensible choice.   
    When's the best time?   Spring and autumn are ideal, summer offers cool relief, and winter brings snow to the peaks and passes.   
    Is a night in the mountains worth it?   Yes, if you can spare it — a valley kasbah or guesthouse turns a glimpse into a real experience.   
 


----- essaouira-guide -----
TITLE: Essaouira: The Coast Escape from Marrakech

 Essaouira is the coast escape that first-timers fall for: a fortified Atlantic port about three hours from Marrakech, windswept and walkable, with a calm, hassle-free medina that feels a world away from the big cities. It makes an easy day trip and an even better overnight, offering fresh seafood, sea ramparts and a slower pace. Here is what to do there and how to reach it. 

 

 Why Essaouira feels different 
 Essaouira's appeal is its calm: the medina is compact, walkable and notably relaxed, with far less of the hard-sell pressure that defines Marrakech or Fez, and the constant Atlantic wind keeps it cool when the interior bakes. The whitewashed, blue-accented town within its ramparts is a UNESCO-listed gem that rewards aimless wandering. For travellers worn down by the intensity of the imperial cities, it is a genuine exhale. 

 What to do 
 Essaouira is small enough to see in a relaxed day or two, and its flat, walkable layout makes it gentle after the vertical mazes of the older cities. The pleasures here are simple and coastal: walk the Skala de la Ville ramparts above the crashing sea, watch the blue fishing boats unload at the working harbour, eat grilled fish straight off the day's catch, and browse the relaxed souks known for thuya woodwork and art. The wide beach draws windsurfers and kitesurfers thanks to the reliable wind, and a stroll along it is the classic end to an Essaouira afternoon. 
 Eating the catch 
 Essaouira's signature meal is fresh fish grilled simply at the harbour or in the medina, chosen from the day's catch and cooked without fuss. Agree the price by weight before it hits the grill, as you would in any market, and enjoy one of the freshest, most straightforward meals in the country. The seafood is the reason many travellers extend a day trip into a night. 

 A little history in the walls 
 Part of Essaouira's calm comes from its history as a fortified 18th-century trading port, its ramparts and street grid laid out by a European engineer, which gives the medina an orderly, airy feel unusual in Morocco. That heritage is why the town is UNESCO-listed and why wandering it feels less like a maze than Fez or Marrakech. You can read the past in the sea walls and the old merchants' quarters as you walk. 

 Day trip or overnight 
 Essaouira works as a long day trip from Marrakech, but an overnight is better: it lets you feel the town empty of day-trippers in the evening, catch the light on the ramparts, and eat a relaxed harbour dinner. One or two nights is plenty to absorb the place without running out of things to do. As a restful bookend to a busy loop, it earns its nights. 

 Who Essaouira suits 
 The town rewards travellers after calm, coast and character rather than a checklist of monuments — its pleasures are atmosphere, seafood and sea air. Anyone worn down by the intensity of Marrakech or Fez tends to love it, while a visitor craving big-hitter sights may find it quiet. Knowing which you are keeps Essaouira from being a mismatch and, for the right traveller, makes it the highlight of the trip. 

 How to get there 
 Essaouira is reached from Marrakech by road in about three hours, most easily by CTM or Supratours coach, by shared grand taxi, or with a private driver — there is no train to the coast here. The coach is comfortable and inexpensive and runs several times a day, making the town one of the simplest excursions from Marrakech. Book ahead in high season, when the popular departures fill.

TITLE: Riads in Marrakech: How to Choose One (Not Which One)

 Choosing a riad in Marrakech is less about finding the single best one than about knowing how to read a listing, because the right riad depends on where in the medina it sits, how easily you reach it, and details a photo will not show. Get the method right and dozens of riads become good choices; get it wrong and a beautiful courtyard turns into a nightly ordeal of stairs and dark lanes. Here is how to choose. 

 

 What a riad actually is 
 A riad is a traditional courtyard house within the medina walls, built inward around a central patio, and staying in one is the classic Marrakech experience — intimate, atmospheric, and a world apart from a modern hotel. The trade is that riads sit inside the pedestrian medina, so cars cannot usually reach the door and rooms in historic buildings vary widely. Understanding that shape is the start of choosing well. 

 Location within the medina 
 The most important variable is where in the medina the riad sits, because "in the medina" ranges from a short, clear walk off a main artery to a deep warren of turns that empties after dark. A riad close to a car-accessible point and a recognisable landmark is far easier to live in than one buried six turns deep, however lovely its photos. Ask how far it is from the nearest taxi access and from Jemaa el-Fnaa on foot. 
 Ask about the arrival 
 Good riads meet guests at the nearest vehicle point and walk them in, which matters enormously on a first night with luggage in unfamiliar lanes. Confirm this before booking, and arrange the meeting time and place in writing. A riad that leaves you to find it yourself in the dark is telling you something about the stay to come; one that meets you is showing its hospitality from the first minute. 

 Reading the details that matter 
 Beyond location, a few details separate a great riad stay from a frustrating one: whether rooms face the courtyard (quieter) or the street (louder, early starts), whether there is a rooftop terrace, how many floors of stairs stand between the entrance and your room, and whether breakfast and airport transfers are included. Historic buildings rarely have lifts, so ask about stairs if that matters to you. These are the things photos flatter and reviews reveal. 

 Medina riad or easier comfort 
 The honest trade is immersion against convenience: a medina riad puts you in the heart of the experience with the friction of lanes and stairs, while a hotel in Gueliz or a riad near a medina gate offers easier access and a quieter night. For a first visit of a few nights, most travellers find the medina riad worth every inconvenience; families, late arrivals, or anyone valuing calm may prefer to split the stay. The neighborhood you pick sets the tone before the riad does. 

 What you're really paying for 
 Riad prices span an enormous range, and the money mostly buys location, size and service rather than a fundamentally different experience of the courtyard house. A simple, well-run riad in a good spot can beat a lavish one buried deep in the medina, because access and hospitality matter more day to day than thread count. Decide what you actually value — atmosphere, ease, a pool, a rooftop — and pay for that rather than for photos. 
 Families and longer stays 
 For families or stays of several nights, the calculus shifts toward practicality: a riad near a medina gate or a hotel in a quieter district can spare you the daily haul of luggage and tired children through the lanes. Some travellers book a medina riad for the first couple of nights and something easier afterward, getting the immersion while it is novel and the comfort when they are worn. Matching the base to the trip's shape is the real skill. 

 Booking with confidence 
 Read recent reviews for mentions of the walk-in, the stairs and the noise rather than just the decor, message the riad with your arrival details and any needs, and book directly or through a reputable platform. A short exchange before booking — asking about access, transfers and rooms — tells you as much about the hospitality as any star rating. The riad that answers warmly and clearly is usually the one that looks after you on the ground.

TITLE: Etiquette and Customs in Morocco

 A little cultural awareness goes a long way in Morocco, and none of it is complicated or anxiety-inducing — a few habits of dress, greeting and courtesy cover almost every situation and are warmly received. Morocco is a welcoming, Muslim country used to visitors, so the aim is respect rather than performance. Here are the customs worth knowing before you go, kept practical. 

 

 Dress 
 Dressing modestly — shoulders and knees covered, looser rather than tight — is the single most useful habit, showing respect and reducing unwanted attention at once. This applies more in rural areas and religious sites than on a city rooftop, and you will see a range on other travellers, but erring modest is always the safe, considerate choice. A light scarf is invaluable for covering up near mosques or in conservative areas. 

 Greetings and the right hand 
 A warm greeting matters here: "As-salaam alaikum" (peace be upon you) is met with "wa alaikum salaam", and a handshake is common, though some conservative Moroccans of the opposite sex may prefer a hand on the heart instead — follow their lead. Use your right hand for eating, giving and receiving, since the left is traditionally considered unclean. These small gestures signal respect and are noticed and appreciated. 

 Photography 
 Always ask before photographing people, especially women and in rural areas, and accept a refusal graciously — a camera pointed without consent causes genuine offence and sometimes a demand for payment. Some performers and stallholders, particularly around Jemaa el-Fnaa, will expect a tip for a photo, so agree that first or don't shoot. Landscapes, architecture and street scenes without identifiable faces are unproblematic. 

 Mosques and religious sites 
 Most mosques in Morocco are closed to non-Muslims, which surprises some visitors — the grand Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca is a notable exception offering guided tours. Dress conservatively around religious sites, remove shoes where required, and keep noise and photography respectful. Madrasas (former Islamic schools) are often open to visitors and are among the most beautiful buildings you can enter, so they scratch the architectural itch mosques leave. 

 Hospitality, tea and eating 
 Moroccan hospitality is generous and sincere: mint tea is a ritual of welcome, and accepting it graciously is part of many interactions, from a shop to a home. If invited to eat, the meal may be shared from a communal dish, eaten with the right hand or with bread, and a small gift for a host is appreciated. Accepting hospitality warmly, even briefly, is one of the pleasures of travelling here — just stay aware in a shop that tea can also open a sale. 

 Bargaining as etiquette 
 Haggling in the souk is itself a custom, and the etiquette is as much about manner as money: keep it warm, unhurried and good-humoured, treat it as a social exchange rather than a contest, and only open a negotiation on something you would genuinely buy. Walking away is a normal part of the dance, not a rudeness, but naming a price and then abandoning it is considered bad faith. Approached lightly, it is one of the pleasures of the market. 
 Tipping and small courtesies 
 Small tips for services are woven through daily life here and are part of ordinary courtesy — a few dirham for a porter, a guide, a parking attendant. Carrying small notes to give promptly is itself a form of good manners, sparing the awkward fumble for change. Combined with a warm greeting and a little patience, these gestures smooth nearly every interaction a visitor has. 

 Ramadan and everyday courtesy 
 During Ramadan, avoid eating, drinking or smoking conspicuously in public during daylight out of respect for those fasting, though you are not expected to fast yourself. Year-round, public displays of affection are best kept minimal, patience and good humour open doors, and a few words of Arabic or French are warmly received. None of this requires walking on eggshells — ordinary politeness, tuned slightly for the setting, is all it takes.

----- alcohol-in-morocco -----
TITLE: Alcohol in Morocco: Where You Can and Can't Drink

 Alcohol in Morocco is legal but not universal, and knowing where you can and cannot expect a drink saves both awkwardness and false assumptions. This is a Muslim country where most locals do not drink, yet licensed hotels, tourist restaurants and certain shops serve alcohol perfectly openly. The rule is less about legality than about setting and discretion. Here is where a drink is easy, where it is not, and how to handle it respectfully. 

 

 Where you can drink 
 Alcohol is readily available in licensed hotels, upscale and tourist-oriented restaurants, rooftop bars in the cities, and dedicated bars in tourist areas, where wine, beer and spirits are served without fuss. Morocco even produces its own decent wines and a local beer, which you will find on these menus. For a traveller who wants a glass of wine with dinner or a drink on a terrace, the cities make it straightforward. 

 Where not to expect it 
 Alcohol is simply absent from most everyday Moroccan life, and that is worth setting expectations around rather than discovering mid-trip. Do not expect it in traditional local restaurants, in the medina's ordinary cafes and eateries, or in more conservative and rural areas, where it is simply not served and asking can be awkward. Most everyday Moroccan dining is alcohol-free, and mint tea or fresh juice is the accompaniment to a meal. This is not a prohibition so much as a cultural default, and matching it where it applies is the courteous move. 

 Buying alcohol to take away 
 Beyond bars and restaurants, buying alcohol to drink privately is a common approach for travellers who want a quiet glass without going out. For drinking in your riad or hotel, some supermarkets — notably larger chains like Carrefour and Marjane — have a dedicated alcohol section, and there are specialist liquor shops in the cities. Availability tightens during Ramadan, when sales to Muslims are restricted and some shops limit hours. Buy discreetly, carry it wrapped, and drink it in private rather than in public spaces. 

 Moroccan wine and beer, briefly 
 Morocco has a small domestic wine industry centred on the regions around Meknes and Benslimane, producing reds, whites and rosés that appear on hotel and restaurant lists and are perfectly pleasant, and a couple of local lagers fill the beer role. Choosing a Moroccan wine with dinner is both cheaper than an import and a small way into the country's own production. Ask the waiter for the local option and you will usually be pointed to something decent. 

 Discretion is the whole etiquette 
 The single guiding principle is discretion: drinking is fine in the settings meant for it and inappropriate in public streets, near mosques, or conspicuously anywhere during Ramadan daylight. Do not carry an open drink through the medina or offer alcohol to locals who may not drink. Keeping it to bars, restaurants and your own accommodation covers every situation and shows the respect the setting asks for. 

 Drinking in your riad 
 Many travellers find the easiest approach is a drink in their riad or hotel rather than seeking out bars, buying a bottle discreetly from a supermarket alcohol section and enjoying it on the terrace. Check first that your riad permits it, since some traditional houses prefer guests not to, and keep it low-key regardless. This sidesteps the question of which restaurants serve and keeps everything comfortably private. 

 During Ramadan 
 Ramadan tightens everything: alcohol sales to Muslims are restricted, some bars and shops reduce service, and public drinking is especially inappropriate during the fasting month. Licensed tourist hotels generally continue to serve their guests discreetly, so a drink is still available if you want one, but the respectful approach is to keep it low-key and private. As with food, the courtesy is simply not to be conspicuous. 

 

 Where alcohol fits — the licensed hotel, the tourist restaurant, the private terrace, never the public street — is one more reading of setting and discretion that runs through a Moroccan trip. TITLE: Travel Insurance for Morocco: What It Needs to Cover

 Travel insurance for Morocco is worth having not because the country is dangerous — it is a safe, rewarding destination — but because the specific risks of a trip here are the kind insurance exists for: pay-first private hospitals, adventure activities in the desert and mountains, and the ordinary chance of a cancelled flight or lost bag. Knowing what a policy should cover lets you buy the right one rather than the cheapest. Here is what matters. 

 

 Why insurance matters here specifically 
 Morocco's public health system is limited for visitors, and the private hospitals you would actually use typically expect payment upfront or a guarantee before treatment, which can mean a large sum at exactly the wrong moment. Travel insurance that covers medical costs and, crucially, medical evacuation turns that from a crisis into a claim. This is the single strongest reason to carry a policy on a Morocco trip. 

 Medical and evacuation cover 
 The core of any Morocco policy is solid medical cover with a high enough limit and, importantly, emergency medical evacuation — because a serious problem in a remote area like the desert or the High Atlas may require transport to a major city or home. Check the medical limit is substantial and that evacuation is included rather than an add-on. For most travellers this is the part of the policy that justifies its entire cost. 

 Adventure activities 
 Standard policies often exclude the very things people come to Morocco to do, so if your trip includes camel trekking, quad biking, trekking Toubkal, surfing at Essaouira or hot-air ballooning, confirm those activities are covered. Adventure or activity add-ons exist precisely for this, and reading the exclusions before you buy is far better than discovering a gap after a fall in the dunes. Match the policy to your actual itinerary. 

 Cancellation, delays and belongings 
 Beyond health, a good policy covers trip cancellation and interruption, flight delays and missed connections, and lost, stolen or delayed baggage — the ordinary mishaps of any international trip. Given that petty theft is the realistic risk in Morocco rather than anything worse, baggage and personal-belongings cover is genuinely useful. Keep receipts and report any theft to the police to get the report a claim will need. 

 What a claim actually needs 
 Insurance only helps if you can substantiate a claim, so the practical habits matter: keep receipts for anything valuable, get a police report for theft, retain medical paperwork and bills, and photograph or note the details of any incident. Report problems promptly, and contact your insurer's emergency line before incurring major medical costs where possible, since some require prior authorisation. Good documentation is the difference between a smooth claim and a denied one. 
 Existing conditions and the fine print 
 Read the exclusions before you rely on the cover: pre-existing medical conditions often need declaring, high-value electronics may have per-item limits, and some policies cap or exclude specific activities. A few minutes with the policy wording when you buy prevents the worst kind of surprise, the one discovered at the moment of claiming. Match the declared details to your real trip, and the cover does what you bought it for. 

 Practical points 
 Buy the policy when you book the trip, not at the airport, so cancellation cover applies from the start, and carry the policy number and the insurer's emergency line somewhere accessible, including offline on your phone. Check whether it covers your whole itinerary and the full duration, and keep documentation of anything you might claim for. A policy you can actually reach and use in a remote area is worth more than a marginally cheaper one you cannot.
