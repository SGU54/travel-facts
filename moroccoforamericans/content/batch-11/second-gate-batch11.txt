----- things-to-do-fez -----
TITLE: What to Do in Fez: The Essential First-Timer's List

 Fez rewards a first-timer with something Marrakech cannot quite match: a living medieval city, largely unchanged in layout for a thousand years, where the sights are not monuments set apart but the working fabric of the place. The essentials all sit inside Fès el-Bali, the walled old city, and two well-planned days cover them. Here is what to prioritise and how to approach it without getting lost or hassled. 

 

 Start with Fès el-Bali itself 
 The single greatest sight in Fez is the medina as a whole: Fès el-Bali is the world's largest car-free urban area, a genuine medieval maze of thousands of lanes, workshops and markets that functions today much as it always has. Simply walking it, getting a little lost, and watching the craft trades at work is the core experience. A licensed guide for the first day turns the labyrinth from daunting into legible, which is why most first-timers hire one. 
 Enter through the Blue Gate 
 Bab Bou Jeloud, the grand tiled Blue Gate, is the classic entrance to the old city and a landmark to navigate back to when you lose your bearings. From here the two main arteries lead down into the medina toward the tannery and the great monuments. Fixing the gate in your mind as a reference point makes the maze far less intimidating on a first walk. 

 The Chouara tannery 
 The Chouara tannery, the honeycomb of stone dye pits worked by hand, is the iconic image of Fez and viewed from the terraces of the leather shops that surround it. There is no ticket — you step onto a shop's balcony and are expected to look at its goods. Morning light is best, and knowing the "it's closed, follow me" line is a redirect toward a commission-paying shop saves the usual hassle. Our  tannery guide  covers it in full. 

 The madrasas and the university 
 Fez's madrasas — former Islamic colleges — are among the most beautiful buildings you can enter in Morocco, and unlike the mosques they are open to visitors: the Bou Inania and Al-Attarine madrasas show exquisite Marinid carving, tile and cedar work in intimate courtyards. Al-Qarawiyyin, founded in the 9th century and often called the world's oldest existing university, anchors the medina, though non-Muslims view it mainly from the gates. These are the quiet counterpoint to the tannery's drama. 

 Souks, food and the craft quarters 
 Fez's souks are organised loosely by trade, and wandering the metalworkers', the weavers' and the food quarters is a highlight in itself, less pressured than Marrakech's and more workaday. Sample the street food at a busy stall, watch artisans at their benches, and let the sensory density of the place carry you. The medina is where Fez is most itself, and unhurried time in it is better spent than a rush between ticketed sights. 

 Beyond Fès el-Bali 
 Two quieter quarters reward the time if you have a third day: Fès el-Jdid, the "new" fourteenth-century city, holds the royal palace gates and the old Jewish quarter, the Mellah, with its distinctive balconied houses. Above the medina, the Merinid Tombs offer the classic panorama over the whole city, best at sunset when the call to prayer rises from a thousand rooftops. Neither is essential on a first two days, but both deepen the picture. 
 The rhythm of the medina day 
 Fès el-Bali runs on its own clock: mornings are for the tanneries and the fresh light, midday empties as the heat and the lunch hour arrive, and late afternoon brings the souks back to life. Pacing your day to that rhythm, rather than fighting it, is how the medina stops feeling relentless. A mid-afternoon pause on a terrace with mint tea is not lost time — it is how locals do the same day. 

 How to plan two days 
 A sensible shape is a guided first day covering the medina, tannery and madrasas to get oriented and handle the touts, then a second day exploring independently at your own pace, now that the maze makes sense. Mornings are best for the tannery and the light; afternoons for the souks. Build in time simply to be lost pleasantly, because that, more than any single sight, is what a first-timer remembers about Fez. 

 

 Fez's sights are inseparable from the medina that holds them, and how the city fits a first-timer loop — its place, its pace, the guided first day — is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For getting oriented, the  Fez first-timer's guide  covers the basics. 

 
   Things to do in Fez: FAQ 
    What's the top sight in Fez?   The medina itself — Fès el-Bali, the world's largest car-free urban area. Walking it is the core experience, best with a guide on day one.   
    Can I enter the mosques and university?   Mostly no — they're closed to non-Muslims. But the madrasas are open and stunning, and Al-Qarawiyyin can be seen from its gates.   
    How do I see the tannery?   From a leather shop's terrace — there's no ticket. Go in the morning, and ignore anyone claiming it's "closed".   
    Do I need a guide?   For the first day, it's strongly recommended. Fès el-Bali is a genuine maze, and a guide handles navigation and the touts.

----- fez-vs-marrakech -----
TITLE: Fez vs. Marrakech: Which to Visit (or Do Both)

 Marrakech and Fez are Morocco's two great imperial cities, and first-timers often wonder which to choose — though the honest answer for most trips is both, in sequence. They pull in genuinely different directions: Marrakech is the buzzing, accessible showpiece geared to visitors, while Fez is the older, deeper, more overwhelming medieval maze. Knowing how they differ helps you weight your days even when you see the pair. 

 

 Marrakech: the accessible showpiece 
 Marrakech is the easier introduction to Morocco: its sights are navigable, its tourist infrastructure is extensive, and the energy of Jemaa el-Fnaa and the souks is immediate and thrilling. It is well connected by air and rail, rich in riads and restaurants, and forgiving of a first-timer finding their feet. If you have limited time or want a smoother entry into the country, Marrakech is the natural base. 
 Who Marrakech suits 
 Marrakech suits first-timers wanting a vibrant, manageable introduction, travellers on a shorter trip, and anyone using the city as a springboard to the desert, the Atlas or the coast. It is the more comfortable of the two to arrive in cold, and the easier to enjoy without a guide. For many, it is both the start and the hub of a Morocco trip. 

 Fez: the deeper medieval city 
 Fez is the older spiritual and cultural heart, and its medina is a larger, more labyrinthine and more intense experience — less polished for tourism, more authentically medieval, and more overwhelming on arrival. It rewards the traveller willing to engage with that intensity, offering a sense of a living historical city that Marrakech, for all its energy, does not quite match. It is the connoisseur's city of the two. 
 Who Fez suits 
 Fez suits travellers drawn to history and authenticity over convenience, those on a longer trip with time to go deeper, and anyone who wants the medieval maze at its most genuine. It benefits more from a guided first day than Marrakech does, precisely because it is harder. For a second Moroccan city, or a second trip, it is often the more memorable. 

 The case for both 
 On a trip of a week or more, seeing both is the standard and rewarding choice, since they complement rather than repeat each other — the showpiece and the maze, connected directly by train. A common shape is Marrakech and the desert first, then the train north to Fez, absorbing the country's range in one loop. Doing both is less about ticking two boxes than about experiencing two genuinely different Moroccos. 

 Getting between the two 
 The practical detail that makes "both" easy is the train: Marrakech and Fez are linked directly by ONCF rail, a comfortable journey of several hours that turns the two cities into the natural endpoints of a single loop. There is no need to double back or arrange a driver — you simply take the train, often via Casablanca. That direct connection is a large part of why seeing both is the standard first-timer plan rather than an ambitious one. 
 Which order to visit them 
 Most loops start in Marrakech, because it is the gentler introduction and the usual springboard for the desert, then move north to Fez once you have found your feet. Arriving in Fez first is not wrong, but landing cold in its harder maze asks more of a jet-lagged first-timer. Ending in Fez also lets the trip build from the accessible showpiece toward the deeper, more demanding city, which many find the more satisfying arc. 

 If you must choose one 
 Forced to pick, choose Marrakech for a first, shorter, or more cautious trip, and Fez for a deeper, longer, or second visit. Neither is wrong, and the "better" city depends entirely on what you want — ease and energy, or depth and authenticity. Weight your days toward whichever pull is stronger for you, and use the other, if you have time, as the counterpoint that makes both sharper. 

 

 Whether you do one city or both, and in what order against the desert and the coast, is a sequencing decision that shapes the whole trip. How the imperial cities fit the loop is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide .

   Fez vs Marrakech: FAQ 
    Which is better for a first trip?   Marrakech, usually — it's more accessible, better connected, and a smoother introduction. Fez rewards a deeper or second visit.   
    Should I see both?   On a week or more, yes. They complement each other and are linked directly by train — the showpiece and the medieval maze.   
    How are they different?   Marrakech is the buzzing, visitor-geared showpiece; Fez is the older, larger, more intense and authentic medieval city.   
    Which needs a guide more?   Fez. Its medina is a bigger, harder maze, so a guided first day helps more there than in Marrakech.   
    If I only have time for one?   Marrakech for a shorter or cautious trip; Fez for a longer, history-focused one. Pick by what you want — energy or depth.   
 


----- morocco-argan-cooperatives -----
TITLE: The Argan Oil Cooperative Visit: Real vs. Tourist Trap

 An argan oil cooperative is one of the more pleasant stops on a Moroccan road trip, and also one of the most reliable places a driver or guide will deliver you for a commission. Genuine women's cooperatives do real good and sell a real product; staged ones exist mainly to pay whoever brought you. Telling them apart is straightforward once you know what the commission structure makes the difference, and what to look for on the ground. 

 

 Why you keep being taken there 
 Argan cooperatives sit on the tourist routes between Marrakech, Essaouira and the south, and drivers and guides are often paid a commission to stop at particular ones, exactly as they are for carpet and craft shops. That does not make the stop bad — the oil is genuine and the visit interesting — but it explains why it appears on your itinerary whether or not you asked. Knowing who benefits lets you enjoy the stop without being steered into overpaying. 

 What a genuine cooperative looks like 
 A real women's cooperative is a working operation: you see women cracking the argan nuts and grinding the paste by hand, the process is explained without heavy pressure to buy, and the prices are fixed and fair rather than negotiated. These cooperatives were set up to give rural women income and independence, and a genuine one feels like a workplace you are visiting, not a showroom built around a till. The demonstration is real work, not a performance staged for the coach. 
 The signs of a staged stop 
 A staged operation tips its hand: a hard sell the moment you arrive, prices that flex if you hesitate, a "demonstration" that is thin and hurried, and a shop far larger and glossier than the tiny production area behind it. If the pressure to buy outweighs the interest in showing you the craft, you are in a commission trap wearing a cooperative's name. You can look, decline, and leave — the visit costs you nothing but the driver's disappointment. 

 Buying argan oil well 
 If you want to buy, and it is a lovely thing to bring home, buy at a cooperative that shows genuine production, choose culinary or cosmetic grade knowingly, and pay the fixed price without feeling rushed. Real argan oil is not cheap, since the process is enormously labour-intensive, so a suspiciously low price is its own warning. Buying from the source supports the women who made it and usually means better oil than a souk middleman offers. 

 What argan oil actually is 
 Argan oil is pressed from the nuts of the argan tree, which grows almost exclusively in southwestern Morocco, and the traditional hand process is genuinely laborious: cracking the hard shells between stones, roasting or leaving the kernels raw, and grinding them into a paste that yields the oil. Culinary argan, from roasted kernels, is nutty and used in food; cosmetic argan, from raw kernels, goes on skin and hair. Understanding the two grades stops you buying the wrong one. 
 Why real argan costs what it does 
 The sheer labour behind each bottle is why genuine argan oil is never cheap, and why a bargain price should make you suspicious rather than pleased — it usually signals dilution with other oils. Buying from a cooperative that shows the production, at a fixed and frankly high price, is the reliable way to get the real thing. The cost is the craft; paying it is how you know you are getting argan rather than something cut with cheaper oil. 

 How to handle the stop 
 Treat the cooperative as you would any commission-driven stop: go in curious, enjoy the demonstration, and buy only if you genuinely want to and the operation feels real. You are never obliged to purchase to justify the visit, whatever the atmosphere implies. A polite thank-you and a walk back to the car is always available, and a good driver takes it in stride as part of the job.

TITLE: Guided Day Tours in Morocco: What They Really Include

 Guided day tours are one of the easiest ways to see Morocco's sights without arranging your own transport, but the cheap headline price often conceals where the tour actually makes its money: the shopping stops. Understanding what a day tour typically includes, what is quietly extra, and how the commission stops work lets you book the right one and enjoy it without the mid-trip surprises travellers most often report. 

 

 What's usually included 
 A standard day tour generally includes transport in an air-conditioned vehicle, a driver and sometimes a guide, and the route between the advertised sights. What it often does not include, despite the impression the price gives, is entry fees to monuments, lunch, and any activities at the stops. Reading the inclusions line by line before booking is the single habit that prevents a "cheap" tour becoming an expensive day once the extras are added. 

 The commission stops 
 Many day tours, especially the cheaper ones, build in stops at carpet shops, argan cooperatives or craft workshops where the operator earns a commission on what the group buys, and these stops can eat a real chunk of the day. The stop itself is not a scam — the goods are genuine — but a tour priced below the obvious cost of the transport is usually recouping the difference at the till. If the itinerary is vague about shopping, ask directly. 
 Ask before you book 
 The questions that save the day are simple: what exactly is included, are entry fees and lunch extra, and how many shopping stops are on the route. A reputable operator answers clearly and in writing; evasiveness on the shopping question is itself informative. Booking a slightly more expensive tour that is honest about its inclusions often costs less overall than a cheap one padded with commission stops. 

 Group tours versus private 
 Day tours come as shared group trips or private ones, and the trade is familiar: group tours are cheaper and social but fixed in pace and prone to the shopping stops, while private tours cost more and buy flexibility, fewer detours, and the ability to say "no shopping today" and be heard. For a family or a group, a private day tour is often better value than it first appears once you factor in the control it buys. 

 The long-day trap 
 Some cheap tours pack so much driving and so many stops into a single day that the sights themselves get only minutes, and the commission stops get the hours. A day trip that promises the desert, a pass, a kasbah and three viewpoints all in one day is often mostly a day in a minibus. Reading the itinerary for how long you actually spend at each place, versus in transit and at shops, tells you whether the day is a tour or a transfer with detours. 
 Multi-day tours and the same rules 
 Everything true of a day tour applies doubly to a multi-day one, where the shopping stops and vague inclusions compound over several days. Confirm the same points — entry fees, meals, shopping stops, time at each sight — before booking a two or three-day desert trip, and get the answers in writing. The longer the tour, the more a few unasked questions can shape, for better or worse, how the days actually feel. 

 Getting a good day out of it 
 To make a day tour work, book one with transparent inclusions, confirm the shopping stops in advance, carry cash for the entry fees and lunch that may be extra, and be comfortable declining to buy at any stop. A well-chosen tour is a genuinely efficient way to see distant sights; a badly chosen one is a slow day of showrooms. The difference is almost entirely in the questions you ask before you pay.

TITLE: Cooking Classes in Morocco: Worth It and How to Choose

 A cooking class is one of the highest-value experiences a first-timer can book in Morocco: a few hours that teach you the dishes, demystify the spices, and send you home able to recreate a tagine, usually for a very reasonable price. The good ones combine a market visit, hands-on cooking and a shared meal, and choosing well is mostly about matching the format to what you want. Here is how they work and what to look for. 

 

 What a good class includes 
 The best cooking classes follow a satisfying arc: a guided visit to a local market to buy fresh ingredients, an explanation of the key spices and techniques, hands-on preparation of a few classic dishes, and then sitting down to eat what you cooked. A class that includes the market trip teaches you as much about Moroccan food culture as about the recipes, and the shared meal at the end is half the pleasure. Look for that full shape rather than a quick demonstration. 

 The market visit 
 The market portion is where a class earns its keep beyond the recipes: a good instructor walks you through the spice stalls, the produce and the butchers, showing you what to buy and how, so the souk becomes legible rather than bewildering. For many travellers this is the moment Moroccan food finally makes sense, and it doubles as a gentle, guided introduction to shopping in a market you will otherwise navigate alone. It is worth choosing a class that includes it. 

 What you'll cook 
 Most classes teach the staples a first-timer wants to take home: a tagine or two, couscous, Moroccan salads, and often bread or pastries, with mint tea throughout. The dishes are chosen to be reproducible in a home kitchen, and a good instructor sends you off with the recipes and the confidence to make them again. It is the rare souvenir that keeps giving long after the trip, and it travels home in your hands rather than your luggage. 

 How to choose one 
 Pick a class by its format and reviews: confirm it includes the market visit and a full hands-on session rather than a watch-and-taste demonstration, check the group size (smaller is more hands-on), and read recent reviews for the quality of the instructor. Riads and hotels often run or recommend well-regarded classes, and booking through your accommodation is an easy, reliable route. Marrakech and Fez both have excellent options. 

 Classes for families and dietary needs 
 Cooking classes adapt well to families and to dietary requirements, which makes them a flexible booking: children often enjoy the hands-on element, and vegetarian or other needs are easily accommodated since Moroccan cuisine is rich in vegetable dishes. Mentioning any requirements when you book lets the instructor plan the market trip and menu around them. It is one of the few activities that works for a whole family at once, across ages. 
 What to expect to pay 
 A group cooking class is inexpensive for what it delivers — a market trip, hands-on tuition and a full meal — while a private class costs more and buys a tailored menu and pace. Either way the value is high relative to a comparable restaurant meal, since you leave with a skill as well as a full stomach. Booking through a well-reviewed operator or your riad is the simplest way to be sure of quality. 

 Why it's worth the time 
 On a packed itinerary a cooking class can feel like a luxury to cut, but it is often the experience travellers remember most warmly: it slows the trip down, teaches a genuine skill, and gives you a direct, hands-on connection to the culture through its food. For the price of a nice dinner, you get a half-day of learning and a meal you made yourself. Few bookings on a Morocco trip return as much for the money.
