----- ctm-vs-supratours -----
TITLE: CTM vs. Supratours: Morocco's Coach Companies Compared

 Where Morocco's trains stop, its coaches take over, and two companies dominate the intercity network: CTM and Supratours. For a first-timer they are the reliable, comfortable way to reach Chefchaouen, Essaouira and the towns off the rail line, and the difference between them is small but worth knowing before you book. Here is how the two compare, where each one goes, and the luggage detail that catches people out. 

 

 The two companies at a glance 
 CTM is Morocco's long-established national coach line, running a comprehensive network of comfortable, air-conditioned buses across the country and to many smaller towns. Supratours, run by the national railway ONCF, was built to extend the train network by coach, so its routes deliberately connect to rail hubs and reach destinations like Essaouira that the trains do not. Both are modern, reliable and inexpensive, and either is a fine choice on the routes they share. 
 How they actually differ 
 In practice the differences are modest: CTM has the broader independent network and its own station buildings, while Supratours excels where a journey combines train and coach, since its timetables and ticketing dovetail with ONCF rail. On a route both serve, pick by schedule and price rather than agonising over the brand. Where only one runs your route — Supratours to Essaouira, for instance — the choice makes itself. 

 Where each one goes 
 Between them the two cover the country's coach needs comprehensively: the classic first-timer routes include the coach from Fez to Chefchaouen, Marrakech to Essaouira, and connections to the desert gateways and the smaller towns off the rail line. Supratours is the usual choice for Essaouira and for legs that continue a train journey; CTM is the workhorse for the wider network. For the places trains reach, the train still wins, but for everywhere else these coaches are the backbone. 

 The luggage fee catch 
 The detail that surprises travellers is that both companies charge a small separate fee for hold luggage, paid at the station when you check your bag, on top of the ticket. It is inexpensive but unexpected if you have not budgeted for it, and you get a tag for your bag in return. Keep a little cash for this, arrive with time to check the bag, and hold onto the tag for collection at the other end. 

 How to book 
 Both companies sell tickets online and at their station counters, and for popular routes in high season — especially Fez to Chefchaouen — booking a day or two ahead secures a seat on the departure you want rather than the next one hours later. Off-peak, buying at the station on the day is usually fine. Arrive with time to check luggage and find the right bay, and keep your ticket accessible for the journey. 

 What the ride is actually like 
 Both companies run modern, air-conditioned coaches with assigned or open seating depending on the route, and the longer journeys usually include a rest stop where you can buy snacks and use facilities. The buses are a comfortable, low-stress way to cover distance, and on scenic routes like Fez to Chefchaouen the window views are part of the pleasure. Bring water, a snack and something to read, and the hours pass easily. 
 Arriving at the right station 
 One practical wrinkle: CTM often uses its own dedicated terminals, which can sit apart from the main municipal bus station, so confirm which station your departure leaves from when you book. Turning up at the wrong terminal is the classic missed-coach mistake. Your ticket states the departure point, and a quick check the day before — or a word with your riad — saves a stressful taxi dash across town on the morning. 

 Coaches versus the alternatives 
 On routes with no train, the coach beats the shared grand taxi for comfort and predictability, though the taxi is faster and more flexible if you do not mind the format. A private driver costs more and buys door-to-door convenience. For most first-timers reaching Chefchaouen or Essaouira, a CTM or Supratours coach is the sweet spot of comfort, price and reliability — the sensible default where the rails run out. 

 

 Choosing coach, train, taxi or driver for each leg is one thread of the sequencing that decides how a whole trip flows and where you can base. How the modes fit a first-timer route is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the rail side, see our  trains guide ; for the full picture, the  transport overview  covers every mode.

----- marrakech-to-fez-transport -----
TITLE: Getting from Marrakech to Fez: Every Option

 Marrakech and Fez are the two anchors of a classic Morocco loop, and getting between them is one of the trip's defining logistical choices — not because it is hard, but because the option you pick reshapes the itinerary. You can take the direct train, a coach, a private driver, or fold the journey into a multi-day desert tour that turns the transfer into the highlight of the trip. Here is every option and when each makes sense. 

 

 The train: the standard choice 
 The ONCF train is the default way between Marrakech and Fez, a comfortable ride of roughly seven to eight hours, usually with a change at Casablanca, and it is the option most independent first-timers take. First class buys a reserved seat for a small premium — worth it on a journey this length, especially with luggage. It is calm, reliable and lets you read or watch the country pass, and it drops you in the centre of each city. 
 Why the train usually wins 
 For a straightforward city-to-city transfer, the train beats the alternatives on comfort and simplicity: no driving, no negotiation, a fixed schedule and a seat you can book. Booking a day ahead in high season secures first class. Unless you specifically want the desert in between or door-to-door service, the train is the sensible default, and the change at Casablanca is simple rather than a hassle. 

 The coach 
 A CTM coach runs the route too, comparable in time to the train and slightly cheaper, and it is a fine alternative if the train schedule does not suit or seats are sold out. It is comfortable and air-conditioned, though it lacks the train's room to move on a long journey. For most travellers the train edges it, but the coach is a solid backup and occasionally the better fit for timing. 

 The private driver 
 Hiring a private driver for the Marrakech-to-Fez run costs considerably more but buys door-to-door service and the freedom to stop along the way, and it makes most sense if you want to see sights between the cities rather than travel directly. A driver can route you past Beni Mellal or through scenery a train skips, turning the transfer into a touring day. For a family or a group, the cost per head narrows and the convenience can justify it. 

 Flying between them 
 There are also domestic flights on some city pairs, though for Marrakech to Fez the short flight time is usually outweighed by the airport rigmarole at both ends, and the train often wins door to door once you count check-in and transfers. Flying makes more sense for longer hops — reaching the far south or the coast — than for a route the train covers comfortably in an afternoon. For most first-timers, the direct train remains the better use of the day. 

 The desert tour route 
 The most rewarding option for many is not a direct transfer at all: a multi-day desert tour that starts in Marrakech and ends in Fez, crossing the High Atlas, Aït Benhaddou, the Dades Valley and the dunes of Merzouga on the way. This turns the journey between the cities into the centerpiece of the whole trip, and it neatly solves the transfer by making it the Sahara leg. If the desert is on your list, ending it in Fez is the elegant way to sequence a loop. 
 One-way tours solve the logistics 
 Because these tours run one-way, Marrakech to Fez, they save you doubling back over the long southern drive, and they fold the transport, the guide and the desert night into a single booking. It is the reason so many first-timer loops put the desert in the middle: you are travelling between the cities anyway, so you might as well do it through the Sahara. Confirm the tour ends in Fez when you book. 

 

 Whether the Marrakech-Fez leg is a train ride or the desert centerpiece is the single choice that most shapes a first loop's order and pace. How that decision ripples through the trip is worked out in  the complete Morocco trip guide . 

 For the desert version, see our  Sahara tours guide ; for the rail option, the  trains guide  covers routes and classes.

    How long does the train take?   Roughly seven to eight hours with a change at Casablanca. Book first class a day ahead in high season for a reserved seat.   
    Is a private driver worth it?   If you want to stop at sights along the way or need door-to-door service, yes. It costs more but turns the transfer into a touring day.   
    Can I see the desert on the way?   Yes — a multi-day one-way desert tour from Marrakech to Fez crosses the Atlas and the dunes, turning the transfer into the trip's highlight.

----- morocco-private-driver -----
TITLE: Hiring a Private Driver in Morocco: Costs and How It Works

 Hiring a private driver is one of the best-value decisions on a Morocco trip, and it costs far less than most Americans assume — a fraction of what an equivalent service would run at home. It removes the hardest logistical parts of the country, the long drives and the navigation, and often doubles as informal guiding. Knowing what it costs, what it should include, and where it beats the train helps you decide when to book one. Here is how it works. 

 

 What a private driver actually is 
 A private driver in Morocco means hiring a car with a professional driver, typically by the day or for a multi-day route, who handles all the driving, navigation and often the practical arrangements along the way. It is not a chauffeur in the luxury sense so much as a practical, affordable way to move around a country where the drives are long and the roads demanding. Many drivers speak good English and know the routes and stops intimately. 

 What it costs, roughly 
 Private drivers are usually priced as a day rate, sometimes with fuel included and sometimes added, and the figure is genuinely modest by American standards — this is one area where Morocco offers real value. The rate typically covers the car, the driver and the driving; it does not usually cover your accommodation, meals or entry fees, nor the driver's tip at the end. Confirm exactly what is included before you book, and get it in writing. 
 What's included and what isn't 
 Establish up front whether fuel is in the rate, whether the driver's own meals and lodging on a multi-day trip are your responsibility (they often are, and are inexpensive), and whether the itinerary includes shopping stops — because a driver paid commission at cooperatives and shops has a quiet incentive to detour. A good driver is transparent about all of this. State clearly that you are not shopping if that is your preference, and a professional respects it. 

 When a driver beats the train 
 The driver wins where the train cannot help: the desert and the deep south, the Atlas Mountains, scenic routes between towns off the rail line, and any itinerary where stopping at sights along the way is the point. It also suits families and groups, for whom the per-head cost drops and the door-to-door convenience with luggage and children is worth a great deal. On the rail spine between the imperial cities, though, the train is usually the better and cheaper choice. 

 The commission question, plainly 
 The one thing to settle honestly with any driver is shopping stops, because some drivers earn a commission at the cooperatives, carpet shops and craft workshops they bring clients to, and an itinerary can quietly fill with retail detours if you do not raise it. This does not make drivers dishonest — it is simply how part of the trade works — but stating clearly at the outset that you are not shopping, or want only stops you request, keeps the days yours. A good driver agrees without fuss. 

 How to book a good one 
 The reliable routes are through your riad or hotel, a reputable tour operator, or a personal recommendation, rather than hailing one off the street. Booking through your accommodation means someone has vetted the driver and you have recourse if something goes wrong. Read recent reviews where you can, confirm the language you need, and agree the route, the rate and the inclusions the day before rather than on the morning. 
 Tipping the driver 
 A multi-day driver who has looked after you well is tipped at the end, in a lump, more substantially than any single daily service — there is no fixed figure, but it should feel proportional to the days and the care. This is separate from the rate and expected, so budget for it. A driver who has navigated the long desert roads, handled the stops and been good company has earned a genuine tip.

TITLE: Airport Transfers in Morocco: What to Arrange in Advance

 The ride from a Moroccan airport into town is your first negotiation in the country, arriving at the worst possible moment: jet-lagged, unfamiliar, and holding dirham you withdrew ten minutes ago. It is short and cheap in principle, and the single most common place first-timers overpay. Arranging the transfer in advance removes the whole problem, and the options divide neatly by how much hassle each one spares you. Here is what to arrange and why. 

 

 Why arrange it in advance 
 A prearranged transfer means a driver is waiting for you with your name, at a fixed price agreed before you fly, so there is no haggling at the rank when you are least equipped for it. For a first arrival — especially into a medina where cars cannot reach the door and you must be walked in — this is worth the small premium over a haggled taxi many times over. The peace of mind of a known driver and a known price is the whole point. 
 The medina complication 
 Riad addresses in the medina are often unreachable by car, so arrival involves meeting someone at a car-accessible point and walking through lanes you do not yet know. A transfer booked through your riad folds that into the price and the plan: the driver knows where to drop you and the riad knows to meet you. Arriving cold to find your own way in, in the dark, with luggage, is the classic first-night ordeal this avoids. 

 The options, ranked by hassle 
 In rough order from least to most friction: a riad or hotel transfer arranged in advance is the smoothest; a prebooked private airport-transfer service is similar; a petit taxi from the official rank with a fare agreed before the doors close is the solid standard; and the public bus, where it exists, is the budget option for light, confident travellers arriving in daylight. The right choice depends on how much of a negotiation you want in your first hour — usually as little as possible. 

 The taxi option, done right 
 If you take a taxi from the rank rather than a prearranged transfer, the airport is exactly where the meter is often declared "unavailable," so agree a fixed fare before you get in, and know the going rate beforehand — your riad can tell you the day before. A fair, slightly-above-city airport fare is reasonable; a wildly inflated one is the tourist tax you decline by taking the next car. Settle the number outside the taxi, never at the destination. 

 Grand taxi versus petit taxi at the airport 
 At the rank you may be offered a grand taxi (a larger shared or private car) or a petit taxi (the small city cab), and for an airport run with luggage the grand taxi is often the practical choice, especially for a family or a group. Either way the principle holds: agree the fare before the doors close. The petit taxi is metered in the city but frequently runs on a fixed "airport price," so the negotiation matters more here than anywhere else in town. 
 Late-night arrivals 
 Many US flights land in the evening, exactly when the arrivals hall is quietest and your leverage lowest, which is the strongest argument for a prearranged transfer on the first night. A waiting driver at a known price turns a tired, dark arrival into a simple pickup, with no debate over fares at eleven at night. Save the taxi rank for daytime departures later in the trip, when you know the fair rate and have your bearings. 

 What to arrange before you fly 
 The simple pre-trip checklist: message your riad or hotel to ask about an airport transfer and its fixed price, or book a reputable transfer service; confirm the meeting point and time in writing; and, if you would rather taxi, ask what the going airport fare should be so you have a number in your head. Ten minutes of arranging before you leave turns the first hour in Morocco from a negotiation into a simple handshake with a waiting driver.

TITLE: Day Trips from Fez: Meknes, Volubilis and Beyond

 Fez is not just a destination but a base, and some of the most rewarding half-days of a Morocco trip lie within an easy drive of it: the imperial city of Meknes, the Roman ruins of Volubilis, and the sacred hilltown of Moulay Idriss, often combined into a single excellent day. After the intensity of the Fez medina, a day in the surrounding countryside and its quieter historic sites is a welcome change of pace. Here is what to see and how to do it. 

 

 Volubilis: the Roman ruins 
 Volubilis is the best-preserved Roman site in Morocco, a UNESCO-listed city of columns, arches and remarkably intact floor mosaics set in rolling green countryside about an hour from Fez. Walking among the ruins of a Roman provincial capital, with its triumphal arch and basilica standing against the hills, is a startling contrast to the medieval medina you left that morning. A guide on site brings the mosaics and the layout to life, and the light is best in the morning or late afternoon. 

 Moulay Idriss: the sacred town 
 A short distance from Volubilis, Moulay Idriss is one of Morocco's holiest towns, a whitewashed settlement tumbling across two hills around the tomb of the country's founder, and for centuries it was closed to non-Muslims entirely. Today visitors are welcome to wander its steep lanes and enjoy the views, though the shrine itself remains for Muslims. It pairs naturally with Volubilis, a ten-minute drive away, and adds a very different flavour — living pilgrimage town beside ancient ruin. 

 Meknes: the fourth imperial city 
 Meknes is the least-visited of Morocco's four imperial cities and the better for it: a grand 17th-century capital of monumental gates, vast granaries and stables, and a walkable medina without the crowds of Fez or Marrakech. The colossal Bab Mansour gate and the Heri es-Souani granaries are the highlights, and the whole city has an unhurried ease. Barely an hour from Fez by train or road, it makes an easy half or full day on its own or combined with Volubilis nearby. 

 Combining them in a day 
 The classic outing bundles all three — Meknes, Volubilis and Moulay Idriss — into one full day from Fez, since they cluster together to the west, and this is how most organised tours and private drivers structure it. It is an efficient, varied day: an imperial city, a Roman ruin and a sacred hilltown, all within an hour or so of each other. If you would rather go deeper on fewer sites, Meknes alone or Volubilis-plus-Moulay-Idriss each make a relaxed half-day. 

 What to bring and when to go 
 Volubilis, Meknes and Moulay Idriss are largely open-air sites, so bring sun protection, water and comfortable shoes — Volubilis in particular has little shade among its ruins, and the midday sun is fierce in the warmer months. Spring and autumn are ideal; summer rewards an early start before the heat builds. Dress modestly for Moulay Idriss as an active religious town, and allow enough time at Volubilis to walk the site properly rather than rushing the mosaics. 
 A quieter alternative to the desert day 
 For travellers who cannot fit the long desert trip, or who want a gentler pace, this cluster of sites is an excellent substitute: it delivers Roman history, a sacred town and an imperial city in a single comfortable day, all close to Fez and without the hours of driving the Sahara demands. It is one of the best-value days in the north, and a reminder that Morocco's depth is not only in the south. 

 How to go 
 You can reach Meknes independently by train in about an hour, but Volubilis and Moulay Idriss have no rail link, so the practical options are an organised day tour from Fez or a hired private driver, either of which handles the whole cluster comfortably. A driver gives you flexibility over timing and how long you linger at each; a group tour is cheaper and social. For the three-site day, a driver or tour is far easier than piecing it together yourself.
