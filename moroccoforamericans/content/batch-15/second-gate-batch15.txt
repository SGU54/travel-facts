----- what-to-eat-in-morocco -----
TITLE: What to Eat in Morocco: The Essential Dishes

 Moroccan food is one of the great pleasures of a trip, and knowing the essential dishes before you arrive turns a bewildering menu into a plan. The staples belong to a broader North African and Mediterranean culinary tradition, rendered in a distinctly Moroccan style, and a first-timer can eat superbly for very little by knowing what to seek out. Here are the dishes worth prioritising, what each one is, and how to order them well. 

 

 Tagine: the everyday centrepiece 
 The tagine — a slow-cooked stew named for the conical earthenware pot it is made in — is the dish you will eat most, and its Moroccan versions are superb: chicken with preserved lemon and olives, lamb with prunes and almonds, or a vegetable tagine rich with spices. Every region and cook has their own, so it rewards repeat ordering. Served with bread to scoop rather than cutlery, it is comforting, cheap and endlessly varied. 

 Couscous: the Friday dish 
 Couscous — steamed semolina grains topped with vegetables and meat — is a North African staple traditionally eaten on Fridays, and a proper Moroccan couscous, mounded high with seven vegetables and tender meat, is a feast. Because the authentic version is a Friday ritual, that is the best day to seek it out, when homes and restaurants make it fresh. It is generous, sociable food, meant to be shared from a communal dish. 

 Pastilla and the savoury-sweet tradition 
 Pastilla is the showpiece: a flaky pie, traditionally of pigeon (now often chicken), dusted with cinnamon and sugar over a savoury spiced filling — the savoury-sweet combination that runs through the region's festive cooking. It is richer and more elaborate than everyday food, so it is worth ordering once at a good restaurant to understand this side of the cuisine. It captures a whole tradition of celebration food in a single dish. 

 Street food and snacks 
 The street food is where the everyday flavour lives: grilled skewers (brochettes), bowls of spiced snails from a cart, fried sardines on the coast, sfenj (the North African fried dough rings) at breakfast, and fresh bread from neighbourhood ovens. Jemaa el-Fnaa in Marrakech turns this into a nightly food theatre. Eating where locals queue, and confirming prices at tourist-heavy stalls, gets you the best of it cheaply and safely. 

 Bread, salads and the supporting cast 
 Every meal arrives with khobz, the round flatbread used as an edible utensil, and often a spread of cooked and raw Moroccan salads — zaalouk of smoky aubergine, taktouka of peppers and tomato, and others — that are a highlight in their own right. Harira, the hearty soup of tomatoes, lentils and chickpeas, is the traditional way to break the fast in Ramadan and a satisfying meal any time. These sides are half the pleasure of eating here. 

 Regional specialities worth chasing 
 Beyond the national staples, the regions reward a curious eater: the coast at Essaouira and along the Atlantic serves superb grilled fish and seafood straight off the boats; the south and the desert lean on hearty tagines and dates; and Fez is known for refined, elaborate cooking including its own take on pastilla. Ordering the local speciality wherever you are — asking what the region or town is known for — turns a week of meals into a tour of the country's genuine culinary variety. 
 Sweets and pastries 
 The sweet tradition deserves its own attention: honey-soaked pastries, almond-stuffed cookies, sesame and nut confections, and dates are served with tea and at celebrations, and they are part of a wider regional culture of festive sweets. A plate of assorted pastries with mint tea is the classic way to end a meal or fill an afternoon pause. They are intensely sweet by design, so a little goes a long way, but sampling them is part of eating your way through the country. 

 How to eat well as a first-timer 
 The strategy that works: eat tagine and salads freely at casual places, seek out couscous on a Friday, order pastilla once at a good restaurant, graze the street food where locals do, and finish with mint tea and pastries. Ask what is fresh and regional rather than ordering the same thing everywhere. Eat this way and Morocco is one of the world's great affordable food destinations, with genuine variety across its regions and cities.

----- moroccan-mint-tea -----
TITLE: Mint Tea in Morocco: The Ritual and the Etiquette

 Mint tea is the constant companion of a Moroccan trip: offered in shops, homes, riads and at every pause, it is a gesture of hospitality woven through daily life across North Africa. For a first-timer the ritual can be slightly bewildering — when to accept, whether it obligates you, why it is poured from such a height — and understanding it makes every encounter smoother. Here is what the tea means and how to navigate it gracefully. 

 

 What it is 
 The tea is green tea brewed with fresh spearmint and a generous amount of sugar, served in small glasses rather than cups, and it is drunk throughout the day, not just after meals. Sweet, hot and aromatic, it is central to social life across the Maghreb, and Morocco has its own beloved version. The sugar is traditional and heavy; if you prefer it less sweet, you can ask, though the classic serving is frankly sweet. 

 The pouring ritual 
 You will notice the tea poured from a height, a long stream from pot to glass, and this is deliberate: it aerates the tea, creates a light foam on top, and is a small performance of hospitality and skill. The host may pour the first glass back into the pot to mix it before serving. Watching the pour is part of the pleasure, and a well-executed one from height is a point of pride for the person serving. 

 The etiquette of accepting 
 When tea is offered — in a home, a riad, or a shop — accepting is the gracious response, as it is a genuine gesture of welcome, and sharing a glass is a small act of connection rather than a commitment to anything. In a carpet or craft shop, though, be aware that the tea is also part of the sales ritual: accepting it is fine and does not obligate you to buy, but understand that the hospitality and the sales pitch are intertwined. You can enjoy the tea and still decline the goods. 
 Can you refuse? 
 Refusing tea is not catastrophic, but accepting is warmer, and if you genuinely cannot — time, too much sugar already — a polite, appreciative decline is understood. In a home especially, accepting at least a glass honours the hospitality. The tea is offered in a spirit of generosity, and meeting it in the same spirit, even briefly, is the graceful path. A flat refusal can read as cold in a culture where the offer means a great deal. 

 Tea in the shop, plainly 
 Because mint tea appears the moment you show interest in a carpet or craft shop, it is worth naming the dynamic: the glass is real hospitality and a softening ritual before the negotiation, both at once. Accept it if you like, enjoy it, and keep a clear head about the separate question of whether and what to buy. The tea creates a warm obligation by design, and knowing that lets you receive the warmth without being moved by the obligation. 

 Tea beyond the shop 
 Away from the commerce, the tea is simply lovely: offered by a riad on arrival, poured at the end of a meal, shared on a rooftop as the call to prayer sounds, it punctuates the day with small warm pauses. Cafes serve it all day, and a pot with a plate of pastries is one of the cheapest and most pleasant ways to rest between sights. Learning to enjoy it unhurried is learning something real about the pace of the place. 
 Making it at home 
 Many travellers take the ritual home, buying a pot, glasses and green tea in the souks to recreate it, and a cooking class often teaches the proper method — the brewing, the sugar, the pour from height. It is an inexpensive, evocative souvenir that carries the trip into your kitchen. If you want to buy the kit, the souks have every grade of teapot and glass, and bargaining applies as it does to any craft purchase. 

 Making it part of the trip 
 The best approach is to lean into the ritual: accept the tea when it is offered in good faith, learn to enjoy its sweetness, and treat each glass as the small human moment it is meant to be. It is one of the genuine warmths of travelling in Morocco, and the pauses over tea — on a rooftop, in a shop, in someone's home — are often what travellers remember most fondly. Just keep the shop version's dual nature in mind.

----- vegetarian-vegan-morocco -----
TITLE: Vegetarian and Vegan in Morocco: How to Eat Well

 Morocco is more accommodating to vegetarians than many travellers fear, thanks to a cuisine rich in vegetables, legumes and bread, but it holds a few traps — hidden meat stock, the assumption that chicken is not "meat" — and it is genuinely harder for strict vegans. Knowing which dishes work, what to ask, and the phrases that head off misunderstandings lets plant-based travellers eat very well. Here is how to navigate it. 

 

 Why it's easier than you'd think 
 Moroccan cooking leans heavily on vegetables, pulses and grains, so much of the supporting table is already plant-based: the cooked and raw salads, bread, olives, and vegetable tagines are everywhere and excellent. A vegetarian can assemble satisfying meals from these without special requests in most places. The abundance of vegetable dishes means you are rarely stuck with a plain plate, which is the fate vegetarians often expect in a meat-forward country. 

 The dishes that work 
 The reliable vegetarian options are the vegetable tagine, couscous with vegetables (confirm the broth), the array of salads, lentil and chickpea dishes, and bread with dips like zaalouk. Harira soup is often but not always vegetarian, since it can be made with or without meat stock, so it is worth checking. Between the tagines, the salads and the legume dishes, a vegetarian has real variety rather than a single fallback. 
 The hidden-stock trap 
 The main pitfall is stock: a "vegetable" couscous or soup may be built on a meat broth, since that is the default in many kitchens, so asking specifically whether the broth is meat-free matters. Similarly, be explicit that you eat no meat at all, because in some places chicken or even meat stock may not register as "meat" in the way you intend. Clear, specific questions prevent the well-meaning misunderstanding that puts stock in your "vegetarian" dish. 

 Vegan is harder 
 Strict veganism is more challenging, because bread may contain butter, tagines may use ghee or a meat base, and pastries are rich with butter and eggs, so the plant-based-by-default dishes need more careful checking. The salads, plain vegetable dishes, fruit, olives and bread (confirm no butter) remain safe staples. Vegans travel best by learning to ask precise questions and by anchoring meals on the clearly plant-based dishes rather than assuming a vegetable name guarantees vegan content. 

 The phrases and habits that help 
 Learning a few key phrases pays off: how to say you eat no meat, no chicken, and (for vegans) no butter or eggs, ideally in French or with a card in Arabic. Choosing restaurants that see enough travellers to understand dietary requests, and cooking classes or riads that cater to them, smooths the trip further. Stating your needs clearly and early, rather than mid-meal, is the single most effective habit for eating well without animal products here. 

 Where plant-based eating is easiest 
 The tourist-facing restaurants of Marrakech, Fez and the other main cities increasingly understand vegetarian and vegan requests and often flag suitable dishes on the menu, so eating out in the cities is the easy part. It gets trickier in smaller towns, on the road, and at basic local eateries, where the concept may be less familiar and the safe options fewer. Planning your more careful meals around the cities, and carrying snacks for the gaps, smooths the rural and travel days. 
 Cooking classes and riads help 
 Booking a cooking class that caters to your diet, or a riad that does, is a reliable way to eat well and learn the plant-based side of the cuisine at the same time, since Moroccan cooking has so many naturally vegetable-forward dishes to teach. Mentioning your requirements when you book lets them plan the market trip and menu around them. It is one of the more enjoyable ways to guarantee a few excellent meals that fit your needs. 

 Eating well, plainly 
 The bottom line: vegetarians can eat abundantly and easily with a little checking on stock, while vegans should plan more, ask precisely, and lean on the naturally plant-based dishes. Neither needs to go hungry — Moroccan cuisine's vegetable wealth is genuinely on your side — but both benefit from clear communication. Do that, and the country's food is a pleasure rather than a puzzle, whatever you do or do not eat.

TITLE: Is the Tap Water Safe to Drink in Morocco?

 The practical answer on Moroccan tap water is simple: most visitors should stick to bottled or filtered water, not because the water is necessarily unsafe but because its different microbial content commonly upsets stomachs unused to it. A little care with water, ice and washed foods prevents the traveller's stomach trouble that can derail a few days. Here is what to drink, what to watch, and how to stay hydrated without a mountain of plastic. 

 

 The short answer 
 Stick to sealed bottled water or water you have filtered or purified yourself, particularly for the first part of your trip while your system adjusts. Tap water in cities is treated and locals drink it without issue, but visitors frequently react to the unfamiliar microbes even where the water meets local standards. The upset is usually mild and short, but it is exactly the kind of thing that costs you a sightseeing day, so caution early is worth it. 

 Ice, salads and the less obvious sources 
 Water reaches you in ways beyond the glass: ice made from tap water, raw salads and fruit rinsed in it, and toothbrushing are the common overlooked routes. At reputable hotels and tourist restaurants, ice and washed produce are generally fine; at basic street stalls, more caution helps. Peeling your own fruit, favouring cooked vegetables early on, and using bottled water to brush your teeth if you are being careful are simple habits that remove the hidden exposures. 

 Bottled water, done sensibly 
 Bottled water is cheap and universally available, so keep a supply in your room and carry one when out, checking the seal is intact when you buy. It is the easy default, and the small cost is trivial against a ruined day. Just be conscious that relying entirely on single-use bottles generates a lot of plastic over a trip, which is where a reusable solution earns its place. 
 Cutting the plastic 
 A reusable bottle with a built-in purifying filter, or purification tablets, lets you treat tap water and drink safely while cutting the plastic waste dramatically — a good investment for a longer trip or for the desert and mountains, where bottled supply thins out. For travellers who care about the environmental cost, this is the responsible middle path: safe water without the daily pile of empty bottles. Bring it from home, as such filters are hard to buy locally. 

 Staying hydrated in the heat 
 Morocco gets genuinely hot, especially in summer and the desert, so hydration is a real health matter, not just comfort — carry more water than you think you need and drink steadily through the day. Mint tea and soft drinks do not replace water in the heat. Heat and dehydration cause more trouble for visitors than the water itself, so the discipline of drinking enough safe water is the actual priority. Pair it with sun protection and shade in the fierce hours. 

 Water in the desert and mountains 
 Away from the cities, in the desert and the High Atlas, bottled water supply thins and the heat intensifies, so carry more than you expect to need and top up whenever you can — a desert camp or a mountain village may have limited stock. This is exactly where a purifying filter bottle proves its worth, letting you treat whatever water is available. Running low on water in the remote heat is a genuine risk, so err generously on how much you carry into the empty stretches. 

 If your stomach does turn 
 Should you get a mild stomach upset anyway, it usually passes in a day or two with rest, bland food and — importantly — plenty of safe fluids and rehydration salts to replace what you lose. Carry rehydration sachets and a basic anti-diarrhoeal from home for the worst of it. Persistent or severe symptoms warrant a pharmacy or doctor, both easily found in the cities, but the ordinary traveller's upset resolves on its own with sensible care.

TITLE: The Hammam in Morocco: What to Expect and How to Go

 The hammam — a steam-bath and scrub ritual with deep roots across North Africa and the wider region — is one of the most rewarding experiences a first-timer can have in Morocco, and one of the most intimidating if you do not know what to expect. From the public neighbourhood bath to the plush spa version, it ranges widely, and understanding the ritual, the options and the etiquette lets you relax into it. Here is how the hammam works and how to go. 

 

 What a hammam actually is 
 A hammam is a communal steam bath where you sit in progressively hotter, humid rooms to open the pores, then are scrubbed vigorously with a coarse mitt and black soap to slough off dead skin, and rinsed clean. It is a ritual of deep cleansing and relaxation practised across the Maghreb, and in Morocco it is woven into ordinary life — many locals go weekly. The result is a level of clean and relaxed you rarely reach otherwise, and it is genuinely worth doing at least once. 

 Public versus private 
 There are two very different experiences under the same name. The public neighbourhood hammam is cheap, authentic and local: you bring your own kit, share the steam rooms with locals, and either scrub yourself or pay an attendant a small sum. The tourist or spa hammam is more expensive, private or semi-private, and includes the scrub, oils and pampering in a polished setting. First-timers often start with a spa hammam for comfort and try a public one later for authenticity. 
 Which to choose first 
 If you are nervous, begin with a spa or riad hammam where staff guide you through in English and the setting is gentle; if you want the real, local ritual and do not mind the plunge, the public hammam is cheaper and more memorable. Neither is wrong — they are simply different experiences of the same tradition. Many travellers do both over a trip, using the spa version to learn the ritual and the public one to live it. 

 What to bring and expect 
 For a public hammam you typically bring flip-flops, a towel, a change of underwear (worn during the process), black soap, a scrubbing mitt and any toiletries — though spa hammams provide everything. Expect to be largely undressed (underwear on) in a single-sex setting, and expect the scrub to be more vigorous than you anticipate. It is not painful, but it is thorough, and the sheets of dead skin it removes are startling the first time. Modesty norms are respected throughout. 

 The etiquette that matters 
 Hammams are single-sex, either separate facilities or separate hours, so check before you go, and keep your underwear on throughout, as full nudity is not the norm. Bring a small amount of cash to tip the attendant who scrubs you. Move calmly, follow the lead of those around you in a public bath, and do not photograph inside — it is a private, communal space. Observing these simple norms makes you a welcome rather than awkward presence. 

 Who should skip or take care 
 The hammam's intense heat means a few travellers should be cautious: those who are pregnant, have heart conditions, low blood pressure, or certain other health issues should check with a doctor first and consider a gentler spa version over the fierce public bath. Stay hydrated, do not push through dizziness, and step out to cool down if the heat becomes too much. For most people it is entirely safe and deeply relaxing, but listening to your body in the heat matters. 
 Booking and timing your visit 
 Spa hammams are easily booked through your riad or online, and they get busy in high season, so reserve a day or two ahead for the time you want. Public hammams need no booking — you simply turn up during the correct single-sex hours, which locals or your riad can tell you. Allowing a relaxed hour or more, with nothing scheduled immediately after, lets you enjoy the post-hammam glow rather than rushing back out into the day. 

 Making it part of the trip 
 The ideal moment for a hammam is after a few days of dusty walking, or at the end of a desert trip, when the deep clean feels most earned and most restorative. Book a spa hammam through your riad, or ask locally for the neighbourhood bath, and allow an unhurried hour or more. It is a highlight travellers consistently wish they had done sooner — a reset for body and mood that also opens a window onto an everyday ritual of the culture.
