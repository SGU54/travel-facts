TITLE: Morocco for First-Timers: Everything You Need to Know Before You Go

<p>Morocco is one of those places that feels impossibly far from home and then, three hours after landing, feels like somewhere you've always wanted to be. For a first-time American traveler, it's an easy introduction to North Africa and the Arab world: it's set up for tourism, the distances are manageable, and you can pack sweeping desert, medieval cities, and Atlantic beaches into a single trip. This guide covers the things first-timers actually worry about, so you can plan with confidence rather than guesswork.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Aerial view of Marrakech medina rooftops with the Atlas Mountains behind -->

<h2>Do Americans need a visa for Morocco?</h2>
<p>No. U.S. citizens can enter Morocco visa-free for tourism and stay up to 90 days. You'll get a stamp on arrival; there's no fee and no online application to fill out beforehand. That said, entry rules are the one thing worth double-checking close to your trip, since governments change them without much notice. Make sure your passport is valid for at least six months beyond your entry date and has a blank page for the stamp. We cover the details in our <a href="/article/morocco-visa-for-americans">Morocco visa guide for Americans</a>.</p>

<h2>Is Morocco safe?</h2>
<p>For the vast majority of visitors, yes. Violent crime against tourists is rare, and Morocco is generally considered one of the safer countries in the region — millions of travelers, including solo women, visit every year without incident. What you'll actually deal with is petty stuff: pickpocketing in crowds, aggressive touts, and medina scams like a stranger insisting a street is "closed" so they can lead you somewhere. None of it is dangerous, but it's worth knowing about so it doesn't rattle you. Our <a href="/article/is-morocco-safe">honest safety guide</a> walks through it, and the <a href="/article/morocco-scams-to-avoid">common scams</a> are easy to sidestep once you know the playbook.</p>

<h2>Money: the dirham is a closed currency</h2>
<p>Here's the single most useful money fact for a first-timer: you cannot get Moroccan dirham before you arrive. It's a "closed" currency, which means you can't buy it at your bank at home the way you might grab euros or pounds. Instead, you withdraw dirham from an ATM once you land (there are ATMs right in the Casablanca and Marrakech arrival halls) or exchange dollars at a licensed bureau. Morocco runs heavily on cash — souks, taxis, and small cafes are cash-only — while hotels and nicer restaurants take cards. Read our <a href="/article/moroccan-dirham-money-guide">dirham and money guide</a> before you go, and our <a href="/article/morocco-trip-cost">cost breakdown</a> to build a budget in dollars.</p>

<h2>When should you visit?</h2>
<p>Spring (March to May) and fall (September to November) are the sweet spots: warm days, cool nights, and comfortable conditions for both the cities and the desert. Summer gets brutally hot inland — Marrakech, Fez, and the Sahara can push past 100&deg;F — though the coast at Essaouira stays mild. Winter is cool and pleasant in the cities, cold at night in the desert, and snowy in the Atlas Mountains. If your dates overlap Ramadan, the rhythm of daily life changes; it's a fascinating time to visit but worth understanding first. See our <a href="/article/best-time-to-visit-morocco">month-by-month guide</a>.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: Traveler walking through a narrow, lantern-lined souk alley -->

<h2>Where to go on a first trip</h2>
<p>The classic first-timer loop covers a handful of standout places, each completely different from the last:</p>
<h3>Marrakech</h3>
<p>The sensory heart of Morocco and the usual starting point, with the famous Jemaa el-Fnaa square, endless souks, and gorgeous gardens. See our <a href="/article/marrakech-first-time-guide">Marrakech guide</a>.</p>
<h3>Fez</h3>
<p>Home to the world's largest car-free medieval city — a genuine maze where a local guide for the first day pays for itself.</p>
<h3>Chefchaouen</h3>
<p>The blue-washed mountain town, small, relaxed, and endlessly photogenic.</p>
<h3>The Sahara</h3>
<p>A night in a desert camp among the dunes of Erg Chebbi is the bucket-list experience — camel ride at sunset, dinner under the stars, sunrise over the sand.</p>
<h3>Essaouira and the Atlas Mountains</h3>
<p>A windswept Atlantic beach town and the Berber villages of the High Atlas round out the trip if you have time.</p>

<h2>Getting around</h2>
<p>Morocco is easier to navigate than you'd expect. Comfortable ONCF trains connect Casablanca, Rabat, Fez, and Marrakech, with a high-speed line up north. For places off the rail network — Chefchaouen, Essaouira, and the desert gateways — CTM and Supratours coaches are the reliable options. In cities, small metered "petit taxis" handle short hops (insist on the meter or agree the fare first). The Sahara is almost always done as a multi-day tour with a driver. More in our <a href="/article/getting-around-morocco-transport">transport guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Language: will you get by?</h2>
<p>Yes. Arabic and Tamazight (Berber) are the official languages, and French is widely spoken in business, government, and tourism — a legacy you'll notice on signs and menus. In the places tourists spend time, English is common: hotel and riad staff, guides, and popular restaurants will usually handle English fine. Away from the tourist track, a few words of French go further than English, and any attempt at Arabic — a simple "salam" (hello) or "shukran" (thank you) — is warmly received. You don't need to speak the language to travel here comfortably, but a handful of phrases makes every interaction friendlier.</p>

<h2>What a first trip actually feels like</h2>
<p>Set your expectations for contrast, because that's the Morocco experience. One hour you're haggling for a lantern in a chaotic souk with a dozen smells competing for your attention; the next you're sipping tea in silent riad courtyard. The cities are intense and can be tiring — the constant sensory input and the occasional tout wear on you — so build in downtime and don't try to cram every day full. The desert and mountains, by contrast, are serene. A well-paced first trip alternates the two, and most people leave already planning a return.</p>

<h2>Practical first-timer tips</h2>
<p>A few things that smooth out a first trip: dress modestly, especially outside tourist zones — covering shoulders and knees is respectful and also helps with sun and heat. Get an <a href="/article/morocco-esim-sim-wifi">eSIM</a> so you land connected, which is handy when you need maps or a ride the moment you arrive and don't have dirham yet. Accept the mint tea when it's offered; it's hospitality, not a sales trap. And decide early whether you want a <a href="/article/morocco-guided-vs-independent">guided tour or independent travel</a> — both work well here, and it shapes how you book everything else.</p>

TITLE: Do Americans Need a Visa for Morocco? Entry Rules Explained

<p>If you're a U.S. citizen planning a trip to Morocco, the entry situation is refreshingly simple: <strong>you do not need a visa for tourism.</strong> Americans can enter Morocco visa-free and stay for up to 90 days. There's no application to fill out in advance, no fee to pay, and no online authorization to arrange. You show up, hand over your passport, get a stamp, and you're in. Still, a few details are worth getting right before you fly, because the border is not the place to discover a problem.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: A U.S. passport being stamped at a Moroccan airport immigration desk -->

<h2>What U.S. citizens need to enter Morocco</h2>
<p>For a standard tourist visit, you need surprisingly little:</p>
<h3>A valid passport</h3>
<p>Your passport should be valid for at least six months beyond your date of entry. This is the standard recommendation, and while enforcement varies, you don't want to gamble on it — airlines sometimes deny boarding over passport validity before you even reach Morocco. Make sure there's at least one blank page for the entry stamp.</p>
<h3>Proof of onward or return travel</h3>
<p>Immigration or your airline at check-in may ask to see a return or onward ticket showing you plan to leave within the 90-day window. In practice this is usually your return flight. It's rarely a hard checkpoint, but have it ready.</p>
<h3>Nothing else, in most cases</h3>
<p>There's no requirement to show hotel bookings or cash for a normal tourist stay, though proof of sufficient funds and an address can occasionally be requested. Filling in the arrival card fully and having your first night's accommodation written down keeps things smooth.</p>

<h2>How long can you stay?</h2>
<p>The visa-free tourist stay is up to <strong>90 days</strong>. If you overstay, you can face fines and delays when you try to leave, so keep an eye on the date stamped in your passport. If you want to stay longer, you'd need to look into an extension through the local authorities or a proper visa — but for the overwhelming majority of trips, 90 days is far more than enough.</p>

<h2>Do you need an eVisa or travel authorization?</h2>
<p>As things stand, no. Unlike some countries that have rolled out an electronic travel authorization (the kind of pre-registration you may have done for other destinations), Morocco does not require U.S. tourists to register online before arrival. This is exactly the kind of rule that can change, though, so it's the one thing we'd flag to verify close to your travel date. Watch out for third-party websites that charge a fee for a "Morocco visa" or "travel authorization" for Americans — since none is required, these are best avoided. Rely on official Moroccan government or U.S. State Department information rather than a site that found you through an ad.</p>

<h2>Entry by land or sea</h2>
<p>The same visa-free rules apply whether you fly in or arrive overland — for example, crossing from the Spanish enclaves of Ceuta or Melilla, or by ferry from Spain across the Strait of Gibraltar. You'll clear passport control and get the same stamp. If you're doing a multi-country trip, just remember each exit and re-entry starts a fresh interaction with immigration, so keep your documents accessible and expect the officer to stamp you both out and in.</p>
<p>One practical note that trips up some travelers: the 90-day clock is generous, but tracking it is your responsibility, not the border's — there's no automatic reminder. If your itinerary loops through Morocco more than once, which is common on a broader North Africa or southern Europe trip, keep a rough count of your in-country days so a later re-entry doesn't accidentally push you toward the limit. For a normal one-and-done vacation this is a non-issue, but it's worth a moment's thought on a longer journey.</p>

<h2>Check your stamp at the border</h2>
<p>It sounds obvious, but take ten seconds at immigration to confirm you actually received an entry stamp and that the date is legible. On busy arrival days, a rushed officer occasionally forgets, and a missing entry stamp can create a headache when you try to leave — you may have to explain when and where you entered. If you cross overland or at a smaller point of entry, this is even more worth a glance. It's a small habit that saves a rare but annoying problem.</p>

<h2>A note on customs</h2>
<p>Entry rules aren't just about your passport. Morocco has strict customs rules worth knowing: drones are effectively banned without a hard-to-get license and will be confiscated at the airport, and the local currency can't be brought in or taken out freely (the dirham is a closed currency). We cover the money side in detail in our <a href="/article/moroccan-dirham-money-guide">dirham guide</a>. For most travelers, customs is a non-event, but leave the drone at home.</p>

<h2>Bottom line for first-timers</h2>
<p>Morocco is one of the easier countries for Americans to enter: no visa, no fee, no paperwork, 90 days. Bring a passport valid six months out with a blank page, have your return ticket handy, skip any site trying to sell you a visa you don't need, and give the entry requirements a quick re-check in the weeks before you fly. With that done, the only thing left is the fun part — planning where to go, which our <a href="/article/morocco-first-time-guide">first-timer's guide</a> lays out.</p>

----- moroccan-dirham-money-guide -----
TITLE: The Moroccan Dirham: Why You Can't Get It Before You Arrive (and What to Do)

<p>Money is where first-time visitors to Morocco most often get tripped up, and it starts before you even leave home. The Moroccan dirham (MAD) is a <strong>closed currency</strong> — you cannot buy it at your bank in the U.S. the way you'd pick up euros or pounds before a trip. That surprises a lot of travelers, so here's exactly how money works in Morocco and how to arrive prepared.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Colorful Moroccan dirham banknotes and coins fanned out on a table -->

<h2>What "closed currency" actually means</h2>
<p>A closed currency is one the government restricts from leaving or entering the country. For you, that has two practical consequences. First, you can't get dirham before you arrive — no ordering it from your bank, no exchange desk at your home airport. Second, you can't take much of it home: the export limit is 2,000 MAD, and exceeding that is actually against the rules, not just something to declare. So the game is simple — get dirham when you land, and spend it down before you leave.</p>

<h2>How to get dirham when you arrive</h2>
<h3>ATMs are your best bet</h3>
<p>The easiest way to get cash is an ATM, and there are ATMs right in the arrival halls at Casablanca (CMN) and Marrakech (RAK) airports. Withdraw from machines attached to major banks — Attijariwafa, Bank of Africa (BMCE), Banque Populaire, or CIH — and you'll get the official rate automatically. Tell your bank you're traveling so your card isn't frozen, and expect a per-withdrawal cap that can feel low, so you may make more than one withdrawal over a trip.</p>
<h3>Exchange bureaus</h3>
<p>You can also exchange U.S. dollars (bring clean, untorn bills) at licensed bureaux de change — look for the official Bank Al-Maghrib certification. Keep your exchange receipts: you'll need them to convert leftover dirham back to dollars at the airport when you leave.</p>

<h2>What's the exchange rate?</h2>
<p>Exchange rates move daily, so we don't print a fixed number here — check the live rate before you go:</p>
<div class="feature-converter" data-from="USD" data-to="MAD">100 USD &asymp; &hellip; MAD (enable JavaScript for the live rate)</div>
<p>As a rough mental model, a mid-range restaurant main might run you around <span class="feature-price" data-amount="90" data-currency="MAD">90 MAD</span>, and a short petit-taxi ride in the city something like <span class="feature-price" data-amount="25" data-currency="MAD">25 MAD</span>. The site shows today's dollar equivalent next to each so you always know what you're actually spending.</p>

<h2>Cash vs. card in Morocco</h2>
<p>Morocco runs heavily on cash. Souks, taxis, small cafes, and anything in rural areas or the desert are cash-only, full stop. Cards (Visa and Mastercard) are accepted at hotels, upscale restaurants, and modern city shops — but the moment you step into a medina, you'll want dirham in hand. Carry a mix of small notes and coins; vendors rarely have change for big bills, and small change is essential for tips and taxis. For a full budget breakdown in dollars, see our <a href="/article/morocco-trip-cost">Morocco trip cost guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Avoiding the money traps</h2>
<p>A few small habits keep more of your dollars in your pocket. When an ATM or card machine asks whether to charge you in dollars or dirham, always choose dirham — the "pay in USD" option (called dynamic currency conversion) bakes in a worse rate. Skip the exchange counters at your home airport entirely, since you can't get dirham there anyway, and avoid changing money on the street, which is both illegal and a common way to get shortchanged or handed old notes. Stick to bank ATMs and certified bureaux. And bring a backup card stored separately from your main one; if a machine eats a card or your bank flags a withdrawal, you don't want to be cardless in a cash country.</p>

<h2>Tipping in Morocco</h2>
<p>Tipping is expected but modest. In restaurants, around 10% is normal if a service charge isn't already added. For taxis, round up. Small tips of roughly 5 to 20 dirham are appreciated for guides, porters, and hammam attendants. It adds up to very little and smooths countless small interactions.</p>
<p>The practical upshot is to keep a stash of small notes and coins on you at all times — 5, 10, and 20 dirham denominations especially. ATMs tend to dispense larger bills, and a common frustration is standing in front of a parking attendant or a stall holder with only a 200-dirham note and no way to break it. Early in your trip, break big bills whenever you get the chance: buy something small at a supermarket or cafe that takes cards but pay cash, and bank the change. A pocket of small notes is the quiet secret to smooth days in Morocco.</p>

<h2>Before you fly home: spend it down</h2>
<p>Because you can't take dirham out (beyond that 2,000 MAD limit) and it's hard to exchange abroad, aim to spend your remaining cash before departure — the airport duty-free is a fine place to offload coins. If you have a larger amount left and kept your exchange receipts, you can reconvert dirham to dollars or euros at the airport bureau before you go through passport control.</p>

<h2>One tip that saves the arrival scramble</h2>
<p>Because you'll land without any local cash, it helps to arrive already connected. An <a href="/article/morocco-esim-sim-wifi">eSIM</a> activated before you fly means you have maps and can arrange a ride the moment you touch down, before you've even hit the ATM. It removes the one genuinely awkward moment of a Morocco arrival — needing to get somewhere before you have a single dirham in your pocket.</p>

----- is-morocco-safe -----
TITLE: Is Morocco Safe for American Tourists? An Honest Guide

<p>"Is Morocco safe?" is the question first-time visitors ask more than any other, and the honest answer is: yes, for the vast majority of travelers, Morocco is safe — including for solo travelers and solo women who take normal precautions. Millions of Americans and other tourists visit every year without any serious problem. But "safe" doesn't mean "hassle-free," and knowing the difference is what keeps a first trip relaxed instead of tense.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Busy but relaxed evening scene in Marrakech's Jemaa el-Fnaa square -->

<h2>The honest risk picture</h2>
<p>Violent crime against tourists in Morocco is rare. What you'll actually encounter is petty crime and persistent hustling: pickpocketing in crowded medinas and markets, aggressive touts, overcharging, and the occasional scam. Traffic is a genuine hazard too — roads can be chaotic, and crossing streets in a busy city takes attention. None of this is unusual for a popular travel destination, and none of it should scare you off. It's the background noise of travel here, not a reason to stay home.</p>
<p>For context, the U.S. State Department has placed Morocco at Level 2, "Exercise Increased Caution," primarily citing terrorism — the same level as many popular European countries. Advisory levels change, so check the current one before you travel, but Level 2 is a routine caution, not a warning to avoid the country.</p>

<h2>The scams you'll actually meet</h2>
<p>Most "safety" issues in Morocco are really scams aimed at your wallet, and they follow predictable scripts. Once you know them, they lose their power:</p>
<h3>The "helpful" stranger</h3>
<p>Someone tells you the street ahead is closed, or that the tannery or square you're heading to is shut, and offers to guide you a better way — then demands payment or leads you to a shop where they get a commission. A polite but firm "no thank you" ("la shukran") and walking on handles it.</p>
<h3>Faux guides</h3>
<p>Unofficial guides latch on in the maze-like medinas of Fez and Marrakech, then expect payment. If you want a guide, arrange one through your riad or a reputable tour operator instead.</p>
<h3>The broken taxi meter</h3>
<p>A driver claims the meter is broken to charge a flat, inflated fare. Insist on the meter, or agree the price before you get in.</p>
<h3>Photo demands and henna</h3>
<p>Performers in Jemaa el-Fnaa and henna artists may engage you and then demand money. Agree a price before any photo, and keep your hands to yourself around henna sellers.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: A friendly Moroccan shopkeeper pouring mint tea for visitors -->

<h2>Simple habits that keep you safe</h2>
<p>The precautions here are the same common-sense ones you'd use in any big city, tuned slightly for Morocco:</p>
<p>Keep valuables secure and out of sight in crowds — a money belt or a zipped inner pocket beats a back pocket. Use ATMs inside banks and shield your PIN. Stick to bottled water to avoid an upset stomach. Dress modestly, which draws less attention and shows respect. Learn a couple of Arabic or French phrases; a confident "la shukran" ends most unwanted approaches. And trust your instincts — if a situation feels off, step into a shop or cafe and reset.</p>

<h2>Staying healthy</h2>
<p>The most common thing to actually go wrong on a Morocco trip isn't crime — it's an upset stomach. Stick to bottled or filtered water, including for brushing your teeth if you're sensitive, and be a little cautious with ice and raw salads from unknown kitchens early in your trip. Busy food stalls with high turnover are generally a safer bet than a quiet one where food sits. No vaccines are required to enter from the U.S., though a travel clinic may suggest routine shots plus Hepatitis A and typhoid for food-and-water exposure — worth a conversation before you go. Pack any prescription meds in their original containers, and bring a small kit for the predictable traveler's stomach.</p>

<h2>Getting lost in the medina</h2>
<p>Every first-timer gets turned around in the medinas of Fez and Marrakech — the lanes twist, dead-end, and aren't signed in any way that helps. This is normal and rarely unsafe, just disorienting. Download an offline map before you go, note a couple of major landmarks or gates near your riad, and don't panic if your phone's blue dot spins uselessly between tall walls. If you're truly stuck, a shopkeeper will usually point you the right way; a child may offer to lead you out for a small tip. It's part of the experience, not a danger.</p>

<h2>Is Morocco safe for solo female travelers?</h2>
<p>Yes, with awareness. Solo women travel in Morocco all the time and have wonderful trips, but street harassment — comments, unwanted attention — is more common than in the U.S. and can wear on you. Dressing modestly, walking with purpose, sticking to daytime for medina wandering, and staying in well-reviewed riads all help. Many solo travelers also find that a guided desert tour or a day with a local guide makes the busier cities more comfortable. Our guide on <a href="/article/solo-female-travel-morocco">solo female travel in Morocco</a> goes deeper.</p>

<h2>Emergency numbers to save</h2>
<p>Before you go, save these in your phone. From a mobile, <strong>112</strong> is the universal emergency number. For police in cities, dial <strong>19</strong>; in rural areas, the Gendarmerie is <strong>177</strong>. For an ambulance, call <strong>15</strong>. Major cities also have dedicated tourist police who often speak some English and French.</p>

<h2>The bottom line</h2>
<p>Morocco is a safe, welcoming country that happens to come with a layer of hustle in its tourist hotspots. Go in expecting warm hospitality punctuated by the occasional tout, keep your wits about you the way you would anywhere, and you'll almost certainly come home with nothing worse than a few funny stories about the guy who swore the souk was closed. For the specific scam playbook, see <a href="/article/morocco-scams-to-avoid">common Morocco scams and how to avoid them</a>.</p>

TITLE: Riads Explained: Where to Stay in Morocco and Why

<p>Ask anyone who's been to Morocco where you should stay, and the answer comes back fast: a riad. For a first-timer it's one of the easiest ways to make a trip feel special — but "riad" is a word most Americans arrive not quite understanding. Here's what a riad actually is, why it's worth it, and how to pick one that fits your trip.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Interior courtyard of a traditional Moroccan riad with a central fountain and zellij tilework -->

<h2>What is a riad?</h2>
<p>A riad is a traditional Moroccan house or palace built inward around a central courtyard or garden, often with a fountain at its heart and rooms opening onto the open-air middle. The design turns its back on the busy street outside — thick walls, few exterior windows — and opens up internally into a calm, tiled, plant-filled sanctuary. Many have been lovingly restored and converted into small guesthouses, typically with just a handful of rooms, a rooftop terrace, and a level of personal attention closer to a bed-and-breakfast than a hotel.</p>
<p>The magic is the contrast: you step off a chaotic medina lane through an unremarkable door and into a hushed courtyard with a trickling fountain, intricate zellij tilework, and carved cedar. It's a genuinely Moroccan experience you can't really get anywhere else.</p>

<h2>Why stay in a riad?</h2>
<h3>Location inside the medina</h3>
<p>Most riads sit inside the old medina, which puts you steps from the souks, squares, and sights — exactly where you want to be, and where cars can't go anyway.</p>
<h3>Atmosphere and hospitality</h3>
<p>Small size means personal service: staff who help you plan days, arrange a trusted taxi, or serve mint tea when you arrive. Breakfast on a sunny rooftop terrace overlooking the medina is a standard riad pleasure.</p>
<h3>A calm base in an intense city</h3>
<p>Marrakech and Fez can be gloriously overwhelming. Retreating to the quiet of a riad courtyard at the end of the day is what makes the intensity sustainable.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: Rooftop terrace of a riad set for breakfast overlooking a medina -->

<h2>What to know before you book</h2>
<p>Riads are wonderful, but a few realities are worth setting expectations around:</p>
<p>Because they're tucked into the medina maze, riads can be genuinely hard to find on arrival — good ones will arrange to meet you at a car-accessible point and walk you in, so message ahead. Rooms in historic buildings vary; some are small or dark by design, so read recent reviews and look at actual room photos. Not every riad has an elevator or easy luggage access, which matters if stairs are a concern. And while many riads take cards, some smaller ones prefer cash — check when you book.</p>

<h2>What's usually included</h2>
<p>Most riads include breakfast — often a generous spread of Moroccan breads, pancakes (msemen and beghrir), olives, fresh juice, and mint tea, served on the terrace or in the courtyard. Beyond that, the standout riad extra is service you won't find at a big hotel: staff will book a reliable airport transfer, arrange a trusted local guide, call ahead to a hammam, or even cook a traditional dinner on request with a little notice. Many riads also offer this home-cooked dinner as an option, which is a lovely, low-key first night when you don't want to head back out into the medina. Ask what's on offer when you book — the personal touches are the whole point.</p>

<h2>How much do riads cost?</h2>
<p>Riads span the full range. A simple, well-run guesthouse in the medina can be genuinely affordable, mid-range riads offer beautiful rooms and rooftop terraces for a comfortable nightly rate, and restored-palace riads climb into luxury territory with plunge pools and spa services. Because the best-reviewed small riads have only a few rooms, they book up — reserve early for peak spring and fall dates. For dollar figures across accommodation tiers, see our <a href="/article/morocco-trip-cost">Morocco trip cost guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Riad or hotel?</h2>
<p>You don't have to choose one for the whole trip. A common first-timer approach is to stay in riads in the atmospheric cities — Marrakech, Fez, Chefchaouen — where the experience shines, and opt for a modern hotel where it makes more sense, such as near the airport on a travel day, or in Casablanca. Out in the desert, the equivalent experience is a night in a <a href="/article/sahara-desert-camps">Sahara desert camp</a>, and in the mountains it's a kasbah or guesthouse. Matching your lodging to each place is part of the fun.</p>

<h2>How to book a riad</h2>
<p>Riads across Marrakech, Fez, and beyond are listed on the major booking platforms, which makes it easy to compare courtyards, rooftop terraces, locations, and recent guest reviews in one place. <!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: hotels (Booking) — natural CTA point: comparing/booking riads --> Filter by medina location and read the latest reviews closely — for riads more than most stays, the recent guest experience tells you what you're really getting. Once you've found one you love, message the property before arrival to arrange the medina meet-up, and you'll start your trip the right way: stepping out of the noise and into your own tiled courtyard.</p>

<h2>Choosing where in the medina</h2>
<p>Not all medina locations are equal, and it's worth a moment's thought. A riad deep in the heart of the old city is the most atmospheric but the hardest to reach with luggage; one nearer a main gate (a "bab") or a car-accessible edge is easier to get in and out of, especially on arrival and departure days. Light sleepers should check whether a riad sits near a mosque — the pre-dawn call to prayer carries — or above a busy lane. None of this should scare you off a medina stay; it just helps to read reviews with an eye for the practical, not only the pretty. The best riads strike a balance: immersive enough to feel special, reachable enough that day one doesn't start with a suitcase-dragging odyssey.</p>
