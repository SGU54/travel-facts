# Thailand, Covered. — frame, essays and field sheet

# Essay · There is no best time. There are three, and they disagree.

**scope:** Thailand's two coasts have opposite wet seasons, and the north keeps a third season of its own that no beach calendar mentions.

Every account of Thailand opens with a best time to go. There are three of them, they are each correct, and they govern different parts of the same country — which is why a trip can be planned entirely sensibly and still land in the wrong month.

The national answer is three seasons. Cool and dry from roughly November to February — the peak, and the most reliable weather, which is also why it is the busiest and the priciest. Hot from March to May, genuinely punishing inland, with Songkran in mid-April. Rainy from June to October.
That last one is worth not flinching from. The rain "typically comes in intense bursts, often in the afternoon, leaving plenty of dry hours around them — so a rainy-season trip is far from a washout," and the reporting notes that seasoned travellers actively prefer it for the value and the quiet.
The coastal answer overrides the national one, because the two coasts run opposite calendars. The Andaman side — Phuket, Krabi, Phi Phi, Koh Lanta — is best roughly November to April, with peak monsoon around September and October. The Gulf side — Samui, Phangan, Koh Tao — is often best mid-year, with its wettest stretch around October to December. So there is almost always a coast with good beach weather; the trick is being on it.
The northern answer contradicts both, and it is the one Americans are least often told. The north is at its best November to February — and then, "roughly February to April, agricultural fires cause poor air quality across the north." Nine separate articles in the reporting behind this book say so independently, which is as close to unanimous as this reporting gets about anything.
Put the three calendars on one axis and a popular window turns awkward. A November or December trip that wants Chiang Mai and the Gulf islands has chosen the north's best month and the Gulf's wettest stretch at the same time. Neither half is a mistake on its own. This is the shape of trap the whole book keeps finding, and the reporting states it best about ferries: "The travelers who get caught are rarely the ones who picked the wrong ferry. They are the ones whose island, month and departure arrangements were each individually reasonable."

Mid-afternoon in the wet season. The rain arrives like this, in bursts, and the dry hours sit on either side of it. The street does not stop.


# Essay · The risk is not the one you packed for

**scope:** Visitors arrive braced for crime and unbraced for traffic. The reporting puts it the other way round, and it is not close.

Visitors arrive braced for crime and unbraced for traffic. On the numbers that exist, it is the other way round, and it is not close — which makes this the one page in the book that argues with what the reader already believes.

Start with what is rare. Violent crime against tourists is relatively uncommon, most of the country sits at the lowest US advisory level, and the scams, of which there are many and which are well catalogued, are explicitly "about separating you from your money through deception," not about force. They are predictable enough to be listed, which is the opposite of dangerous.
Now the part that is not rare, and here the figures come from outside this desk. Traffic crashes are the most common cause of non-natural death for American travellers abroad, on the US State Department's own count — ahead of homicide, terrorism and natural disaster. Among destinations, Thailand sits at the top for fatal road crashes per visit, and the State Department's Thailand page names the vehicle: multiple US citizens die each year in crashes involving two- and three-wheeled vehicles. Roughly three-quarters of Thai road deaths are motorbike riders and passengers.
So it is not a risk among risks, and it is produced by the single most casually-made decision on the whole trip: renting a scooter at a counter, with no licence check, on an island with no streetlights.
Read that beside Decision 01 and Decision 05 and the three facts close into one. The reporting's own summary of the road risk names the remedy in the same breath as the licence: "wear a helmet, have a motorcycle license (also an insurance requirement), and go slowly," or simply avoid scooters. Three separate industries, licensing, insurance and whoever paints the road, arrive at the same sentence.
The scams deserve their catalogue, but they fall to habits rather than vigilance. Assume the Grand Palace is open and walk to the official entrance. "Temple closed today" is the oldest one there is, and it ends at a jewellery shop. Insist on the meter or use Grab. Photograph a rented scooter or jet-ski before you take it, and never leave a passport as a deposit. Use only the official site for the arrival card; paid look-alikes exist for a document that is free.
Two more that are neither crime nor traffic, and both are avoidable by knowing them. Bootleg spirits cut with methanol occasionally cause serious harm. Sealed, reputable brands only. And riptides affect some beaches, where the flags and the local answer are the whole of the advice.


# Essay · The fee that does not shrink

**scope:** Thai ATMs generally charge foreign cards a flat local withdrawal fee — flat, not a percentage — so it is the number of withdrawals that costs you, never the amount.

For years the answer was one number: 220 baht, at every Thai bank, on every foreign card. "That figure is still quoted in most guides, and it is no longer reliably what you will see on the screen."

Two things about that fee are structural and will outlast any number. It is flat, so it is the count of withdrawals that costs you and never the amount. And it is now set by card network, so the same machine charges differently depending on which card goes in — with the supermarket-chain ATMs cheaper than the banks. The current schedule is on the Field Sheet, because it is precisely the kind of figure that will be wrong before this book is.
The consequence is easy to miss. If you are carrying both a Visa and a Mastercard, which one you reach for changes the cost of every withdrawal for the whole trip, and nothing about the machine tells you in advance. The fee is displayed on screen before you confirm, so the habit worth building is simply reading that screen instead of clicking through it.
Because the largest charge is flat, taking out 2,000 baht and taking out 20,000 baht incur exactly the same fee. It is the cadence that costs, and the spread across a realistic trip is wider than almost anyone assumes.
Three other charges sit underneath. Your home bank's foreign transaction fee, typically nothing to 3%, which scales with the amount and so pushes gently the other way. The network's exchange margin, built into the rate rather than itemised, which is why even careful people miss it. And dynamic currency conversion — the avoidable one.
At some point the machine offers to charge you in dollars rather than baht. Accepting hands the conversion to the ATM operator "at a rate typically 3–7% worse than the interbank rate your card network would have used. On a large withdrawal that single tap costs more than the flat fee did." The rule has no exceptions: always choose baht. The screen is designed to make accepting look like the safe, familiar option.
There is a ceiling and there is a counterweight. A single withdrawal is capped, and the cap differs by bank, so there is a floor under how few withdrawals a trip can involve — the figures are on the Field Sheet. And cash you are carrying is cash that can be lost or stolen, and a hotel safe is not always available or trustworthy. The arithmetic pushes toward large infrequent withdrawals; sense pushes back. Land in the middle on purpose rather than by drifting.
What sets the cash floor is not the budget but the shape of the spending. Thailand is still largely cash-based for everyday life: street food, markets, small vendors, tuk-tuks, songthaews and most rural businesses want notes. Cards are comfortable at hotels, malls, and chain and upscale restaurants, and unreliable outside them. The local QR system, PromptPay, is everywhere among Thais and generally needs a Thai bank account, so it is not an option for a visitor. A trip weighted toward the first list needs materially more cash in hand than one weighted toward the second, and that, rather than the fee, is what should set how often you visit a machine.
Carrying dollars is the other route and it is worth costing properly. Exchanging cash at a dedicated booth can beat repeated withdrawals for larger sums once the flat fee is counted, and airport counters are consistently worse than city booths. So the airport is the place to change the minimum you need to get into town, not the trip's cash. Two small mechanics travel with it: bring crisp, undamaged bills, because torn or marked notes are sometimes refused, and keep a mix of denominations. Breaking a 1,000-baht note at a food stall is its own small problem.
Which is why this sits in a book about routing rather than a book about budgets. "Withdrawal cadence is not really a money decision. It is a routing decision." People make twenty small withdrawals "not because they miscounted the fee, but because their itinerary never let them plan further ahead than the next two days." Decision 03 is what determines whether there is a bank ATM within reach at all.

Priced by hand, paid in cash. Stalls, markets and songthaews are what set how often you stand at a machine, not the size of your budget.


# Essay · The fine is capped. The ban is not.

**scope:** Thailand's overstay rules price the act rather than the wallet, and the date that governs is the one an officer writes on your stamp.

This is not a decision and it is not presented as one. It is a set of deadlines handed to you at a desk, and the only choice inside it, how close to the stamped date to plan, has a one-word answer.

The fine is 500 baht per day, capped at 20,000. The cap is reached at 40 days and the fine stops accumulating there. It is paid at the immigration counter on departure; officers calculate the days, collect, issue a receipt and then stamp you out.
One mercy is rarely mentioned. For an overstay of a few hours caused by a flight delay or a schedule change, the fine is usually waived if you explain the situation at departure. The Royal Thai Embassy states this directly. "A missed connection that pushes you past midnight is not the scenario the penalty structure is aimed at." Read that next to Decision 04, which is about exactly which missed connections are yours to own.
The ban is the part that scales, and the table opposite is the whole shape of it: the money flat-lines at 40 days while the consequence keeps climbing for years. Being caught rather than presenting yourself carries substantially harsher consequences: detention at an immigration facility before deportation, and a longer ban, while the cash fine is identical either way.
As this edition goes to press the number itself has just moved, which is the best possible illustration of why it is not a decision. The 60-day exemption introduced in 2024 was rolled back on 15 September 2026: most nationalities including Americans now receive 30 days on arrival, extendable once by up to 30 more at a Thai immigration office inside the country, for roughly 60 days maximum per entry. A second new clause limits visa-exempt entries by land border to two per calendar year; arrivals by air and sea are not limited, so it does not touch a normal fly-in holiday.
What that change did not touch is everything else on this page. The fine, the cap, the ban ladder, the arrival card, the onward-ticket requirement and the funds rule all stand exactly as written. That is the shape of this whole category: one volatile number wrapped in a structure that does not move, which is why the number belongs on the Field Sheet and the structure belongs in the book.
And the date that governs is still the "admitted until" date on the entry stamp. Not the exemption length in the abstract, not what an airline or a booking site said, not a calculation from the arrival date. Officers write it by hand or stamp it, and it is worth reading before you leave the desk.
Two requirements are enforced in places travellers do not expect. Proof of onward or return travel is usually enforced by the airline at check-in rather than by Thai immigration on arrival — "the requirement is most likely to bite thousands of miles before Thailand," at your own departure airport, with no Thai official involved. And a funds requirement exists in the regulations, commonly cited as 20,000 baht per person or 40,000 per family, that is not routinely checked: the honest description is "a rule that exists and is enforced inconsistently."
Finally, the arrival card. The Thailand Digital Arrival Card replaced the paper TM6 in May 2025. It is mandatory for foreign nationals, free, and filed online before arrival at the official immigration site. The filing window has changed since introduction, so the current timing is worth taking from the official site rather than from any guide, including this one.


# Essay · The north is a different trip, and this book hands it back

**scope:** Around 5,000 words of the reporting behind this book are about Chiang Mai and the mountains. None of it produced a decision, and the honest thing is to say why rather than to manufacture one.

A decision, in this book, is a choice that is hard to reverse once you are there and that fails together with other choices. The north is neither, and padding it into a ninth chapter is precisely what the previous edition did with entry requirements.

What the reporting does support is a clear and unglamorous shape. Three to four days for a first visit: the Old City temples, the mountain temple Doi Suthep, a cooking class, and a full day at an observation-only elephant sanctuary. An hour and a quarter from Bangkok by air, or the sleeper overnight. Songthaews run fixed routes for a small flat fare, Grab works, and the old city is walkable.
None of that is hard to reverse. You can decide the whole of it from a seat on the plane, and nothing else in the trip breaks if you change your mind. Its one genuine constraint is seasonal, and it is not in this chapter. The burning season belongs to the calendar, and the calendar is at the front of the book where it can govern the decisions that follow it.
One caution the reporting does supply belongs with Decision 01 rather than here. The mountain roads out to Chiang Rai and Pai are long and winding, and most travellers book an organised tour or a private driver rather than self-driving. The Pai road "has over 700 curves and can induce motion sickness."
And if the north is the trip rather than an addition to it, the reporting supplies its own seven-day shape: two days in Bangkok, three in Chiang Mai, one for a side trip (Chiang Rai at about three hours each way, Pai, or Doi Inthanon) and a flight back to Bangkok for the departure. That is a coherent week and it is a different book from this one.


# Essay · What this book refused, and why

**scope:** Ninety-seven articles and 83,533 words of reporting stand behind these pages. A good deal of it did not become one, and a book that cannot say what it left out is asking to be trusted on faith.

Every refusal below was decided before a word of the chapter was written, which is the only point at which such a decision is honest. Afterwards it becomes a justification.

The pattern is consistent. Material was refused when it was not a choice: a deadline handed to you, a season you cannot move. Or when the reporting behind it would not carry the weight a chapter puts on it. Nothing was refused for being inconvenient to the argument.
Three of these refusals became essays rather than disappearing, because the reporting is good and the reader still needs it. The difference is that an essay does not pretend to a verdict. Two of them, prices and anything with a filing window, went to the Field Sheet on the next page, which is where a fact with a shelf-life belongs.

# Eight decisions, in the order they get made

This is not a guidebook and it does not describe Thailand. It takes the eight choices that are hard to reverse once you are there, and works each one out to a call you can disagree with.

Each one opens with the call and the exception to it, in that order. The argument, the comparison and the reversal follow. If you read nothing else on a page, read the box at the top — it is the whole answer, and the rest is there so you can overrule it on purpose.
The table on each decision compares like with like on one stated measure, and the row that changes the choice is named in words rather than marked in colour. Where the reporting does not support a column — a price, a ranking — the column is absent rather than estimated.
This book is built from 97 articles published on thailandforamericans.com — the same desk, not an outside authority. Sentences in quotation marks are verbatim from those articles, and the ledger at thailandforamericans.com/sources lists every one, so any or claim can be traced to the page it came from. Where a claim rests on something other than that reporting, the source is named in the line itself.
It names no hotel, no restaurant, no stall, no elephant venue and no insurer. It does not rank the islands, because the reporting behind it describes them and does not score them. Anything that moves faster than a printed page is marked as such and sent to the Field Sheet at the back.
