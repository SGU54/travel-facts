# Thailand, Covered. — the decisions

# Decision 01 · Ride — What may you actually drive, and what are you insured on?

**scope:** You intend to move around an island under your own steam.

Rent the scooter only if your home licence carries a motorcycle endorsement. Not because the shop will ask, and it usually will not, but because the endorsement is the condition your travel policy attaches to its motorbike exclusion. Without it you are riding uninsured on the islands that have a clinic rather than a hospital.

The exception that matters: on Phuket, Samui and Ao Nang the alternative exists — Grab runs, and songthaews run to a pattern. On Koh Tao, Koh Phangan, Koh Lanta and Koh Chang it does not, and the ride you decline has to be replaced with a fixed-rate taxi you arrange each time.

Every account of Thailand treats the scooter as a question of nerve. It is not. It is a question of entitlement, and the answer was settled before the trip, in a licence office at home.

Driving a car in Thailand requires a valid home driving licence plus an International Driving Permit issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention. In the United States, AAA and AATA are the only authorised issuers, at around $20, same-day in person. That much is widely repeated and correct.
What is almost never said beside it: the permit translates the entitlement you already hold; it does not add one. A car needs the ordinary licence an American already has. A motorbike needs a motorcycle endorsement on that licence, which most Americans do not have and which an International Driving Permit does not confer.
That would be a paperwork footnote if the insurance side did not close on it. Motorbike riding is commonly excluded from travel policies or requires a rider, and the motorbike exclusion carries a further condition attached: a valid motorcycle licence. The two facts are written in different documents, by different industries, and they meet on an island road.
There are two costs here and conflating them is what leads people astray. Police checkpoints are common in tourist areas, and riding without the right permit or without a helmet typically means a fine of a few hundred baht, commonly in the region of 400 to 500. Travellers who have been stopped once reasonably conclude the whole issue is overblown. The fine is not the risk. Where the exclusion applies, the insurer can deny the claim in full. Not reduce it: deny it. A serious motorbike injury requiring surgery runs into the tens of thousands of dollars at a Thai private hospital, and evacuation is the line item on top of that.
It is worth being exact about the rental counter, because the transaction feels like approval and carries none. Vendors rarely check licence details with any care, and plenty will hand over a bike against a passport photocopy and a cash deposit. The vendor not checking is not permission. It is simply the reason most people discover the problem after a crash rather than before one. Two practical corollaries: the helmet is legally required for rider and passenger and is frequently a condition of the coverage itself, and the deposit is a photocopy or cash. Never the passport itself, which in someone else's drawer becomes a bargaining chip in any dispute about damage.
The road is the third fact. Street lighting is poor to absent outside Bangkok and the main urban areas — island roads, mountain roads and rural stretches are largely unlit after dark, and most island roads have no footpaths at all. This is the most consistent finding across every region surveyed, and it applies equally to a car, a scooter and someone walking home from dinner.

**The call.** Count the endorsement before you count the fare. A day of songthaew hops is friction; a day of riding without the entitlement your policy names is a different category of exposure, and it is at its worst precisely where the scooter is most tempting: the small islands, where the alternative is thinnest, the road is unlit and the clinic cannot treat what the road produces.

No footpath, no lighting column, and the carriageway runs straight into the verge. This is the most consistent single finding in the reporting, and it applies equally to a car, a scooter and a walk back from dinner.


# Decision 02 · Sequence — How many islands actually fit, and in what order?

**scope:** You are choosing how many places to sleep in.

Two places in a week, three in a fortnight, and one coast whichever it is. Not because more is unpleasant, but because a transfer costs half a day or more at each end and a coast change costs a return to the mainland. Those are days taken out of the middle of a trip that is already short at both ends.

The exception is not an island, it is a category. An un-serviced island is not one transfer: it is three legs each way, each with its own timetable and most often booked separately from the others. Added to a seven-night trip it does not cost half a day; it costs the whole recovery margin.

The reporting is unanimous on the shape of the mistake: "each island transfer eats half a day or more," and the checklist trip is the one that loses. The question is not how many islands you can reach. It is how many nights survive the reaching.

The subtraction starts before Thailand does. There are no nonstop flights from the United States, you arrive eleven or twelve hours out of sync with the East Coast, and the first day is an arrival-and-recovery day, so the realistic time on the ground is a day or two less than the trip length. A seven-night trip is a five- or six-night trip, and it is the middle that the transfers then come out of.
Then each move takes its own cut. Bangkok to Phuket or Krabi is about an hour and twenty-five minutes in the air and roughly half a day door to door once airport travel at both ends and the thirty-to-sixty-minute run from the island airport are counted. That is why the one-week shape the reporting names is three nights in the city and four on one southern island. Not a compromise, the honest arithmetic.
The coast is a boundary rather than a preference. The Andaman side runs roughly November to April and the Gulf mid-year, and "crossing between the two coasts mid-trip means returning to the mainland and a long transfer." A plan with one island on each coast has bought a backtrack in order to visit two places, one of which is in its wet season.
And the four change the unit of measurement entirely. A journey to Koh Tao is "a flight, a road transfer, and a crossing — three legs, each with its own timetable, most often booked separately from one another." Three legs out, three back, inside a week, from eleven time zones away.

**The call.** Count moves, not islands. Every plan in the table reaches the sea; they differ in how much of the week is spent getting there and how much margin is left when a crossing is suspended. On a first trip the second island is the most expensive thing on the itinerary, and it is the item nobody prices.

The fare is the smallest part of the cost. The half-day at each end is the rest of it, and a second island buys two more of them.


# Decision 03 · Base — Which island — and which side of the line?

**scope:** The trip includes an island at all.

Decide which side of the line your trip can afford to be on, then choose freely inside it. The line is not size, price or beauty. It is whether the island has an airstrip and a hospital, or a clinic and a boat.

The reporting supplies both counterexamples itself, and they are worth holding. Koh Tao has hyperbaric chambers where larger islands may not. "The facilities follow the diving, not the population." And Samui's airport is privately operated by Bangkok Airways, so the island with the best air access has the fewest alternatives when that access fails.

Four separately observed measures — air access, medical capability, how many booked legs a journey takes, and what moves you around once you are there — draw the same line through the same seven places. That is why this is a threshold and not a theme.

The reporting reaches it from three directions and notices. From the air side: "These are the same four islands that have clinics rather than hospitals, and Koh Tao and Koh Phangan are among the crossings most frequently suspended in rough weather." From the medical side: "The place where a transfer is most likely to be needed is the place where it is slowest to arrange."
On the ground the difference is structural rather than a matter of degree. Koh Samui and Phuket have full private hospitals capable of handling serious cases without a transfer. Koh Phangan, Koh Tao, Koh Lanta and Koh Chang have clinics: they handle ordinary problems and stabilise serious ones. "On Samui a serious injury is treated where it happens. On Koh Tao it is stabilised and moved."
The daily-movement picture is a separate measurement again, and it does not follow size. "A large island with a concentrated centre can involve less daily movement than a small island with scattered bays." Koh Tao is under eight kilometres long and asks more of you than its size suggests; Ao Nang is on the mainland and asks almost nothing.
One consequence of the movement picture deserves stating here rather than in Decision 01, because it is settled at this moment and not at that one. Whether you will end up riding anything is decided by where you sleep. Islands and towns whose beaches, viewpoints and restaurants are spread out are the ones where nearly everyone rents something, whatever they intended when they booked; places with a walkable centre, or cheap and frequent songthaews, are the ones where a scooter never comes up. By the time the question is live, the licence endorsement is no longer obtainable.
So the figure below is not a ranking, and this book will not produce one. The reporting describes these places; it does not score them. What the table does is put the four axes in one place, so a trip can be matched to the side of the line it is equipped to be on.

**The call.** The island is not chosen from a photograph; it is chosen from the left-hand columns. Airstrip and medical tier are fixed, knowable while the itinerary is being built, and unknowable at the moment they become relevant. Everything in the right-hand column can be arranged on arrival.

Nothing in this frame is a runway, and nothing in it is a hospital. That is the whole of Decision 03: the island is chosen from the left-hand columns of FIG. 1, not from the photograph.


# Decision 04 · Transit — How do the legs connect, and who owns the failure?

**scope:** Any trip with a domestic leg, which is nearly every trip.

Put the domestic leg on the same ticket as the long-haul, or accept in advance that you own every failure on it. Separate bookings are separate contracts: the onward carrier owes you nothing for a connection missed because the inbound ticket failed, and the bag is not through-checked. Plan as though the replacement ticket is yours to buy.

When the two ends genuinely cannot go on one ticket, put the slack there instead. An overnight in Bangkok rather than a same-day connection; the earlier crossing rather than the later one, because "if a day's sailings are going to be cancelled, the later ones are likelier to go first."

Most travellers assume an invisible safety net spans the legs of one trip, because the legs feel like one trip. It does not exist, and the place it most often fails is a fifty-kilometre gap in the middle of Bangkok.

Bangkok has two airports about fifty kilometres apart, and travellers routinely book an international arrival at one and a domestic departure from the other without noticing. Suvarnabhumi takes full-service and most international carriers; Don Mueang takes primarily low-cost carriers and a large share of the domestic network. "They are separate airports roughly 50 km apart by road, not two terminals of the same place."
None of the connection happens airside. You clear immigration, reclaim checked baggage, exit to landside, cross fifty kilometres of Bangkok, and check in again from scratch: "a complete re-entry into the system, not a transfer within one." The road leg runs forty-five to sixty minutes off-peak and ninety minutes to two and a half hours in traffic, and the hour is set by when your inbound lands rather than by anything you choose.
For a connection between the two airports, no official minimum connection time is published at all. That absence is itself the fact: the figures circulating in guides and forums cite no airport or airline standard. What exists instead is the arithmetic above. The free inter-airport shuttle is real but narrow. It runs for transferring passengers who can show an onward boarding pass and a passport, and the airport authority states that non-connecting passengers are not eligible, so a night in the city between two flights pays its own way in both directions.
The same structure decides the small things. Bags are through-checked to the final destination on a single ticket and between codeshare partners, and not across separate bookings, which is the mechanical reason each step has to be repeated. And a carry-on-only low-cost fare and a transpacific suitcase are not compatible; the add-on bought at the airport costs more than the same add-on bought at booking.

**The call.** The question is never which leg is fastest. It is which leg, if it fails, you have to buy twice. Ask it once, at booking, while both tickets are still hypothetical. It is the only moment at which the answer is still free to change.

Baggage Claim 4 at Suvarnabhumi, with Udon Thani and Chiang Mai on the board — the second of the five steps. Between two separately booked tickets it happens in full, and then again at the far end of fifty kilometres of road.


# Decision 05 · Insure — What are you exposed to, and what does a policy actually answer?

**scope:** You are going to an island with clinic-level care.

Buy the policy for the evacuation line rather than the medical line. In an emergency, get treated first: nothing on this page is a reason to delay care. Whenever circumstances allow — and for anything that is not an emergency they usually do — call the assistance line before the billing is arranged. That call is what decides whether the hospital bills your insurer or bills you.

The distinction the industry blurs and the reporting does not: "evacuation and repatriation are separate benefits." A policy with evacuation and no repatriation "will get someone from Koh Tao to a Bangkok hospital" — and stop there.

A travel policy is four or five separate coverages sold as one product, and the gap between what it covers and what a traveller assumes it covers "usually sits in the definitions rather than in the fine print."

Start with the mechanic, because it decides more than any coverage limit. Thai private hospitals commonly require payment, or a guarantee of payment, before treating a foreign patient. Direct billing happens when the insurer issues a guarantee of payment to that specific hospital: a document, arranged through the assistance line, not something the hospital produces on request.
The consequence is blunt: "calling after treatment turns a potential direct-billing case into a reimbursement case." Under reimbursement someone needs the funds available at the moment of the emergency, potentially a large sum, on a card, in a country far from home. The difference is not administrative.
The scale is what makes it a decision rather than a formality. Medical evacuation from a Thai island runs into the tens of thousands of US dollars, with a dedicated air ambulance at the higher end and a commercial flight with a medical escort at the lower. And American domestic health insurance generally provides little or no coverage abroad and almost never covers evacuation at all.
One practical detail the reporting insists on and no policy schedule mentions. The insurer's assistance line coordinates the evacuation. Not the patient, not the treating clinic, not a family member calling from home. So the number that matters is the one printed on the policy, and "it is worth having it somewhere other than an email inbox on a phone that may be in a bag on a boat."

**The call.** Two questions to the insurer, before buying: do you direct-bill in Thailand, and does this policy include repatriation as well as evacuation? Neither answer usually appears in the schedule, and both of them change what happens on the worst day of the trip.

Where the street looks like this, the clinic is a first response and the hospital is somewhere else. What the policy is really buying is the journey between them.


# Decision 06 · City — Bangkok: where do you sleep, and how do you cross it?

**scope:** You are spending nights in Bangkok rather than transiting through it.

Choose the station first and the neighbourhood second. "Staying near a BTS or MRT station is the best transport decision you can make in Bangkok." An otherwise lovely hotel a sweaty twenty-minute walk from one "will quietly cost you hours over a few days."

The exception is the Old Town, and it is a real one. The Grand Palace, Wat Pho and Wat Arun are precisely the sights the trains do not directly serve. They are served by the river instead, which is also a line, also cheap, and also immune to the traffic.

Bangkok is this book's spine inverted. It is the place where every system works, which is exactly why arriving here tells you nothing about the rest of the trip, and why here, uniquely, the decision is measured in minutes rather than in exposure.

That makes one instrument sufficient for the whole comparison: which line the district sits on, and how far you walk to reach it. The BTS Skytrain and the MRT metro are "clean, fast, cheap, air-conditioned," and, crucially, they fly over or under the traffic." Every district below is compared on that and nothing else, because everything else about them is preference.
The river counts as a line. The Chao Phraya Express and the cross-river ferries are "the smart way to reach the historic temple area, which the trains don't directly serve," connecting from the BTS at Saphan Taksin. Orange-flag commuter boats are the cheapest and most frequent; the blue-flag tourist boat costs more and stops at the main riverside sights.
One more line runs where the trains do not, and it is worth naming as a line rather than as a boat trip. The canal boats on Khlong Saen Saep are "a fast, local way to cut across the city center, if a bit chaotic," and motorbike taxis, the riders in coloured vests, exist for exactly the short hop that a jam makes impossible. Neither is a sightseeing choice; both are the answer to a specific ten minutes.
The buses are the honest omission: very cheap, and "slow, hot, and hard to navigate without local knowledge." Most visitors skip them. One caution on the stored-value cards, because it is the commonest wrong assumption about this city: there is no single card for both networks. The BTS takes one and the MRT another, so a day using both means carrying two or buying singles at the gate.
The road is the failure mode, and it is predictable. The city gridlocks roughly 7–9am and 4–7pm, and "if you must cross town by road at peak time, allow far more time than the distance suggests." Tuk-tuks are not cheaper than a metered taxi or a Grab, they require hard negotiation, and some drivers run detours to a showroom that pays them a commission. A novelty rather than transport.

**The call.** If you want the lowest-friction first choice, book near Asok and stop deciding. If temples and the river matter more than malls and nightlife, take the Old Town and accept the commute deliberately. It is a real cost, paid for a real thing, which is the only kind of trade this book endorses.

The viaduct is the argument for the district: it goes over the traffic rather than through it. Choose the station first and the neighbourhood second.


# Decision 07 · Culture — The temple door, and the elephant question

**scope:** You will enter a temple, and you are considering an animal attraction.

Carry a scarf or a sarong, and use riding as the first filter on an elephant venue. One instantly covers shoulders or wraps as a skirt; the other ends the search early: if a venue offers rides, stop there. Passing that test is necessary, not sufficient — the table names what else to read.

On the dress code there is no exception, which is the point. Shoulders and knees covered applies to men and women alike, it is actively checked at the entrance of the major sites, and visitors who do not meet it are turned away.

Two culture questions on a Thailand trip have an actual answer rather than a range of opinions. Both are decided before you arrive at the door, and both are cheap to get right.

The temple rule is simple and enforced: cover the shoulders and cover the knees. "At the most visited sites, staff actively check visitors at the entrance and will turn away anyone who doesn't meet the code." Shoes come off before entering temple buildings, not just the grounds, along with hats and sunglasses; socks are usually fine. The shoes-off custom extends to guesthouses, homes, some shops and certain restaurants: "if you see a pile of shoes at the entrance, that's your cue to add yours to it."
Two additions for women. Clothing should not be see-through or very tight, and, the one that catches visitors out, a woman should never touch a monk or hand anything directly to one. Place the item down for him to pick up, or pass it via a man. Major sites including the Grand Palace often lend or rent cover-ups, but not everywhere does, and it can mean a queue or a deposit.
The elephant question is a decision rather than a sentiment, because the reporting supplies a single test that anyone can apply. "Whether a place offers elephant rides is the single most reliable test of its ethics… A venue that has chosen not to offer rides has usually rethought its entire relationship with the animals; one that still does, whatever it calls itself, has not." Use it before looking at anything else.
The complication is real and worth stating rather than smoothing away. Logging was largely banned in 1989, leaving thousands of captive elephants and their mahouts without work, and tourism became the alternative livelihood. Captive elephants cannot simply be released. There is not enough protected habitat, and many have known only human care. "The goal is supporting genuinely welfare-focused operations, not demanding an impossible purity."

**The call.** Apply the riding test first. If a venue passes it, read the table above for the rest. It is a filter you can run yourself on any operator, in about thirty seconds, without trusting a review or a certification, which is exactly why this book carries it instead of a list of venues that will be out of date before you go.

At the busiest sites this door is staffed, and visitors with bare shoulders or knees are turned away. A scarf in the day bag settles it before you arrive.


# Decision 08 · Eat — Ordering well, without a reservation system you can use

**scope:** Every trip.

Eat where the queue is, and order at the heat you can actually take. A stall busy with locals means fast turnover, and fast turnover is simultaneously the quality test and the safety test. The queue is the first inspection; the rest of this page is the second.

The caveat on heat, stated by the reporting rather than softened: "even 'a little spicy' by Thai standards can be hot for many Westerners," and some vendors will make a dish milder for an obvious tourist than for a local. The phrase is a starting point, not a guarantee.

Street food in Thailand is not merely the cheap option. "Vendors often specialize in a single dish perfected over years or generations, and locals eat from stalls daily." It is frequently the best food in the country, sold from a cart.

The ordering mechanics take a minute to learn and work everywhere. Point at what you want, or at what someone else is eating; hold up fingers for quantity; carry three words — mai phet (not spicy), phet nit noi (a little spicy), aroi (delicious, and always appreciated). Have small notes ready, because stalls do not take cards.
The safety habits and the quality habits are the same habits, which is what makes the rule easy to keep. Choose busy stalls with high turnover; eat what is cooked fresh and hot in front of you; be cautious with raw items, pre-cut fruit that has been sitting out, and uncooked shellfish; drink bottled water, though ice in established places is generally fine. Give the stomach a day or two to adjust.
Dietary needs are workable with the right word rather than the right sentence. Fish sauce and shrimp paste are common hidden ingredients, so jay signals strict vegetarian or vegan — look for the yellow-and-red symbol — and mang-sa-wirat signals vegetarian. For an allergy, carry a translation card and accept that cross-contamination at a busy stall cannot be controlled.
And the food changes as the trip moves, which is an argument for eating regionally rather than searching for the same dish twice. Khao soi and sai ua in the north; som tum, laab and gai yang in Isan; bolder curries and seafood in the south; and in Bangkok, Yaowarat in Chinatown.

**The call.** Follow the crowd, start at mai phet, and work up. Most people's tolerance grows over a trip — what is punishing on day one is often pleasant by the end of a fortnight. And if a dish catches you out, reach for rice rather than water: water spreads the chili oil around.

The queue is the inspection. Busy means fast turnover, and fast turnover is the first inspection you can make from the pavement.
