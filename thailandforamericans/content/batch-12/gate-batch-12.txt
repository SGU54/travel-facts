TITLE: Should You Take a Thai Cooking Class?
CATEGORY: street-food-markets

<p>A Thai cooking class is one of the most rewarding and fun activities you can do in Thailand — and one of the few souvenirs you bring home in your own skills. You'll learn to make dishes you've been eating all trip, usually starting with a market tour, and come away able to recreate real Thai food in your own kitchen. Here's why it's worth it and how to choose a good one.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: A Thai cooking class with woks, fresh ingredients, and students -->

<h2>Why take a cooking class?</h2>
<p>Beyond being genuinely enjoyable, a cooking class deepens your whole trip. You learn <strong>what's actually in the dishes</strong> you've been eating, demystify Thai ingredients and techniques, and gain a lasting skill. It's social, hands-on, and suits all ages and abilities. For food lovers it's often a trip highlight, and even reluctant cooks tend to leave delighted — plus you eat everything you make, so it doubles as a great meal.</p>

<h2>What to expect</h2>
<p>A typical class runs a half or full day and follows a familiar shape. Many start with a <strong>guided market visit</strong>, where the instructor introduces Thai vegetables, herbs, pastes, and ingredients. Back at the kitchen, you'll <strong>cook several dishes yourself</strong> — commonly a curry (often making the paste from scratch in a mortar and pestle), a stir-fry like pad thai or pad krapow, a soup such as tom yum, a salad like som tum, and a dessert like mango sticky rice. You eat each dish as you go, and usually take home a recipe booklet.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: getyourguide — Thai cooking class -->

<h2>Where to take one</h2>
<p>You can take a cooking class almost anywhere tourists go, but a few places stand out. <strong>Chiang Mai</strong> is often considered the best — it has a huge number of excellent, well-organized classes, many at scenic farm settings outside the city. <strong>Bangkok</strong> offers everything from market-tour classes to upscale culinary experiences. The <strong>islands</strong> (Phuket, Krabi, Koh Samui) have classes too, often with a beachy, resort feel. Wherever you are, you'll find an option.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: Fresh Thai herbs, chilies, and curry paste ingredients -->

<h2>How to choose a good class</h2>
<p>Look for a class that <strong>includes a market tour</strong> (it adds a lot), has <strong>good recent reviews</strong>, offers a <strong>menu you're excited about</strong> (some let you choose your dishes), and keeps <strong>group sizes reasonable</strong> so you get hands-on time. Check whether it accommodates <strong>vegetarian or dietary needs</strong> if relevant. Farm-based classes outside the cities often include a lovely setting and a garden tour; city classes are more convenient. Both are great — pick based on your schedule and what you want to cook.</p>

<h2>Is it worth it?</h2>
<p>For most travelers, absolutely. It's affordable, fun, educational, and gives you a skill and memories that outlast the trip — far more lasting than a typical souvenir. Unless you have no interest in cooking whatsoever, a Thai cooking class is one of the easiest activities to recommend, and a highlight of many people's time in the country. TITLE: What to Wear to Thai Temples (and Other Dress Rules)
CATEGORY: temples-culture

<p>Thailand's temples (wats) are active places of worship with a real dress code, and being turned away from a major sight like the Grand Palace because of your outfit is a common, avoidable disappointment. The rules are simple once you know them. Here's exactly what to wear to Thai temples, plus general dress guidance for your trip.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Visitors appropriately dressed at a Thai temple -->

<h2>The basic temple dress code</h2>
<p>The core rule for everyone is to <strong>cover your shoulders and your knees</strong>. That means:</p>
<p><strong>No bare shoulders</strong> — avoid tank tops, sleeveless shirts, and spaghetti straps. Sleeves (even short ones) or a covering layer are needed.</p>
<p><strong>No exposed knees</strong> — shorts above the knee, short skirts, and short dresses aren't acceptable. Long trousers, long skirts, or below-the-knee shorts work.</p>
<p>This applies to <strong>both men and women</strong>. It's about respect for a sacred space, not fashion, and it's enforced — especially at major temples.</p>

<h2>What you'll need to remove</h2>
<p>Before entering temple <strong>buildings</strong> (not just the grounds), you'll need to <strong>remove your shoes</strong> — so wear footwear that's easy to slip on and off, like sandals. Hats and sunglasses should also come off inside temple buildings. Socks are usually fine to keep on.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: Shoes left outside a temple entrance -->

<h2>Extra notes for women</h2>
<p>The shoulders-and-knees rule is the main thing for women too, but a couple of extras: clothing shouldn't be <strong>see-through or too tight</strong>, and at the most sacred sites you may want to err conservative. Importantly, <strong>women should never touch a monk</strong> or hand anything directly to one — place the item down for him to pick up, or pass it via a man. A <strong>scarf or sarong</strong> is the single most useful item to carry: it instantly covers shoulders or wraps as a skirt.</p>

<h2>What if you're not dressed right?</h2>
<p>Many major temples, including the Grand Palace, offer <strong>sarongs or cover-ups to rent or borrow</strong> at the entrance if you turn up underdressed — but this isn't guaranteed everywhere, can involve a queue or a deposit, and is best avoided. The easy solution is to <strong>carry a lightweight scarf or sarong</strong> in your bag whenever temples might be on the agenda, so you're always covered (literally).</p>

<h2>General dress tips for Thailand</h2>
<p>Outside temples, Thailand is relaxed but it helps to read the setting. <strong>Beachwear stays at the beach</strong> — cover up when walking through towns or to restaurants. In <strong>cities and rural areas</strong>, modest, lightweight clothing is respectful and practical in the heat. <strong>Some rooftop bars and upscale venues</strong> enforce smart-casual dress codes (no shorts, vests, or flip-flops). And lightweight, breathable fabrics that cover up also protect you from sun and mosquitoes. Dressing with a little awareness goes a long way in Thailand. TITLE: The Best Time to Visit Thailand
CATEGORY: when-to-go

<p>Thailand is a year-round destination, but <em>when</em> you go shapes your experience — the weather, the crowds, the prices, and even which part of the country is at its best. The country has three broad seasons, and crucially, its two coasts have different weather patterns. Here's how to choose the best time for your trip.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: A sunny Thai beach or temple under clear blue skies -->

<h2>The three seasons</h2>
<p>For most of Thailand, the year breaks into three seasons:</p>
<p><strong>Cool and dry (roughly November to February):</strong> the peak and generally best season — pleasant temperatures, low humidity, little rain, and sunshine. It's the most reliable weather and also the busiest and priciest time.</p>
<p><strong>Hot (roughly March to May):</strong> very hot and humid, building toward the rains, with temperatures that can be genuinely punishing, especially inland. Songkran (Thai New Year) in mid-April is a highlight of this season.</p>
<p><strong>Rainy/monsoon (roughly June to October):</strong> the wet season, with downpours that are often short and heavy rather than all-day. Lush landscapes, fewer crowds, and lower prices are the upside.</p>

<h2>The best months overall</h2>
<p>For the most reliable weather across the country, <strong>November through February</strong> is the sweet spot — cool, dry, and sunny. This is peak season, so expect higher prices and more crowds, and book ahead. December and January in particular are prime time. If you want great weather and can travel then, this is the safest bet nationally.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: A lush green Thai landscape, suggesting the rainy season's upside -->

<h2>The two coasts have different seasons</h2>
<p>This is the key nuance for a beach trip. Thailand's <strong>two coasts have opposite rainy seasons</strong>:</p>
<p>The <strong>Andaman coast</strong> (Phuket, Krabi, Phi Phi) is best roughly <strong>November to April</strong>, with peak monsoon around September–October.</p>
<p>The <strong>Gulf coast</strong> (Koh Samui, Koh Phangan, Koh Tao) runs differently — often best <strong>mid-year</strong>, with its wettest stretch around October to December.</p>
<p>So there's almost always a coast with good beach weather. If you're traveling in, say, July when the Andaman coast can be wet, head to the Gulf islands instead.</p>

<h2>The north's timing</h2>
<p>Northern Thailand (Chiang Mai) is best in the <strong>cool season (November to February)</strong>, when it's pleasant and clear — and can be genuinely chilly in the evenings. Crucially, <strong>avoid the burning season</strong> (roughly February to April), when agricultural fires cause poor air quality across the north. The lantern festivals (Yi Peng, Loy Krathong) usually fall in November, a beautiful but busy time.</p>

<h2>Balancing weather, crowds, and prices</h2>
<p>The trade-offs: <strong>peak season</strong> (Nov–Feb) gives the best weather but the highest prices and biggest crowds; <strong>shoulder months</strong> offer a good balance of decent weather and lower prices; and <strong>low/rainy season</strong> brings the cheapest prices, fewest crowds, and lush scenery, with the trade-off of rain (often manageable). TITLE: Thailand for Americans: The Complete First-Timer's Guide
CATEGORY: where-to-go

<p>Thailand is one of the world's most rewarding first big international trips for Americans: it's affordable, welcoming, endlessly varied, and easier to travel than first-timers expect. From Bangkok's temples and street food to the northern mountains and the southern islands, it packs an incredible range into one country. This guide pulls together everything you need to plan a first trip.</p>

<p><strong>Note:</strong> entry rules, advisories, and prices change. Verify current requirements with official sources before traveling.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: An iconic, welcoming Thailand scene — temple and skyline or island beach -->

<h2>Entry: what Americans need</h2>
<p>For tourism, U.S. passport holders enter Thailand <strong>visa-free</strong> under the visa-exemption scheme — no visa arranged in advance. You must complete the mandatory, free <strong>Thailand Digital Arrival Card (TDAC)</strong> online before arriving (use only the official site), and your passport should be valid at least six months out. The permitted length of visa-exempt stay has been changing in 2026, so confirm the current allowance on official sites near your travel date — a normal vacation fits comfortably within it.</p>

<h2>When to go</h2>
<p>The best overall weather is the <strong>cool, dry season (November to February)</strong> — also peak season. Remember the two coasts differ: the <strong>Andaman</strong> islands (Phuket, Krabi) are best November–April, the <strong>Gulf</strong> islands (Koh Samui) best mid-year. The north is best November–February; avoid its burning season (February–April).</p>

<h2>Where to go</h2>
<p>Thailand has three headline regions. <strong>Bangkok</strong>, the electric capital, for temples, street food, and markets. The <strong>north</strong> around <strong>Chiang Mai</strong> for culture, mountains, cooking classes, and ethical elephant sanctuaries. And the <strong>southern islands</strong> for beaches and boats. A classic first trip combines all three; with a week, do Bangkok plus one region.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: getyourguide — Thailand tours and experiences -->

<h2>How long and an itinerary shape</h2>
<p><strong>Ten days</strong> is the sweet spot for a first trip — three days in Bangkok, three in the north, three on the islands, plus travel days, linked by short domestic flights. A week works for Bangkok plus one region; two weeks lets you go deeper or add a second island.</p>

<h2>Getting around</h2>
<p><strong>Fly</strong> between regions — domestic flights are cheap, frequent, and quick (1–1.5 hours versus 10+ overland). Locally, use the <strong>BTS Skytrain and river boats</strong> in Bangkok, <strong>Grab</strong> for ride-hailing, songthaews and metered taxis, and <strong>ferries</strong> to the islands. The scenic overnight train to Chiang Mai is a lovely option if you have time.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: skyscanner — domestic flights -->

<h2>Money and costs</h2>
<p>Thailand is <strong>great value</strong>. The big cost is the international flight; daily spending is low. It's largely <strong>cash-based</strong> for everyday purchases, with cards accepted in hotels and malls. Tipping is modest and not obligatory. Use ATMs wisely (withdraw larger amounts to limit fees). Prices and rates change, so check a live converter:</p>

<h2>Food, safety, and culture</h2>
<p><strong>Food</strong> is a highlight — eat street food fearlessly (busy stalls, cooked fresh) and try regional dishes; drink bottled water. Thailand is generally <strong>safe</strong>, with road accidents (especially scooters) and scams the main risks rather than violent crime — use Grab, be scam-aware, and get good travel insurance. Respect the <strong>culture</strong>: revere the monarchy, dress modestly at temples (cover shoulders and knees), mind the head-and-feet customs, and keep your cool. TITLE: How Many Days Do You Need in Thailand?
CATEGORY: where-to-go

<p>"How many days do I need in Thailand?" is one of the first questions travelers ask, and the honest answer is: it depends on what you want to see. Thailand is a large, varied country, and the right length depends on whether you want one region or all three. Here's a breakdown by trip length to help you decide.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: A montage feel of Thailand — temple, mountains, and island -->

<h2>The short answer</h2>
<p>For a first trip that captures the essence of Thailand, <strong>ten days to two weeks is ideal</strong>. That's enough to combine Bangkok with both the northern mountains and the southern islands at a comfortable pace. If you have less time, you can absolutely still have a great trip by focusing on fewer regions — and if you have more, Thailand will happily fill it.</p>

<h2>A long weekend or 3–4 days</h2>
<p>With just a few days, <strong>focus on one place</strong> — almost certainly <strong>Bangkok</strong>, which has more than enough to fill three or four days (temples, street food, markets, day trips). Alternatively, a short trip works for a single beach destination or Chiang Mai. Don't try to combine regions in this timeframe; you'd spend it traveling. A few days is enough for a taste, often as a stopover or an add-on to another trip.</p>

<h2>One week (7 days)</h2>
<p>A week lets you do <strong>Bangkok plus one other region</strong> comfortably. The two classic one-week trips are <strong>Bangkok and the beaches</strong> (three days in the city, four on a southern island) or <strong>Bangkok and the north</strong> (the city plus Chiang Mai). You can't do both the north and the islands well in a week — the transit eats too much time — so pick the one that appeals most.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: A traveler looking out over a Thai landscape, planning feel -->

<h2>Ten days — the sweet spot</h2>
<p>Ten days is the <strong>ideal first-trip length</strong>, because it lets you see all three headline regions without rushing: roughly three days in Bangkok, three in the north around Chiang Mai, and three on the southern islands, with travel days and short domestic flights linking them. This is the most popular first-timer itinerary for good reason — it's balanced, varied, and unhurried.</p>

<h2>Two weeks</h2>
<p>With <strong>two weeks</strong>, you can do everything ten days offers plus go deeper — more time in the north, a second island, central Thailand sights like Ayutthaya, and built-in rest days. It's enough to slow down and travel rather than sightsee, and many travelers feel it's the most satisfying length if you can manage it.</p>

<h2>Three weeks or more</h2>
<p><strong>Three weeks-plus</strong> lets you really explore — adding central Thailand, the deep north, multiple islands, and time to linger in places you love, or to take up a diving course or other multi-day activities. Note that longer stays approach the visa-exemption limits (which have been changing), so verify current rules for trips beyond a couple of weeks.</p>

<h2>How to decide</h2>
<p>Work backward from what you most want: if it's <strong>just beaches</strong>, even a week on the islands is plenty; if you want the <strong>full Thailand experience</strong>, give it ten days to two weeks; if you want to <strong>travel slowly and deeply</strong>, three weeks rewards you. Whatever your length, the key is not to over-pack the itinerary — fewer regions done well beats a frantic dash. For budgeting any length of trip, a live converter helps:</p>
