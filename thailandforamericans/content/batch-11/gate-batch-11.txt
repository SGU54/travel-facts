TITLE: Money in Thailand: Cash, Cards, ATMs, and Tipping
CATEGORY: money-and-costs

<p>Handling money well in Thailand saves you fees and hassle. It's a largely cash-based society for everyday spending, with cards accepted in more upscale settings, and there are a few quirks — like ATM fees and exchange tips — worth knowing. Here's a practical guide to managing your baht.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Thai baht notes and coins, or a currency exchange booth -->

<h2>The currency</h2>
<p>Thailand's currency is the <strong>Thai baht (THB)</strong>, symbol ฿. Notes come in 20, 50, 100, 500, and 1,000 baht; coins in smaller denominations. The exchange rate against the dollar moves daily, so check a current rate rather than assuming a fixed value:</p>
<div class="feature-converter" data-from="USD" data-to="THB">100 USD ≈ … THB (enable JavaScript for today's rate)</div>
<p>Keep a mix of denominations — small notes are handy for street food, markets, taxis, and tuk-tuks, where breaking a 1,000-baht note can be a hassle.</p>

<h2>Cash vs. cards</h2>
<p>Thailand is still largely <strong>cash-based for everyday spending</strong>. You'll need cash for street food, markets, small vendors, tuk-tuks, songthaews, and most rural areas. <strong>Cards are accepted</strong> at hotels, malls, chain and upscale restaurants, and many city businesses, but don't rely on them everywhere. The local QR-payment system (PromptPay) is ubiquitous among Thais but generally requires a Thai bank account, so it's not an option for most tourists. The practical approach: carry enough cash for daily needs, and use cards for bigger payments where accepted.</p>

<h2>ATMs and fees</h2>
<p>ATMs are <strong>everywhere</strong> in cities and tourist areas and accept major international cards (Visa, Mastercard). The catch: Thai ATMs charge a <strong>foreign-card withdrawal fee</strong> per transaction, on top of whatever your home bank charges. Because this fee is per-withdrawal, the smart move is to <strong>withdraw larger amounts less frequently</strong> rather than making many small withdrawals. Check whether your home bank refunds foreign ATM fees or has partner networks, and always choose to be charged in <strong>baht, not dollars</strong>, when the machine asks (declining the "dynamic currency conversion" gets you a better rate).</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: An ATM in a Thai street setting -->

<h2>Exchanging currency</h2>
<p>If you bring dollars to exchange, you'll generally get <strong>better rates at dedicated exchange booths</strong> (like the well-known orange Super Rich booths in Bangkok) than at airport counters or hotels, which tend to offer poor rates. Exchange a small amount at the airport if you need immediate cash, then use better-rate booths or ATMs in the city. Bring crisp, undamaged bills, as torn or marked notes are sometimes refused.</p>

<h2>Tipping customs</h2>
<p>Tipping is <strong>not a deeply ingrained obligation</strong> in Thailand the way it is in the U.S., but it's appreciated and increasingly expected in tourist areas. As a guide: <strong>round up</strong> taxi fares, leave <strong>small change or 20–50 baht</strong> at restaurants (check first whether a 10% service charge is already added, common at upscale places), tip <strong>hotel porters</strong> a small amount, and tip <strong>spa and massage therapists</strong> and <strong>tour guides</strong> if you're happy with the service. Don't feel pressured into American-style percentages — modest tips are the norm.</p>

<h2>Practical money tips</h2>
<p>To sum up: <strong>carry cash</strong> for daily spending and keep small notes handy; <strong>use cards</strong> for bigger payments where accepted; <strong>minimize ATM fees</strong> by withdrawing larger amounts and choosing baht over dollars; <strong>exchange at good-rate booths</strong>, not airports or hotels; <strong>tip modestly</strong> where appropriate; and <strong>notify your bank</strong> of travel to avoid card blocks. Keep some cash in reserve separate from your wallet, too, in case of loss or theft.</p>

<p>Thailand is already one of the world's best-value destinations, and with a few smart habits you can travel it remarkably cheaply without sacrificing the experiences that make it special. The country is built for budget travel — it's where backpackers have flocked for decades — so stretching your money here is easy. Here's how to do Thailand on a budget while still doing it well.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: A budget traveler enjoying a Thai street food market -->

<h2>Save on the biggest cost: flights</h2>
<p>Your international flight is the largest expense, so it's where savings matter most. <strong>Book well in advance</strong>, be <strong>flexible on dates</strong> (mid-week and shoulder-season flights are cheaper), <strong>compare routes</strong> (different Asian or Middle Eastern connections vary a lot), and consider <strong>flying into Bangkok</strong> (the cheapest gateway) rather than directly to an island. Once in Thailand, <strong>domestic budget airlines</strong> are cheap if booked ahead.</p>

<h2>Accommodation: comfort for little money</h2>
<p>Thailand excels at budget accommodation. <strong>Hostels</strong> here are some of the best in the world — stylish, social, and cheap. <strong>Guesthouses and budget hotels</strong> offer private rooms for modest prices, and even <strong>mid-range hotels with pools</strong> are affordable by Western standards. Book a little ahead for the best-value places, stay slightly outside the most touristy spots, and consider fan rooms over air-con to save more. You don't have to sacrifice comfort to travel cheaply here.</p>

<h2>Eat like a local — it's cheaper and better</h2>
<p>This is the easiest big saving and no sacrifice at all: <strong>eat street food and at local restaurants</strong>, where a delicious, filling meal costs a fraction of Western or tourist-restaurant prices. The best food in Thailand often comes from the cheapest places — busy stalls and simple eateries. Drink <strong>local beer</strong> and avoid imported drinks, fill up at <strong>markets</strong>, and you'll eat superbly for very little.</p>

<h2>Transport: use what locals use</h2>
<p>Skip the tourist transport. Use the <strong>BTS Skytrain, MRT, and river boats</strong> in Bangkok; <strong>songthaews</strong> (shared trucks) and <strong>Grab</strong> over negotiated tuk-tuks; <strong>buses and trains</strong> for longer overland journeys (the overnight train doubles as accommodation); and <strong>combined bus-and-ferry tickets</strong> to the islands. Even the famous experiences — an ethical elephant sanctuary, a boat trip, a cooking class — are affordable compared to similar activities elsewhere. Budget for a few paid highlights you really want, and fill the rest with the country's abundant free pleasures.</p>

<h2>Where to spend and where to save</h2>
<p>The smart budget strategy isn't depriving yourself — it's <strong>spending on what matters and saving on what doesn't</strong>. Save relentlessly on food (eat local), transport (go local), and accommodation (book smart), since these barely affect your experience. TITLE: Scuba Diving and Snorkeling in Thailand: Where to Go
CATEGORY: nature-adventure

<p>Thailand is one of the world's great and most affordable places to dive and snorkel, with warm, clear water, colorful reefs, and abundant marine life across both coasts. Whether you want to learn to dive cheaply, log world-class dives, or just snorkel off a beautiful beach, here's where to go and what to know.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Colorful coral reef and tropical fish in clear Thai water -->

<h2>Koh Tao — the place to learn</h2>
<p><strong>Koh Tao</strong>, a small island on the Gulf coast, is one of the <strong>cheapest and most popular places in the world to learn to scuba dive</strong>. It has a huge concentration of dive schools, calm conditions ideal for beginners, and affordable certification courses. If getting your diving certification is a goal, Koh Tao is the obvious choice — many travelers build a few days of their trip around it. It's also great for snorkeling and experienced divers, with plenty of accessible sites.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: getyourguide — Koh Tao diving / snorkeling -->

<h2>The Similan Islands — world-class diving</h2>
<p>On the Andaman coast, the <strong>Similan Islands</strong> (reached from Phuket or Khao Lak) offer some of Thailand's <strong>best diving</strong> — pristine reefs, dramatic granite boulders, and the chance to see larger marine life. They're a protected national park with <strong>seasonal access</strong> (typically open in the dry season, roughly mid-October to mid-May, and closed during monsoon), so plan around that. Liveaboard trips and day trips both run; this is a bucket-list destination for serious divers.</p>

<h2>Other top spots</h2>
<p>Beyond the headliners, the <strong>Andaman coast</strong> around Phuket, Krabi, and the <strong>Phi Phi Islands</strong> has excellent diving and snorkeling among the limestone scenery, including spots like the King Cruiser wreck and Shark Point. <strong>Koh Lanta</strong> accesses the renowned Hin Daeng and Hin Muang dive sites. On the Gulf side, <strong>Koh Phangan</strong> and the waters around <strong>Koh Samui</strong> offer good diving and snorkeling, with Sail Rock a standout nearby site.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: A diver or snorkeler exploring a reef in turquoise water -->

<h2>Snorkeling without diving</h2>
<p>You don't need to dive to enjoy Thailand's underwater world. Excellent <strong>snorkeling</strong> is accessible on island-hopping boat trips throughout both coasts — Phi Phi, the Hong Islands, Ang Thong Marine Park, and countless reefs are reachable on day tours, with gear usually provided. For casual snorkelers, joining a boat trip is the easiest way to see colorful fish and coral without any certification or experience.</p>

TITLE: Thai Street Food: What to Eat and How to Order It Safely
CATEGORY: street-food-markets

<p>Street food isn't just a cheap way to eat in Thailand — it's some of the best food in the country, full stop. From sizzling woks to grills and noodle carts, the streets serve dishes that rival anything in a restaurant, for a fraction of the price. For many visitors, eating their way through the street stalls is a highlight of the whole trip. Here's what to eat, how to order, and how to do it safely.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: A vibrant Thai street food stall with woks and fresh ingredients -->

<h2>Why Thai street food is special</h2>
<p>In Thailand, street food is a serious culinary tradition, not an afterthought. Vendors often specialize in a single dish perfected over years or generations, and locals eat from stalls daily. The result is fresh, fast, flavorful food cooked in front of you — and it's astonishingly cheap. Bangkok's street food is world-famous, but you'll find excellent stalls all over the country, each region with its specialties.</p>

<h2>The must-try street dishes</h2>
<p>Start with the classics: <strong>pad thai</strong> (stir-fried noodles), <strong>pad krapow</strong> (basil stir-fry with minced meat and a fried egg over rice — a local staple), <strong>som tum</strong> (spicy green papaya salad), <strong>satay</strong> (grilled skewers with peanut sauce), and <strong>grilled meats and seafood</strong>. Try the noodle soups (<strong>boat noodles</strong>, <strong>khao soi</strong> in the north), <strong>mango sticky rice</strong> and other sweets, fresh <strong>fruit</strong> and fruit shakes, and regional specialties wherever you are. Half the fun is pointing at what looks good and trying something new.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: getyourguide — street food tour -->

<h2>How to find the best stalls</h2>
<p>The golden rule: <strong>follow the crowds</strong>. A stall busy with locals signals fresh food, fast turnover, and quality — and is also safer. Look for vendors who <strong>specialize</strong> in one or a few dishes (a sign of expertise), where you can <strong>see the food cooked fresh</strong>, and where ingredients look clean and well-handled. Markets, designated street-food streets (like Bangkok's Yaowarat in Chinatown), and areas where office workers and families eat are reliable bets. Don't be put off by humble plastic-stool setups — they're often the best.</p>

<h2>How to order</h2>
<p>Ordering is easy even without Thai. <strong>Point</strong> at what you want or at what someone else is eating, hold up fingers for quantity, and <strong>learn a few words</strong>: "mai phet" (not spicy), "phet nit noi" (a little spicy), and "aroi" (delicious — always appreciated). Many stalls have pictures or English on popular tourist streets. Have <strong>small cash notes</strong> ready, since stalls don't take cards. A smile goes a long way.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: A plate of fresh Thai street food being handed over -->

<h2>How to eat street food safely</h2>
<p>Thai street food is generally very safe, and these habits keep it that way: <strong>choose busy stalls</strong> with high turnover (fresh food, not sitting around); <strong>eat food cooked fresh and hot</strong> in front of you; be a little <strong>cautious with raw items, pre-cut fruit left out, and uncooked shellfish</strong>; <strong>drink bottled water</strong> (ice in established places is usually fine); and <strong>give your stomach a day or two</strong> to adjust to new ingredients and spice. Carry rehydration salts just in case. With common sense, eating from stalls is one of the safest and most rewarding things you'll do in Thailand.</p>

<p>To get the best out of Thai street food: take a <strong>guided street-food tour</strong> early in your trip to learn what's what and discover stalls you'd never find alone; <strong>eat where locals eat</strong>; <strong>try regional specialties</strong> as you travel; and <strong>be adventurous</strong> — some of the best things you'll eat are dishes you can't name.
