# Turkey, Worked Out. — manuscript, part 1

Stage C prose. Register: calm, declarative, no hedging. Marks are seasoning — at
most one per paragraph, on the load-bearing claim only, always at sentence end.
Synthesis is unmarked; facts are marked once.

Running heads and folios are furniture. Nothing below types a chapter name or
the book title into body prose.

---

## OPENING

*Standfirst: What this book is for, and the terms on which it is offered.*

Somewhere in the six weeks before a trip to Turkey, a sensible person sits down
to settle the practical questions and discovers that the answers do not settle.
Not because the information is hidden — Turkey is written about exhaustively —
but because the specific things worth knowing turn out to be scattered across
pages that never refer to one another.

This book collects five of those questions and does the reasoning on them.

It is not a guidebook. It names no hotels, recommends no restaurants, and prints
no prices. What it contains is a small number of decisions that cost real money
or a real day when they go wrong, each one worked through with the arithmetic
visible, **so that you can disagree with us and still come out ahead.** A verdict
you can argue with is a verdict; a verdict you cannot argue with is a
recommendation, and recommendations are free everywhere.

Every claim traces to a published source or to the reporting on our own site. The
marks in the margin tell you which, and how solid it is. Where a number does not
exist — and this book exists largely because of how often that turned out to be
the case — we say so rather than repeating one that someone invented.

## HOW TO READ THIS

*Standfirst: Four marks, two documents, and one way of using this on the road.*

**The marks.** A claim carries at most one, at the end of the sentence it
certifies.

`● ESTABLISHED` — structural, and true for years rather than months.
`◇ OFFICIAL` — a published rule, a regulation, a government position.
`◆ VOLATILE` — a live figure. **These do not appear in this book.** They live on
the Field Sheet, which is dated.

You will notice most paragraphs carry no mark at all. That is deliberate.
Reasoning is ours and is not a sourced claim; the facts underneath it are, and
those are marked once each. If everything were marked, nothing would be.

**Where the reporting comes from.** This book is built from the articles on
turkeyforamericans.com — the same desk, not an outside authority. Every one is
listed at **turkeyforamericans.com/sources**, with its dates.

**The two documents.** This book ages slowly. It carries no price, no exchange
rate, no opening hour. The Field Sheet carries exactly those, is dated, and is
reissued. When the two disagree, the Sheet is right and this book is old.

**On the trip.** The decisions are one page each and are written to be read
standing up. The failure modes at the back are organised by what goes wrong, not
by subject, because at the moment something goes wrong you know the symptom and
not the category.

## THE GROUND

*Standfirst: The one fact that reorganises everything else — in Turkey, the
number you need does not exist.*

Turkey is a country the size of the Gulf Coast and the eastern seaboard combined,
with six regions a visitor might plausibly want and no single hub that touches
all of them. That is worth knowing, and it is not the organising fact.

The organising fact is stranger, and once you have seen it you cannot stop seeing
it: **for each of the decisions in this book, the number you would look up does
not exist.**

Not hard to find. Not buried in Turkish. Not published, anywhere, by anyone.

Consider what a careful person does before a trip. They want to know how long a
connection needs at Istanbul, so they look for the minimum connection time.
Neither Istanbul airport publishes one — not for international-to-international,
not for international-to-domestic, not in either direction. `◇ OFFICIAL` The
figures in circulation, and there are many, trace back to no airport authority
and no regulator. Somebody wrote sixty minutes once, and it has been repeated
ever since.

The same pattern holds for the other four questions a careful person asks. In each
case what is missing is not detail but the figure itself:

- **What a hospital visit costs.** No published self-pay tariff for a foreign
  patient exists; pricing sits inside a ministry framework rather than on a page
  you can consult. `◇ OFFICIAL`
- **What a rental desk will require.** Turkish law sets none of it — the age, the
  licence-holding period, the deposit and the card are each company's contract.
  `●`
- **How old is too old to take home.** The statute contains no age threshold at
  all. `◇ OFFICIAL`
- **What an ATM charges.** One major bank publishes a percentage; the others
  publish nothing. `◇ OFFICIAL`

Five questions. Five vacuums.

### What fills a vacuum

Nature's preference here is not a number but a rumour that resembles one. The
hundred-year rule. The ninety-minute connection. *You need an International
Driving Permit.* A hospital figure from a forum post in 2019.

Each of those is repeated with total confidence by people who never established
whether a published figure exists, and each is doing a job the real answer
cannot: it is short, memorable, and requires no thought. The real answers are
judgements — about how much risk to carry, which threshold binds you, what an
object is. Judgements do not fit in a sentence, so a sentence gets invented and
travels instead.

That is the failure this book is built against. Not ignorance: misplaced
certainty, which is worse, because it stops the reader looking.

### What follows for the trip

Three things follow, and they shape everything after this page.

**The decisions must be reasoned, not looked up.** Every chapter here shows its
working precisely because there is no table to point at. Where the working ends
in a judgement, the judgement is ours and you may take a different one.

**The components are knowable even when the answer isn't.** Nobody publishes how
long a connection needs at Istanbul, but the steps in that connection are
documented and countable: passport control, the baggage hall, a second check-in,
a second security queue, and a walk that can exceed twenty minutes inside a
single terminal. `●` The number is absent; the anatomy is not. That distinction
is the method of this entire book.

**Someone else's number is someone else's risk tolerance.** When a figure has no
source, adopting it means adopting the judgement of a stranger who may have been
travelling hand-luggage-only on a through-ticket while you have a checked bag and
two separate bookings.

## BEFORE YOU FLY

*Standfirst: The things that cannot be arranged later, with the deadline attached
to each.*

Most pre-trip lists are inventories. This one is short because it contains only
the items with a genuine deadline — things that become impossible, rather than
merely inconvenient, once you have left.

**An International Driving Permit, if you intend to drive anything.** It must be
obtained before departure: it is issued in the United States by two authorised
bodies only, and it cannot be arranged from abroad. `◇ OFFICIAL` Turkish law does
not require it, which is the reason most people skip it and the reason skipping
it is a judgement rather than an oversight. The reasoning is on the driving page.

**Your policy's exclusions, read rather than assumed.** The activities most
people book first in Turkey — the balloon, the paraglide at Ölüdeniz, anything
with two wheels — sit close to the lines a standard policy draws around itself.
`◇ OFFICIAL` This is readable today and cannot be changed once something has
happened.

**Whether your policy is primary or secondary.** It does not change what you are
owed; it changes the order of the paperwork and how many months it takes. `◇
OFFICIAL` Establish it while you can still reach your insurer easily.

**Your own bank's withdrawal terms.** Of the layers that make cash expensive in
Turkey, this is the only one you can know in advance, and it is written in your
account terms rather than anywhere in Turkey.

**A card contact route that works from abroad.** A domestic toll-free number is
frequently unreachable from outside the country, which is discovered at precisely
the wrong moment.

**Passport validity of six months beyond your date of entry, with a blank page.**
`◇ OFFICIAL` This is the one item on the list that is genuinely a hard rule, and
the one most often quoted wrongly — the figure of a hundred and fifty days
circulates widely and is not the American-facing requirement.

Everything else can be handled in Turkey. These cannot.

---

**The medication you take daily.** Medicines for personal treatment listed in the
customs annex are admitted in reasonable quantities — reasonable being read
against the length of your stay — when they travel with a document stating you
must use them during the trip. `◇ OFFICIAL` A report from a healthcare
organisation, a doctor's report or the prescription itself all satisfy that; what
does not satisfy it is a labelled bottle and a confident explanation.

The category worth checking before you pack is narcotic pain medication. Several
common United States prescriptions are controlled, restricted or simply
unavailable in Turkey, which matters twice over: once at the border, and again if
you finish the trip needing a refill of something no Turkish pharmacy will
dispense. A pharmacist can advise on and dispense a great deal without a
prescription here, and that generosity does not extend to the controlled list.

**The passport rule that is enforced by someone other than Turkey.** Entry
requires validity beyond your stay and a blank page, and the number that matters
is not the one printed in the entry rules. `◇ OFFICIAL` It is the one your
airline applies at the check-in desk in the United States, because a carrier that
boards a passenger who is then refused entry carries the cost of flying them
home. The same three-threshold shape appears here as in the licence chapter: what
the rule says, what the carrier enforces, and what the desk agent in front of you
has been trained to do. They usually agree. When they do not, the strictest one
wins, and it is applied before you have left the country.

That is the whole argument for treating the pre-departure list as a list of
things that become impossible rather than merely inconvenient. Nothing on it is
hard. All of it is unavailable once the aircraft door closes.

## DAY ZERO

*Standfirst: The arrival day, which every guide skips and every traveller
experiences.*

The most anxious hours of a Turkey trip are the ones between landing and being
somewhere with your bag. They are also the hours least written about, because
they are nobody's idea of a highlight.

Here is what actually happens, in order.

**Immigration.** United States ordinary-passport holders enter without a visa for
tourism, for up to ninety days within any hundred-and-eighty-day period. `◇
OFFICIAL` There is no e-visa step for this passport any more, which contradicts a
great deal of older advice. The passport itself needs six months' validity beyond
entry and a blank page.

**The bag, or not.** If your onward domestic flight sits on the same ticket, the
bag is generally tagged through and you will not see it here. If the tickets are
separate, you will reclaim it, and everything after this point takes materially
longer. `◇ OFFICIAL`

**The airport, which is larger than expected.** Istanbul Airport is a single
terminal with several concourses, and the walk between the furthest gates can
exceed twenty to thirty minutes before any queue. `●` Same terminal does not mean
nearby.

**Which airport you are in.** Both of Istanbul's international airports are
called Istanbul in casual speech and in many booking interfaces. The codes are
the only reliable identifier, and a booking confirmation always carries one even
when the display name does not distinguish them. `●` An onward flight from the
other airport is not a connection; it is a road journey across a city of sixteen
million.

**Money, immediately.** You will want cash within the first day — for a taxi that
is not on an app, for a small restaurant, for tips. What that cash costs depends
on which machine you use rather than how much you take out, and that is the
subject of its own decision.

### The first evening

Nothing in this book improves the first evening. Eat near where you are sleeping,
accept that jet lag makes any plan optimistic, and keep the ambitious sights for
a day when you have slept. The one operational note: if an early domestic flight
follows, the packing happens tonight, because Istanbul traffic in the morning is
not a variable you control.

## TIMING

*Standfirst: The rhythm of a Turkish week, and the two moving holidays that most
foreign planning ignores.*

Turkey's calendar has a fixed layer and a moving one, and visitors reliably
account for the first while never seeing the second.

The fixed layer is the national holidays, which sit on the same dates every year:
the first of January, the twenty-third of April, the first and nineteenth of May,
the fifteenth of July, the thirtieth of August, and the twenty-ninth of October,
that last one preceded by a half-day from one o'clock. `◇ OFFICIAL` They close
banks and government offices and change nothing else.

The moving layer is the two Bayram holidays, and it moves a great deal — roughly
ten to eleven days earlier each year against the Gregorian calendar, because it
follows the lunar one. `● ESTABLISHED` Notes from a previous trip, or a friend's
advice from three years ago, describe a calendar that has since shifted by a
month.

Each Bayram is a legal block rather than a day: a half-day from one o'clock, then
three full days for the first and four for the second. `◇ OFFICIAL` What that
does to transport and hotel availability is not published in any form worth
quoting, which is a recurring theme, but the block itself is a matter of statute
and can be looked up years ahead.

There is a third layer that is decided annually and cannot be planned against at
all: additional administrative leave, and whether motorway tolls are waived for
that year's holiday window. Both arrive by decision rather than by rule. `◇
OFFICIAL`

### Ramadan, which changes less than expected

The fasting month is not a public holiday and closes nothing. Sites, transport
and hotels run normally. What varies is daytime dining, and it varies by place
rather than by rule — unremarkable in the tourist districts of Istanbul and the
coastal resorts, noticeably thinner in a small inland town. `●` Evenings are the
best-tempered hours of the Turkish year.

## MONEY

*Standfirst: Where the money leaks, which is not where travellers look for it.*

Turkey is inexpensive for an American and will feel more so than the arithmetic
warrants, because two quantities are moving at once: domestic prices rising
quickly in lira, and the lira moving against the dollar. Consumer prices rose
about thirty-two per cent year-on-year in the most recent full measurement. `◇
OFFICIAL` Those two movements can offset each other, compound, or diverge, and
the net effect on a trip is not readable from either number alone.

The practical consequence is not that Turkey is expensive. It is that **any
figure you read in an older source is not slightly wrong but structurally
unreliable** — off by an unpredictable proportion, and by a different proportion
from the figure printed next to it. What survives repricing is the *shape* of
spending: a bus against a flight, a neighbourhood restaurant against a tourist
one, a public ferry against a chartered boat. Those relationships hold. The
amounts do not, and the Field Sheet carries the sources that price them today.

### The leak

The money that goes missing on a Turkey trip does not go missing on hotels or
meals. It goes on the mechanics of holding cash, in a country where cards are
widely accepted and several everyday transactions still are not — the small
family restaurant, the bazaar stall where a price is being negotiated, the taxi
that is not on an app, and every tip. `●`

That means a trip consumes cash whether or not you intended it to, and cash in
Turkey carries a cost with an unusual shape. At least one major Turkish bank
publishes a foreign-card ATM withdrawal charge as a percentage rather than a flat
fee. `◇ OFFICIAL` A percentage behaves differently from the flat fees most
travellers are braced for, and it does not behave the same way as your own bank's
charge, which may be flat or proportional or both.

The full arithmetic is a decision of its own. The point for now is where to look:
not at the room rate, which is visible, but at the four layers of a cash
withdrawal, which are not.
