## D-01 · Transit — Should the internal flights sit on the same ticket as the flight home?

**Visual: yes** — the Istanbul funnel. Two airports, the regions they serve, and
which moves pass through which. A locator, not a route map.

**Naive model** *(corpus: `turkey-through-ticket-vs-separate-ticket`)* — *"most
Americans book those separately from the transatlantic ticket because it's
cheaper and the schedules line up better."* Then they leave what feels like a
sensible gap, because every airport has a minimum connection time and two hours
sounds generous.

**Lede (scene):** A traveler stands in the arrivals hall at Istanbul with a
boarding pass for a flight to Kayseri in two hours, a checked bag going round a
belt somewhere behind passport control, and no idea that the two hours were
chosen by nobody.

### The reframe

There is no published minimum connection time at Istanbul Airport `◇ OFFICIAL`,
and none at Sabiha Gökçen either. Not for international-to-international, not
international-to-domestic, not domestic-to-international. The figures in
circulation — sixty minutes, ninety, two and a half hours — trace back to neither
airport operator nor the regulator.

Airlines apply their own internal minima *when they sell a through-ticket*. That
is a carrier deciding what it will underwrite. **On separate tickets nobody has
made that judgement on your behalf, and there is no published number to borrow.**

### The trade-off, scored · one internal leg, bag checked

| | SAME TICKET | SEPARATE TICKETS |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns a missed connection `◇` | the carrier — refund or re-route, passenger chooses | you |
| Bag `◇` | tagged through | reclaim, re-check |
| Passport control on int'l→domestic `◇` | unavoidable either way | unavoidable either way |
| Re-route to the *other* Istanbul airport `◇` | carrier pays the transfer | you pay |
| Delay assistance after 2h (domestic) `◇` | owed | owed on that leg only |
| Typical saving `●` | — | the reason people do it |

### The call

**Buy the internal legs on the same ticket as the international flight where the
route allows it, and treat a separate-ticket internal leg as a leg you have
insured yourself.** The saving is real and often material; what it buys is a
transfer of risk that has no published price, on a connection whose required
length no authority states. That is an unusual thing to accept knowingly, and a
very common thing to accept unknowingly.

**Then it's yes →** the carrier sells the whole itinerary as one booking; the
internal leg is the last one before a fixed international return; you're checking
a bag.

**Then it's no →** the legs are days apart with a night in between; the internal
flight is early in the trip with slack behind it; no through-ticket exists on the
route at all.

**Cross-ref:** the two-airport problem is D-03's neighbour — see the transfer
ledger there. Article 9(3) of the passenger-rights regulation is why the
"re-route to the other airport" row reads the way it does.

`◆ VOLATILE → Field Sheet:` carrier through-ticketing on Turkish domestic routes,
and which carriers interline.

## D-02 · Medical — What are you actually exposed to, and what does a policy answer?

**Visual: no.** A rule with thresholds. The ledger does the work; leave it clean.

**Naive model** *(corpus: `us-travel-insurance-turkey-what-it-covers`)* — *"Most
Americans buy travel insurance as a single product and think of it as a single
promise: something goes wrong, they pay."*

**Lede (scene):** In Turkey you are treated first and billed afterwards, which is
the reassuring half of the sentence. Nobody mentions that no published tariff
exists to tell you what the second half will say.

### The reframe

A travel policy is four separate promises with four separate triggers `●`, and
the one most likely to be claimed on — emergency medical — is the one an activity
exclusion can switch off `◇`. Meanwhile Turkish emergency care is guaranteed
before payment `◇`, which means the sequence that frightens Americans is the one
Turkey handles well, and the sequence that costs them is the one they never
examine.

### Where the cover actually sits · one traveller, one incident

| | WHAT IT ANSWERS | WHERE IT STOPS |
|---|---|---|
| Turkish emergency obligation `◇` | admission and intervention, unconditional | at stabilisation; transport is charged |
| Compulsory third-party motor `◇` | injury and property caused to others | not your vehicle, not you |
| Operator liability, e.g. balloon `◇` | the operator's liability during the activity | not your medical, evacuation or trip costs |
| Your medical section `◇` | treatment | excluded activities take the section with them |
| Your evacuation section `●` | moving you | separate limit, separate conditions |

### The call

**Establish two things before departure and you have done the work: whether your
policy is primary or secondary, and whether the activities you have already
booked appear in its exclusions.** Everything else is documentation you can
gather at the time. Those two are decided months earlier and cannot be changed
from a hospital corridor — and the balloon flight most people book first is the
activity most likely to sit in an exclusion.

**Then it's yes →** you've booked a balloon, a paraglide, a dive, or intend to
ride anything with two wheels.

**Then it's no →** the trip is cities and coastline on foot and in taxis; your
policy is primary; nothing you've booked appears in an activities schedule.

**Cross-ref:** the licence condition inside the two-wheeler exclusion is D-03's
whole subject. Repatriation cost has no defensible figure — three sources, three
non-overlapping ranges; it is stated as an order of magnitude and no more.

`◆ VOLATILE → Field Sheet:` nothing. This decision carries no live number, which
is itself the point.

## D-03 · Driving — Which of the four licence thresholds actually binds you?

**Visual: yes** — the four gates. A small figure: four vertical thresholds side
by side, three lookup-able, one not.

**Naive model** *(corpus: `renting-car-turkey`)* — *"Many travelers do a hybrid:
fly between regions, then rent locally for the coast or Cappadocia leg."* And
they resolve the paperwork question by reading that an IDP is *"recommended and
sometimes required"*, which resolves nothing.

**Lede (scene):** The counter takes ninety seconds. A licence is glanced at, a
card is swiped, a key changes hands — and four different parties have just been
satisfied by one document, or not.

### The reframe

Four thresholds, set by four parties, and they do not agree `●`. Turkish law is
the most permissive of them and the one travelers most fear.

### The four gates · US licence holder, standard rental

| | WHAT IT REQUIRES | LOOKUP-ABLE? |
|---|---|---|
| Turkish law `◇` | a foreign licence, six months from date of entry; no IDP mandated | yes — published |
| The rental desk `●` | whatever its contract says — age, holding period, card, documents | **no** — contractual, per company |
| Your insurer `◇` | a licence valid for the vehicle class, plus local compliance | yes — your own policy |
| The roadside `●` | a document an officer can read | the IDP's entire function |

### The call

**Carry the IDP even though Turkish law does not require it, and read the rental
contract rather than an article about rental contracts.** The permit is cheap,
issued by only two authorised bodies, and answers the one gate that is about
legibility rather than entitlement. The desk is the gate nobody can research for
you, and it is the gate that stops the trip at the counter — which is a
different failure from the one everyone prepares for.

**Then it's yes →** you're renting anything, anywhere, including for one day.

**Then it's no →** you have no intention of driving. There is no middle case; the
document costs less than a tank of fuel.

**Cross-ref:** the two-wheeler licence category is genuinely unsettled in the open
sources and this book prints no threshold for it — see the note in D-02 on what
an activity exclusion reaches.

`◆ VOLATILE → Field Sheet:` IDP fee; whether any Turkish rule on foreign licences
has moved.

## D-04 · Buying — What can actually leave the country with you?

**Visual: a plate.** A market stall of old brassware and ceramics — the photo is
the evidence that the ambiguous objects and the ordinary ones sit on the same
table.

**Naive model** *(corpus: `shopping-in-turkey`)* — *"genuine antiques and
antiquities are tightly restricted for export from Turkey, so be skeptical of
anything sold as truly ancient."* True, and it leaves the buyer with a rule of
thumb everyone repeats: a hundred years.

**Lede (scene):** Somewhere between agreeing a price and handing over a card, a
certificate appears — letterhead, a stamp, a description, sometimes an age. Of
everything you carry home, it is the document that will matter least.

### The reframe

There is no hundred-year rule `◇`. No fixed age or cut-off date exists for movable
cultural property; protection attaches to scientific and cultural criteria, not
to a date. And the penalty behind that framework is five to twelve years'
imprisonment with a judicial fine `◇` — a criminal statute, not a customs
formality.

### What each document does · one purchase, two borders

| | TURKISH EXPORT | US IMPORT |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor certificate `●` | no independent legal force | nothing |
| Receipt `◇` | establishes ownership and price, authorises nothing | valuation and declaration |
| Museum expertise report `◇` | **the determination that binds** | supports lawful-export proof |
| Nothing at all `◇` | the default for ordinary modern goods | declare it and move on |

### The call

**Buy new things freely and treat any object presented as old as a decision
rather than a purchase.** Nearly everything on nearly every stall falls outside
the framework entirely. The moment a seller reaches for a certificate, the object
has moved into a category where the only binding opinion comes from a state
museum, the seller carries none of the risk, and the document that raises the
price is the same document that describes the object as protected.

**Then it's yes →** it's new, it's ordinary, the certificate question never
arises. Keep the receipt for the American side and stop thinking about it.

**Then it's no →** it's presented as antique, it was found rather than bought, or
it's a coin, fossil, fragment or icon. Then the question is a determination, not a
transaction.

**Cross-ref:** the US restriction on Turkish material reaches to 1923, which is
more recent than "antiquity" suggests — the two systems ask different questions
and neither defers to the other.

`◆ VOLATILE → Field Sheet:` the US import-restriction expiry; the $800 personal
exemption.

## D-05 · Cash — How much does it cost to hold Turkish lira?

**Visual: yes** — the fee stack. Four layers as a stacked bar, showing which
scale with the amount and which don't.

**Naive model** *(corpus: `money-in-turkey`)* — *"many travelers skip cash
exchange entirely and just withdraw lira from ATMs as needed, which is simple and
fair-rated."* The site says it, the guidebooks say it, and it is the belief this
decision exists to test.

**Lede (scene):** Offered a choice of currencies, you decline the obvious trap,
take your notes, and walk away satisfied you have just done the sensible thing.

### The reframe

Fair-rated describes the exchange rate. It says nothing about the operator fee,
and at least one major Turkish bank publishes its foreign-card ATM charge as a
percentage running to twenty-five `◇`. No national figure exists; some banks
publish nothing at all.

### The four layers · one withdrawal

| | WHO APPLIES IT | SCALES WITH AMOUNT? |
|---|---|---|
| Turkish operator fee `◇` | the machine's bank — published by some, not all | **yes, where it is a percentage** |
| Your issuer's fee `●` | your bank — fixed, percentage, or both | depends on your issuer |
| Exchange margin `●` | card network and issuer, inside the rate | yes, invisibly |
| DCC `●` | the terminal's provider, only if accepted | yes |

### The call

**Establish your own issuer's structure before you go, then choose the machine
rather than the amount.** The layer that can move most is the one attached to the
bank whose ATM you happen to stand in front of, and it is the only layer you can
change at the moment of withdrawal. Optimising the amount while ignoring which
machine you used is solving the wrong variable — and it is what "just use ATMs"
quietly recommends.

**Then it's yes →** your trip runs through bazaars, small restaurants, non-app
taxis and tips, where cash is the only instrument.

**Then it's no →** you're in cities and hotels on cards throughout, and cash is a
small reserve rather than a running cost.

**Cross-ref:** what your trip actually consumes in cash is a function of where
you eat and how you move, not of Turkey — see the acceptance pattern.

`◆ VOLATILE → Field Sheet:` bank ATM tariffs; the lira rate; the inflation
print. **None of these appears in the Book.**
