## THE SET PIECES

*Standfirst: Three operations a Turkish first trip contains, and the order each
one has to be done in.*

A country trip is not a list of sights; it is a small number of operations that
have an internal order, and getting the order wrong costs a morning or a day.
Turkey has three that recur on almost every first itinerary.

### The balloon

The Cappadocia flight is the single most photographed thing in the country and
the most weather-dependent. Flights are cancelled for wind, and cancellation is
routine rather than exceptional.

The order matters more than the operator. What determines whether a cancellation
ruins the experience or merely delays it is how many mornings remain in
Cappadocia after the one you booked — a structural property of the itinerary,
decided weeks before, and not something the operator controls.

Two things sit underneath it that most travellers never separate. A licensed
Turkish operator must carry passenger liability insurance, and that cover
addresses **the operator's** liability during the flight. `◇ OFFICIAL` It does
not reach the passenger's own medical costs, evacuation, or the rest of a trip
disrupted by an incident — those belong to a policy that may classify ballooning
as an aviation or adventure activity and exclude it. `◇ OFFICIAL`

So the balloon is not one operation but three questions: how many mornings you
have, what the operator's cover does, and what yours says. Only the first is
visible when booking.

### The Istanbul crossing

Every Turkey itinerary passes through Istanbul, and most pass through it more than
once — inbound, between regions, and outbound.

The operational fact is that the city has two international airports on opposite
sides of a strait, both called Istanbul in casual speech, distinguishable only by
code. `●` An itinerary that arrives at one and departs from the other contains a
road journey across a city of sixteen million, and no airline system treats it as
a connection.

Order of operations: establish which airport each leg uses *before* the legs are
booked, not after. The codes are on every confirmation. A single letter is the
difference between a connection and a cross-city transfer.

There is one circumstance in which the transfer stops being yours. Where more
than one airport serves a city and the carrier re-routes you to the alternative,
it must cover your transfer to the airport on your original reservation. `◇
OFFICIAL` That applies when the airline moves you — not when you booked it that
way yourself.

### The coastal move

The Aegean and Mediterranean coasts sit at the far end of the country from
Istanbul and Cappadocia, and reaching them is where a first trip either uses a
domestic flight or loses most of a day.

Our own reporting resolves the base question well, and this book does not repeat it. The
operation underneath it is the move: which airport serves the base you chose,
whether the leg sits on the international ticket, and what happens to the rest of
the itinerary if it fails.

That is the transit decision applied to a specific stretch of coast, and the
arithmetic is on its page.

## EATING

*Standfirst: The rule that outlives any list of restaurant names.*

This book does not name restaurants, for reasons set out in the refusals. What it
can give you is the rule, which does not expire when a chef moves.

Turkish eating is organised by *type of establishment* far more than by
individual reputation, and the types are legible from the street. A **lokanta**
is a working-day restaurant serving prepared dishes from a counter — you point at
what you want and it arrives in ninety seconds. An **esnaf lokantası** is the same
thing aimed at tradespeople, which in practice means the best value in any
Turkish town. **Ocakbaşı** is grill-over-coals, usually in the evening. A
**meyhane** is for meze and rakı and a long night. **Pideci**, **kebapçı**,
**börekçi**, **muhallebici** — each names exactly what it sells.

The rule follows from that. **Choose the type first, and the specific place
second.** A traveller who understands that a lokanta at one in the afternoon is
full of people who work nearby has a better method than a traveller carrying five
names from a list, because the method works in a town nobody wrote about.

### What the method has to account for

**Card acceptance is a property of the type.** Hotels, larger restaurants and
tourist-district places take cards reliably. Small family-run lokantas, street
stalls and bazaar counters frequently do not. `●` The places the rule sends you to
are disproportionately the cash ones, which connects eating to the cash decision
rather than to a budget.

**Breakfast is an institution and not a meal.** A Turkish breakfast is a spread
eaten slowly, and it is served everywhere including in places that serve nothing
else. It is also unaffected by every calendar variation in this book — hotel
breakfast runs normally through Ramadan and every holiday.

**During Ramadan the daytime picture varies by place rather than by rule.** Some
restaurants reduce or suspend daytime service, mostly away from tourist areas; in
the cities and coastal resorts many run normally, and in a small inland town the
options can be genuinely thin. `●` No regulation requires any of it. Each
business decides.

### The three things worth eating that the rule alone won't find

Anticipation is part of what a trip is for, so: a proper Turkish breakfast on a
terrace, taken with no plan for the following two hours. A pide brought to the
table still too hot to hold. And, if the dates fall that way, an iftar table laid
before sunset with everything on it and nobody touching anything — the most
convivial hour in the Turkish year, and open to anyone who walks in.

### If you do not eat everything

The method survives a dietary constraint better than a recommendation would,
which is the point of having a method at all. A lokanta counter is the one
service in Turkey where the entire menu is visible before you commit: the trays
are the menu, and a tray of vegetables cooked in olive oil is a category of
Turkish cooking rather than a concession made for a visitor. That is a structural
advantage over a printed menu in a language you do not read, and it is the reason
the counter is the right room for anyone who has to look before they order.

The constraint that travels worst is not vegetarianism. It is the assumption that
a dish is what its name suggests. Meat stock is ordinary in vegetable dishes, and
the question that resolves it is about the stock rather than about the vegetables.
Ask in the kitchen's terms and the answer is reliable; ask whether something is
suitable and the answer is a courtesy.

None of this requires a list of restaurants, which is fortunate, because a list
of restaurants would be wrong by spring and the counter will still be a counter.
