# Turkey, Worked Out. — manuscript, part 3: THE DECISIONS

Five pages. Each opens on a scene, reframes, shows the ledger, and ends on a
verdict you can lose. Column headers on every ledger. One mark per unit, at
sentence end.

---

## I · TRANSIT

# Should the internal flights sit on the same ticket as the flight home?

*Whether you own the risk of a missed connection, or the airline does.*

A traveller stands in the arrivals hall at Istanbul with a boarding pass for
Kayseri in two hours, a checked bag going round a belt somewhere behind passport
control, and no idea that the two hours were chosen by nobody.

Almost every Turkey itinerary involves at least one internal flight, and most
Americans buy those separately from the transatlantic ticket. The fares are
better and the schedules line up more usefully. That is a real saving, and it is
also a transfer of risk that nobody prices at the point of sale.

### The reframe

There is no published minimum connection time at Istanbul Airport, and none at
Sabiha Gökçen — not for any connection type, in either direction. `◇ OFFICIAL`
The figures in circulation trace back to neither airport operator nor the
regulator.

Airlines apply internal minima when they sell a through-ticket, which is a
carrier deciding what it is willing to underwrite. On separate tickets nobody has
made that judgement on your behalf, and there is no published number to borrow in
its place.

So the question is not *how long a gap do I need* — that has no answer. It is
*who carries it if the gap turns out to be wrong.*

**One internal leg, bag checked**

| | SAME TICKET | SEPARATE TICKETS |
|---|---|---|
| Missed connection | carrier re-routes or refunds, your choice | yours |
| Bag | tagged through | reclaim and re-check |
| Passport control, int'l → domestic | unavoidable | unavoidable |
| Re-route to Istanbul's other airport | carrier pays the transfer | you pay |
| Assistance after a 2-hour domestic delay | owed | owed on that leg only |
| Fare | higher | the reason people do it |

The fourth row is the one nobody knows. Where more than one airport serves a
city and a carrier re-routes you to the other one, it must cover your transfer
back to the airport on your original booking. `◇ OFFICIAL` Istanbul is precisely
the case that clause was written for, and most passengers in that position pay
their own taxi.

### The call

**Put the internal legs on the international ticket where the route allows it,
and treat a separate-ticket internal leg as one you have insured yourself.**

The saving is genuine and sometimes large. What it buys is a risk with no
published price, on a connection whose required length no authority states. That
is an unusual thing to take on deliberately and a very common thing to take on
without noticing.

**Then it's yes** → the carrier sells the whole itinerary as one booking; the
internal leg is the last before a fixed international return; you are checking a
bag.

**Then it's no** → the legs are days apart with a night between them; the flight
sits early in the trip with slack behind it; no through-ticket exists on the route
at all.

*The two-airport problem recurs in the driving chapter. Carrier through-ticketing
on Turkish domestic routes is on the Field Sheet.*

## II · MEDICAL

# What are you exposed to, and what does a policy answer?

*Which of four promises applies, and which one an activity can switch off.*

In Turkey you are treated first and billed afterwards, which is the reassuring
half of the sentence. Nobody mentions that no published tariff exists to tell you
what the second half will say.

Most Americans buy travel insurance as a single product and understand it as a
single promise: something goes wrong, they pay. It is four promises with four
triggers, and they do not fail together.

### The reframe

Turkish emergency care is guaranteed before any question of payment — admission
and intervention are unconditional, and charges are collected afterwards. `◇
OFFICIAL` So the sequence Americans fear most is the one Turkey handles well.

The exposure sits after stabilisation, and the instrument that covers it —
the medical section of your own policy — is the one an activity exclusion can
remove.

**One traveller, one incident**

| | WHAT IT ANSWERS | WHERE IT STOPS |
|---|---|---|
| Turkish emergency obligation | admission and intervention, unconditional | at stabilisation; transport is charged |
| Compulsory third-party motor | injury and property caused to others | not your vehicle, not you |
| Activity operator liability | the operator's liability during the activity | not your medical, evacuation or trip costs |
| Your medical section | treatment | an excluded activity takes the section with it |
| Your evacuation section | moving you | separate limit, separate conditions |

Row three is the balloon. A licensed Turkish operator must carry passenger
liability cover, and it addresses the operator's liability — not the passenger's
medical, evacuation or trip-interruption costs. `◇ OFFICIAL` A licensed operator
with valid insurance and a passenger whose own policy excludes ballooning can
both be true at once. The gap between them is where the passenger sits.

### The call

**Establish two things before departure and the work is done: whether your policy
is primary or secondary, and whether the activities you have already booked
appear in its exclusions.**

Everything else is documentation you can gather at the time. Those two are fixed
months earlier and cannot be changed from a hospital corridor — and the balloon
flight most people book first is the activity most likely to sit in an exclusion.

**Then it's yes** → you have booked a balloon, a paraglide, a dive, or intend to
ride anything with two wheels.

**Then it's no** → the trip is cities and coast on foot and in taxis, your policy
is primary, and nothing you have booked appears in an activities schedule.

*The licence condition inside the two-wheeler exclusion is the next chapter.
Repatriation has no defensible figure — three sources gave three non-overlapping
ranges, so this book states an order of magnitude and no number.*

## III · DRIVING

# Which of the four licence thresholds is the one that binds you?

*Four parties, four requirements, and only three you can research.*

The counter takes ninety seconds. A licence is glanced at, a card is swiped, a
key changes hands — and four different parties have just been satisfied by one
document, or not.

Many travellers do a hybrid trip: fly between regions, then rent locally for the
coast or Cappadocia. They resolve the paperwork by reading that an International
Driving Permit is *recommended and sometimes required*, which resolves nothing.

### The reframe

Turkish law is the most permissive of the four thresholds and the one travellers
most fear. A foreign licence is valid for six months from date of entry, and no
IDP is mandated for a US licence holder inside that window. `◇ OFFICIAL`

The threshold that stops trips is the one nobody can research for you.

**US licence holder, standard rental**

| | WHAT IT REQUIRES | CAN YOU LOOK IT UP? |
|---|---|---|
| Turkish law | a foreign licence, six months from entry; no IDP mandated | yes — published |
| The rental desk | whatever its contract says: age, holding period, card, documents | **no** — contractual, per company |
| Your insurer | a licence valid for the vehicle class, plus local compliance | yes — your own policy |
| The roadside | a document an officer can read | that is the IDP's whole function |

No Turkish regulation sets a minimum rental age, a licence-holding period, a
deposit or a document list. `●` Those are contract terms, they differ between
companies and between branches, and a traveller refused at one counter and served
at another has met two contracts rather than an inconsistency.

### The call

**Carry the permit even though Turkish law does not require it, and read the
rental contract rather than an article about rental contracts.**

The permit is inexpensive, issued by two authorised bodies, and answers the one
gate that is about legibility rather than entitlement. The desk is the gate that
stops the trip at the counter — a different failure from the one everyone
prepares for, and the only one you cannot research in advance.

**Then it's yes** → you are renting anything, anywhere, including for one day.

**Then it's no** → you have no intention of driving. There is no middle case.

*On two-wheelers this book prints no engine-size threshold. The open sources
contradict one another and no official table was found; what survives without a
number is that a US car licence is not self-evidently a motorcycle entitlement,
and the insurance exclusion is written against the category.*

## IV · BUYING

# What can actually leave the country with you?

*Which document binds, and which one merely reassures.*

Somewhere between agreeing a price and handing over a card, a certificate appears
— letterhead, a stamp, a description, sometimes an age. Of everything you carry
home, it is the document that will matter least.

Our own reporting and every guidebook say the same sensible thing: be sceptical of
anything sold as genuinely ancient. True, and it leaves the buyer holding a rule
of thumb everyone repeats: a hundred years.

### The reframe

There is no hundred-year rule. No fixed age or cut-off date exists for movable
cultural property; protection attaches to scientific and cultural criteria rather
than to a date. `◇ OFFICIAL` The figure is trade vocabulary that has been mistaken
for law, and believing it produces a specific error — that a ninety-year-old
object is automatically safe.

Behind the framework sits a criminal penalty of five to twelve years'
imprisonment with a judicial fine. `◇ OFFICIAL` This is not a customs formality.

**One purchase, two borders**

| | TURKISH EXPORT | US IMPORT |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor certificate | no independent legal force | nothing |
| Receipt | ownership and price; authorises nothing | valuation and declaration |
| Museum expertise report | **the determination that binds** | supports lawful-export proof |
| No document at all | the default for ordinary modern goods | declare it and move on |

There is an awkward implication in row one. A certificate that convincingly
establishes age argues the object *toward* the statutory criteria. The same page
that supports the price weakens the position at departure, and the seller carries
none of that risk — the object leaves the shop either way.

### The call

**Buy new things freely, and treat any object presented as old as a decision
rather than a purchase.**

Nearly everything on nearly every stall falls outside the framework. `◇ OFFICIAL`
The moment a seller reaches for a certificate, the object has moved into a
category where the only binding opinion belongs to a state museum.

**Then it's yes** → it is new and ordinary; keep the receipt for the American side
and stop thinking about it.

**Then it's no** → it is presented as antique, it was found rather than bought, or
it is a coin, fossil, fragment or icon. That is a determination, not a
transaction.

*The US restriction reaches ethnological material to 1923 — more recent than
"antiquity" implies. The two systems ask different questions and neither defers
to the other.*

## V · CASH

# How much does it cost to hold Turkish lira?

*Four layers, of which you control one, and it is not the one you think.*

Offered a choice of currencies, you decline the obvious trap, take your notes, and
walk away satisfied you have just done the sensible thing.

The standard advice is to skip currency exchange entirely and withdraw from ATMs
as needed, on the grounds that it is simple and fair-rated. Our own site says it.
It is the belief this chapter exists to test.

### The reframe

*Fair-rated* describes the exchange rate. It says nothing about the operator fee,
and at least one major Turkish bank publishes its foreign-card ATM withdrawal
charge as a percentage running into double figures. `◇ OFFICIAL` No national
figure exists; some banks publish nothing at all.

**One withdrawal**

| | WHO APPLIES IT | SCALES WITH THE AMOUNT? |
|---|---|---|
| Turkish operator fee | the machine's bank — published by some, not all | **yes, where it is a percentage** |
| Your issuer's fee | your bank: fixed, percentage, or both | depends on your issuer |
| Exchange margin | card network and issuer, inside the rate | yes, invisibly |
| Dynamic currency conversion | the terminal's provider, only if accepted | yes |

A percentage and a flat fee respond to withdrawal size in opposite directions.
Which one sits on each layer determines whether taking more per visit helps or
hurts, and the answer is different for different travellers holding different
cards.

### The call

**Establish your own issuer's structure before you leave, then choose the machine
rather than the amount.**

The layer that varies most is attached to the bank whose ATM you happen to be
standing at, and it is the only layer you can change at the moment of withdrawal.
Optimising the amount while ignoring which machine you used is solving the wrong
variable — and it is quietly what *just use ATMs* recommends.

**Then it's yes** → the trip runs through bazaars, small restaurants, non-app
taxis and tips, where cash is the only instrument.

**Then it's no** → cities and hotels on cards throughout, and cash is a small
reserve rather than a running cost.

*How much cash a week consumes is a property of how you travel, not of Turkey.
Bank tariffs, the lira rate and the inflation print are all on the Field Sheet.
None of them belongs in a book.*

## VI · CONNECTIVITY

# Which channel carries the thing that unfreezes your card?

*Whether the message that fixes a refused card can reach you at all.*

A traveller stands at a bazaar stall holding a card the terminal has just
refused, in front of a seller who takes nothing else, forty metres from an ATM
that will refuse the same card for the same reason. The bank that can fix it is
asleep in another time zone. The message it will eventually send is going to a
number switched off at the airport to avoid roaming charges.

Connectivity is the part of a Turkey trip that everyone solves early and nobody
revisits. An eSIM bought before departure lands you connected; coverage is fast
and broad across the cities, Cappadocia and the coast. `●` All of that is true,
and it is exactly why the failure, when it comes, is not a failure of data.

### The reframe

An eSIM buys data. It does not buy your number. Those are two channels, billed
differently and failing differently, and the standard arrangement — home number
on the physical SIM, cheap local data on the eSIM — quietly assumes you keep both
alive. Most travellers switch roaming off entirely, which retires the second one
on the morning they land.

That matters because of what rides on it. Of the ordinary reasons a foreign card
is refused in Turkey, nearly all are decisions or settings at your own bank: a
fraud hold, a daily withdrawal limit, international use not enabled, a wrong PIN,
insufficient funds. `●` The merchant cannot fix any of them and the terminal is
not the problem. Every remedy the issuer has — a verification code, a call-back,
an identity check — travels on the voice-and-text channel, not on data.

Nor is there a figure to look up. What triggers a fraud hold is issuer-specific
risk modelling that no bank publishes, and whether an advance travel notice still
does anything is equally issuer-dependent: some banks have retired the function,
others have not. `●` Your own issuer's current policy is the only authority on
both, and it is readable in your account settings today, in a way it will not be
from a bazaar on a Sunday.

**One traveller, two channels**

| | DATA, ON THE ESIM | YOUR NUMBER, ROAMING |
|---|---|---|
| Maps, translation, ride-hailing | yes | not needed |
| Airline app at a self-connection | yes | not needed |
| The issuer's verification code | no | yes |
| The call-back that lifts a fraud hold | no | yes |
| Typical cost | a fraction of roaming | per-day, which is why it gets switched off |
| If you switch it off | nothing visible fails | the only channel that can unlock the card |

The last row is the one that costs money, and it costs it precisely where a card
was never the fallback: small family lokantas, bazaar stalls, taxis not running
on an app, public toilets, tips.

### The call

**Keep the home number reachable for the whole trip on the cheapest voice-and-text
arrangement your carrier sells, and treat the eSIM as data only — then read your
issuer's card settings before you fly rather than from Turkey.**

The eSIM is the right instrument and the advice to buy one is sound. It simply
answers a different question from the one that strands people. Data failures are
recoverable from any café; a card failure in a cash-only setting, with no way to
receive the code that clears it, is the one that ends an afternoon.

**Then it's yes** → you are carrying one card and one bank; your itinerary
includes bazaars, small restaurants or non-app taxis; you are connecting through
Istanbul on separate tickets and need the airline app on arrival.

**Then it's no** → you carry two cards on two different issuers and have tested
both; your bank authenticates in-app over data rather than by text; your trip is
hotels and card-taking restaurants end to end.

*What a withdrawal costs once it happens is the cash chapter's problem, and the
tariffs are on the Field Sheet. This chapter is about whether the withdrawal
happens at all.*

## VII · OVERLAND

# When is the ground move the safe one rather than the slow one?

*Whether a night on a coach is a cost or a piece of insurance.*

The overland option is presented everywhere as the budget choice, or the scenic
one, or the authentic one. It is filed under how to save money and how to see the
country from a window. It is almost never filed under the only heading that makes
it a real decision on a short trip with a fixed flight home.

Because an overland leg does something no domestic flight can do: it takes the
Istanbul connection out of the itinerary altogether.

### The reframe

Nearly every internal move on a first trip converges on Istanbul. `●` That is the
funnel, and the transit chapter prices what it costs when a connection inside it
fails on separate tickets. The usual response is to buy a longer gap. The move
nobody frames as a response is to remove the connection.

The country's rail network does not offer that everywhere. High-speed rail
radiates from Ankara across the Anatolian plateau; it does not reach Cappadocia,
and it does not reach the Aegean or Mediterranean coasts. `●` For the classic
first-trip triangle it covers at most one leg. The coach network reaches
essentially everywhere the train and the plane do not, which in Turkey is most
places, and on the long corridors it runs overnight.

Then there is the comparison itself, which is almost always made wrong. A flight
looks unanswerable because a seventy-five minute sector is set beside an eleven
hour coach. The sector is not the journey. The journey also contains getting to
one of two airports on opposite sides of Istanbul, a bag-drop cut-off that closes
before departure rather than at it, security, the walk to a gate in a terminal
the size of Istanbul's, reclaiming the bag, and getting from the arrival airport
into the city. Rail and coach arrive in or near city centres and neither has a
security queue. `●`

**One inter-region move, compared honestly**

| | DOMESTIC FLIGHT | OVERNIGHT COACH |
|---|---|---|
| Published sector time | short, and quoted everywhere | long, and quoted everywhere |
| Door to door | sector plus six components | terminal transfer at each end |
| Passes through the funnel | yes | no |
| If the leg fails | your problem on separate tickets | a later departure the same day |
| Costs you a night | no | yes — and returns you a hotel night |
| Where the risk sits | on the international return | nowhere in particular |

The fourth and fifth rows are the trade. Everything above them is the comparison
people actually run; the trade is underneath it.

### The call

**Put the overland leg where the itinerary is tightest — immediately before the
international return — and fly the legs with slack behind them.**

This inverts the instinct, which is to take the coach early while enthusiasm is
high and fly at the end because the end feels important. The end is exactly where
the fixed date sits, and it is the one leg where the funnel can cost the trip
rather than an afternoon.

**Then it's yes** → the last internal move lands within a day of the flight home;
the route has no through-ticket; you were going to lose the evening to an airport
anyway; the corridor is one rail actually serves.

**Then it's no** → the leg crosses the country rather than a region; you are
travelling with people for whom an overnight coach is not restful; the internal
leg already sits on the international ticket, in which case the carrier owns the
risk you would be buying your way out of.

*Journey times vary by operator, route and traffic, and no single official source
publishes them; the Field Sheet names where to look rather than printing a
timetable this book cannot keep true.*
