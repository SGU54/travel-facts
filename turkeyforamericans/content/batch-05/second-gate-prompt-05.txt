----- izmir-guide -----
TITLE: Izmir Travel Guide
<p>Izmir is Turkey's third-largest city and the easygoing capital of the Aegean coast — a palm-lined waterfront, a mild Mediterranean climate, and a famously laid-back, secular feel that surprises Americans expecting another Istanbul. Most travelers use it as the gateway to Ephesus, but the city itself rewards a day or two. Here's how to make the most of it.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Izmir's Kordon waterfront promenade at golden hour, palm trees and the bay, no recognizable faces -->

<h2>How to get there</h2>
<p>Izmir is an easy hop from Istanbul: a domestic flight to <strong>Adnan Menderes Airport (ADB)</strong> takes about an hour, and from there it's roughly an hour's drive south to Selçuk and Ephesus. Turkish Airlines, AnadoluJet/AJet, and Pegasus all fly the route frequently and cheaply. The airport connects to the city center by the İZBAN commuter rail, a taxi, or a Havabus shuttle.</p>

<h2>What to do in Izmir</h2>
<p>Izmir is more about atmosphere than blockbuster sights. Stroll the <strong>Kordon</strong>, the long seafront promenade where locals walk, cycle, and sip tea as the sun sets over the bay. Dive into the <strong>Kemeraltı Bazaar</strong>, a sprawling, atmospheric market that's far less touristy than Istanbul's. Ride the historic elevator at the <strong>Asansör</strong> for a view, and wander the cafe-lined streets of the Alsancak district after dark. History buffs can explore the ancient <strong>Agora</strong> of Smyrna in the city center.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: The covered lanes of Kemeraltı Bazaar in Izmir with stalls and lanterns -->

<h2>Where to stay</h2>
<p>For first-timers, <strong>Alsancak</strong> is the best base — central, walkable to the Kordon, and packed with restaurants and nightlife. The <strong>Konak</strong> area near the bazaar and the iconic Clock Tower puts you in the historic heart. If you're mainly here for Ephesus, consider basing in nearby <strong>Selçuk</strong> instead (see below). Izmir offers strong value across the board thanks to the weak lira, though rates rise in summer, so check current prices and book ahead in peak season.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: hotels — Booking.com CTA for Izmir hotels in Alsancak and Konak -->

<h2>Day trips from Izmir</h2>
<p>This is where Izmir earns its keep as a base. The headline is <strong>Ephesus</strong>, one of the best-preserved ancient cities in the Mediterranean and an easy day trip via Selçuk — see our <a data-internal="ephesus-guide">complete Ephesus guide</a>. Also within reach: the hillside wine village of <strong>Şirince</strong>, the <strong>House of the Virgin Mary</strong> pilgrimage site, and the white travertine terraces of <a data-internal="pamukkale-guide">Pamukkale</a>, though that's a long day better done as an overnight. Beach towns like <strong>Çeşme</strong> on the peninsula make a relaxed summer escape.</p>

<h2>What to eat in Izmir</h2>
<p>The Aegean is Turkey's olive-oil and seafood heartland, and Izmir eats lighter and greener than the interior. Seek out <strong>boyoz</strong>, the city's signature flaky pastry, best with a glass of tea for breakfast. Along the Kordon and in Alsancak, seafood meyhanes serve fresh fish with cold <em>meze</em> built around olive oil, herbs, and vegetables. <strong>Kumru</strong> (a stuffed sesame sandwich) and <strong>gevrek</strong> (the Izmir take on the simit ring) are classic cheap street bites, and the bazaar is full of dried figs, olives, and the region's prized produce.</p>

<h2>Getting around the city</h2>
<p>Izmir is easy to navigate. The metro, tram, and İZBAN commuter rail cover the central districts, and ferries cross the bay between Konak, Karşıyaka, and other neighborhoods — a scenic, cheap way to travel that doubles as a mini-cruise. Pick up an <strong>Izmirim Kart</strong>, the local transit card, to ride them all. The Kordon, Alsancak, and Konak are walkable end to end.</p>

<h2>Money and practical tips</h2>
<p>Like the rest of Turkey, Izmir is excellent value for Americans thanks to the weak lira, though prices rise along the coast in summer — check current rates rather than memorizing a figure. Cards are widely accepted in the city; carry some cash for the bazaar, ferries, and small cafes. Izmir is among Turkey's most relaxed, secular cities, so Western dress is the norm everywhere except when visiting a mosque.</p>

<h2>When to go</h2>
<p>Spring (April–May) and fall (September–October) are ideal: warm, dry, and comfortable for both the city and the nearby ruins. Summer is hot and busy along the coast — fine for the beaches, sweaty for sightseeing at Ephesus, where shade is scarce. Izmir's winters are mild but can be rainy.</p>

----- kusadasi-guide -----
TITLE: Kusadasi & the Ephesus Cruise Port
<p>Kuşadası is Turkey's busiest Aegean cruise port and a lively beach-resort town — for many American travelers, it's the gateway to Ephesus on a Mediterranean cruise itinerary. Whether you're stepping off a ship for the day or basing here for a beach break, here's how to make the most of it.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Kusadasi harbor with cruise ships and the marina, Pigeon Island in the background, no faces -->

<h2>The cruise port and getting to Ephesus</h2>
<p>Kuşadası's port sits right in town, and the main reason most visitors come is the short trip to <strong>Ephesus</strong> — one of the great ancient cities of the Mediterranean, about 30–40 minutes north near Selçuk. If you're on a cruise, you can book a ship excursion or an independent tour; independent guides are usually cheaper and run smaller groups, but make sure any operator guarantees you'll return before the ship departs. Our <a data-internal="ephesus-guide">Ephesus guide</a> covers the site in full, including the extra-ticket Terrace Houses and the House of the Virgin Mary nearby.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: tours — GetYourGuide CTA for a Kusadasi-to-Ephesus shore excursion -->

<h2>What to do in Kusadasi itself</h2>
<p>With time to spare, the town has its own low-key appeal. Walk out to <strong>Pigeon Island (Güvercinada)</strong>, the small fortress-topped islet connected by a causeway that gives the town its name. Browse the harbor-front bazaar (bargaining expected), relax on <strong>Ladies Beach</strong>, or simply enjoy a seafood lunch along the marina. It's a resort town at heart, so expect a holiday atmosphere rather than deep history.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: Pigeon Island fortress connected by causeway at Kusadasi, blue Aegean water -->

<h2>Day trips beyond Ephesus</h2>
<p>If you're staying longer, Kuşadası is well placed for the Aegean's highlights: the hillside wine village of <strong>Şirince</strong>, the white travertine terraces of <a data-internal="pamukkale-guide">Pamukkale</a> (a long day or an overnight), and the <strong>Dil ek Peninsula National Park</strong> for quieter beaches and hiking just south of town.</p>

<h2>Making the most of a day in port</h2>
<p>If you're on a cruise, time is everything. Ephesus deserves at least two to three hours, and the drive each way eats into your window, so a typical port day is Ephesus in the morning (before the midday heat and the biggest crowds) followed by a relaxed harbor lunch and some browsing back in town. If you'd rather skip the ruins, Kuşadası itself fills a half day easily between Pigeon Island, the bazaar, and a beach. Whatever you choose, leave a comfortable buffer before all-aboard time — traffic on the Ephesus road can be unpredictable in peak season.</p>

<h2>Beaches around Kusadasi</h2>
<p>Kuşadası is a beach town at heart. <strong>Ladies Beach</strong> is the closest and liveliest, with sunbeds and cafes; for cleaner water and pine-backed coves, head south to the <strong>Dilek Peninsula National Park</strong>, where a string of quieter beaches sits against a protected mountain backdrop. Boat trips from the harbor also run to swimming spots along the coast in summer.</p>

<h2>Practical tips</h2>
<p>The lira's weakness makes Kuşadası inexpensive for Americans, though the harbor bazaar is firmly tourist-priced — bargaining is expected, so take your time and compare. Cards work in hotels and larger restaurants; carry cash for the market, dolmuş minibuses, and beach cafes. Summer is hot and busy; spring and fall are more comfortable for pairing the beach with Ephesus.</p>

<h2>Should you stay overnight?</h2>
<p>For most travelers, Kuşadası is a stop rather than a destination — a day is enough to combine Ephesus with a wander around the harbor. If you want a beach-resort base for exploring the region, it works well, but for a quieter, more authentic Aegean stay, nearby <strong>Selçuk</strong> or the village of Şirince have more character. See where each fits in our <a data-internal="where-to-stay-aegean">where to stay on the Aegean Coast guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Is Kusadasi too touristy?</h2>
<p>It's worth setting expectations: Kuşadası is unapologetically a resort and cruise town, and on days when several ships are in port the harbor area gets busy and the bazaar can feel pushy. That's the trade-off for its convenience as an Ephesus base. If you came to Turkey for atmosphere and history, treat Kuşadası as a practical hub rather than the highlight — the real magic is at Ephesus and in the quieter Aegean villages inland. If you came for a straightforward beach-and-sun break with easy excursions laid on, it delivers exactly that, and at prices that feel like a bargain to American visitors thanks to the weak lira. Going in with the right frame makes all the difference.</p>

----- where-to-stay-mediterranean -----
TITLE: Where to Stay on the Turkish Riviera
<p>Turkey's Mediterranean coast — the "Turquoise Coast" — runs for hundreds of miles past beaches, pine-covered mountains, and ancient ruins, so where you base yourself shapes the whole trip. From Antalya's atmospheric old town to all-inclusive resorts and laid-back boutique towns, here's how to choose the right stretch of coast and who each suits.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Turquoise Coast view — a beach or bay with pine hills and clear blue water, no recognizable faces -->

<h2>How the coast is laid out</h2>
<p>Before picking a hotel, it helps to picture the geography. Turkey's Mediterranean coast curves for hundreds of miles, and the towns fall into two broad clusters anchored by two airports. The <strong>eastern cluster</strong> around Antalya (served by AYT) holds the main city, the big all-inclusive resort belt, and the great Roman ruins. The <strong>western cluster</strong> around Dalaman (DLM) is more about boutique towns, dramatic beaches, and outdoor adventure. They're a long drive apart — several hours — so most first trips pick one cluster rather than trying to span the whole coast. The four areas below run roughly east to west.</p>

<h2>Antalya (Kaleiçi) — best for old-town charm and a city base</h2>
<p><strong>Kaleiçi</strong>, Antalya's atmospheric Ottoman old town, wraps a restored Roman harbor in cobbled lanes, boutique hotels in converted mansions, and easy access to the wider coast. It's the best all-rounder: you get character, dining, and an airport an hour or less away, plus day trips to ancient sites. The newer beach districts of <strong>Lara</strong> and <strong>Konyaaltı</strong> add long stretches of sand.</p>
<p><strong>Who it's for:</strong> first-timers who want a walkable, characterful base with both city life and the coast on the doorstep.</p>

<h2>Belek, Side &amp; Kemer — best for all-inclusive resorts</h2>
<p>East and west of Antalya, the resort belt of <strong>Belek, Side, and Kemer</strong> is built for the all-inclusive holiday: big beachfront properties, pools, and family amenities, often at prices that astonish American visitors. Side also has genuine ancient ruins right in town. This is the stretch to choose if you want to settle into one resort and barely leave.</p>
<p><strong>Who it's for:</strong> families, couples after a relaxed beach-and-buffet week, and anyone who values value-for-money resort comfort over exploring.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: A relaxed beach-club or resort pool overlooking the Mediterranean on the Turkish Riviera -->

<h2>Kaş — best for laid-back, boutique, and diving</h2>
<p>Farther west, <strong>Kaş</strong> is the antidote to the package-resort belt: a small, bohemian town of whitewashed lanes, bougainvillea, boutique guesthouses, and some of Turkey's best diving and sea kayaking (over the sunken ruins at nearby Kekova). It draws independent travelers rather than tour groups.</p>
<p><strong>Who it's for:</strong> travelers who want personality, outdoor activities, and a quieter, more local feel — and don't need a sprawling resort.</p>

<h2>Fethiye &amp; Ölüdeniz — best for beaches and adventure</h2>
<p>The <strong>Fethiye/Ölüdeniz</strong> hub pairs a working harbor town with one of Turkey's most famous beaches — the Ölüdeniz Blue Lagoon — plus paragliding off Babadağ, gulet cruises, and the Lycian Way hiking trail. It's served by Dalaman Airport (DLM). Base here for an active beach-and-adventure trip.</p>
<p><strong>Who it's for:</strong> active travelers, beach lovers, and anyone planning a <a data-internal="blue-cruise-gulet">Blue Cruise</a> or paragliding.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: hotels — Booking.com CTA for Turkish Riviera hotels by area -->

<h2>Kalkan — a quieter upscale alternative</h2>
<p>Between Kaş and the Fethiye area, <strong>Kalkan</strong> deserves a mention: a steep, pretty harbor town known for boutique villa rentals, rooftop restaurants, and a more grown-up, upscale feel than the big resorts. It pairs well with Kaş for travelers touring the western coast by car, and suits couples and small groups renting a villa for a week.</p>

<h2>What it costs and when to book</h2>
<p>The Turkish Riviera is some of the best beach value in the Mediterranean for Americans, thanks to the weak lira — all-inclusive resorts in particular can cost a fraction of comparable spots in Western Europe. Prices still swing hard by season: July and August are peak and priciest, while late spring and early fall offer warm seas at lower rates. Because the best-located boutique hotels in Antalya's old town and the villas in Kaş and Kalkan are limited in number, book those well ahead for summer. Check live rates rather than relying on a fixed figure, since both demand and the exchange rate move.</p>

<h2>How to pick — and which airport</h2>
<p>Fly into <strong>Antalya (AYT)</strong> for the eastern Med — Antalya city, Belek, Side, Kemer — or <strong>Dalaman (DLM)</strong> for the western towns of Fethiye, Ölüdeniz, Kaş, and Kalkan. For a first trip, Antalya's old town is the easiest, most flexible base; for a resort week choose the Belek/Side/Kemer belt; for boutique calm pick Kaş; for beaches and adventure, Fethiye/Ölüdeniz. The full coast overview is in our <a data-internal="turquoise-coast-guide">Turquoise Coast guide</a>.</p>

----- turquoise-coast-guide -----
TITLE: The Turquoise Coast: A First-Timer's Guide
<p>Turkey's Mediterranean coast — known as the Turquoise Coast for its impossibly blue water — is where ancient ruins meet pine-clad mountains and beach towns string along a dramatic shoreline. It's Turkey at its most relaxed, and a perfect counterweight to the intensity of Istanbul and Cappadocia. Here's what a first-timer needs to know.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Sweeping Turquoise Coast view — a turquoise bay framed by pine-covered mountains, no recognizable faces -->

<h2>Why Americans love it</h2>
<p>For travelers coming from the US, the Turquoise Coast hits a sweet spot that is hard to find at home: Caribbean-clear water and Mediterranean beaches, genuine ancient ruins you can have nearly to yourself, and prices — thanks to the weak lira — that make a comfortable trip remarkably affordable. It is the part of Turkey that feels most like a true vacation rather than a sightseeing marathon, which is why so many itineraries save it for last.</p>

<h2>Where the coast goes</h2>
<p>The Turquoise Coast runs roughly from <strong>Antalya</strong> in the east to <strong>Fethiye</strong> and beyond in the west. Antalya is the main city and eastern gateway, with the atmospheric <strong>Kaleiçi</strong> old town and a Roman harbor; the resort belt of <strong>Belek, Side, and Kemer</strong> stretches nearby. Heading west you reach laid-back <strong>Kaş</strong> and <strong>Kalkan</strong>, then the beaches and adventure hub of <strong>Fethiye and Ölüdeniz</strong>. Our <a data-internal="where-to-stay-mediterranean">where to stay guide</a> breaks down which base suits which trip.</p>

<h2>The best things to do</h2>
<p>The coast blends beach time with genuine history and outdoor adventure:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kaleiçi (Antalya Old Town)</strong> — wander the cobbled lanes and restored Roman harbor.</li>
<li><strong>Ölüdeniz Blue Lagoon</strong> — the iconic beach, with tandem <strong>paragliding</strong> off Babadağ mountain.</li>
<li><strong>Lycian ruins</strong> — ancient sites like Myra, Patara, and Olympos dot the coast, often paired with a beach.</li>
<li><strong>Düden Waterfalls</strong> — falls that plunge dramatically into the sea near Antalya.</li>
<li><strong>Blue Cruise</strong> — a multi-day gulet (wooden boat) sail along the coast; see our <a data-internal="blue-cruise-gulet">Blue Cruise guide</a>.</li>
</ul>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: Ölüdeniz Blue Lagoon from above with paragliders, turquoise water -->

<h2>Getting there and around</h2>
<p>Fly from Istanbul to <strong>Antalya (AYT)</strong>, about 1h15, for the eastern coast, or to <strong>Dalaman (DLM)</strong> for the western towns around Fethiye, Ölüdeniz, Kaş, and Kalkan. A coastal road connects the resort towns, and renting a car is the best way to explore the Lycian coast at your own pace — intercity buses and shared <em>dolmuş</em> minibuses also link the towns. Antalya has a tram in its center.</p>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: tours — GetYourGuide CTA for Turquoise Coast tours: paragliding, boat trips, Lycian ruins -->

<h2>Outdoor adventure on the coast</h2>
<p>Beyond the beaches, the Turquoise Coast is one of Turkey's best outdoor playgrounds. The <strong>Lycian Way</strong>, a long-distance hiking trail, traces the coastline past ruins and coves — you can walk a single scenic stretch without committing to the whole route. <strong>Sea kayaking</strong> over the sunken ruins at Kekova near Kaş is a highlight, as is <strong>scuba diving</strong> in the clear water. Thrill-seekers come for the tandem <strong>paragliding</strong> off Babadağ above Ölüdeniz, regularly rated among the world's best flights. And the multi-day <strong>Blue Cruise</strong> on a wooden gulet is the classic way to reach beaches accessible only by boat.</p>

<h2>What to eat</h2>
<p>Coastal dining means fresh fish and Mediterranean flavors. Seek out seafood <em>meyhanes</em> along the harbors for grilled catch and cold meze, and look for regional specialties as you go — Antalya's <strong>piyaz</strong> (a tahini-dressed white-bean salad) is a local standout. Long, lazy lunches by the water are part of the coast's relaxed rhythm.</p>

<h2>When to go</h2>
<p>The coast has a long season. <strong>Spring (April–May)</strong> and <strong>fall (September–October)</strong> are ideal for combining beach time with sightseeing and hiking — warm seas, comfortable temperatures, fewer crowds. <strong>Summer (June–August)</strong> is peak beach season: hot, busy, and perfect if sand and sea are the goal, less so for long days among the ruins. Winters are mild but quieter, with many resorts winding down.</p>

<h2>Who the Turquoise Coast is for</h2>
<p>The coast suits a wide range of travelers, which is part of its appeal. Beach-and-resort vacationers gravitate to the all-inclusive belt around Antalya for value and ease. Independent and active travelers favor the western towns — Kaş, Kalkan, Fethiye — for diving, hiking, paragliding, and gulet cruises. History lovers can thread ancient Lycian and Roman ruins between swims. And anyone burned out on the intensity of Istanbul will find the coast's slow, sun-soaked pace a relief. The one group that should look elsewhere: travelers chasing big-city culture and nightlife, which the coast offers only in a low-key, seasonal form.</p>

<h2>How it fits a Turkey trip</h2>
<p>The Turquoise Coast pairs naturally with the Aegean and the classic Istanbul–Cappadocia core for a longer trip — see our <a data-internal="10-days-turkey">10-day Turkey itinerary</a>. Even three or four days here makes a relaxing finale after the cities.</p>

TITLE: Antalya Travel Guide
<p>Antalya is the capital of Turkey's Mediterranean coast and the gateway to the Turquoise Coast — a city that pairs a beautifully preserved Ottoman old town with long beaches, Roman ruins, and a backdrop of the Taurus Mountains. It's where many Americans start a coastal trip, and it rewards a couple of days before you head along the shore. Here's the first-timer's rundown.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Antalya's Kaleiçi old harbor with boats and the old town walls above, mountains behind, no faces -->

<h2>How to get there</h2>
<p>Antalya is an easy domestic flight from Istanbul — about 1h15 to <strong>Antalya Airport (AYT)</strong> — with frequent, inexpensive service on Turkish Airlines, AJet, and Pegasus. The airport is about 20–30 minutes from the center, connected by a tram line, airport bus (Havas), or taxi. Antalya is the natural arrival point for the eastern Turquoise Coast.</p>

<h2>A quick orientation</h2>
<p>Antalya is bigger than first-timers expect — a city of over a million spread along the coast — but the part that matters for visitors is compact. <strong>Kaleiçi</strong>, the walled old town, sits on a cliff above the old Roman harbor and holds most of the charm, hotels, and restaurants you'll want. Flanking it are two beach zones: pebbly <strong>Konyaaltı</strong> to the west with mountain views, and sandy <strong>Lara</strong> to the east where the resort hotels cluster. Inland rise the Taurus Mountains, the source of the dramatic day trips and the snowmelt that feeds the city's waterfalls. Keep that simple map in mind — old town, two beaches, mountains behind — and Antalya is easy to navigate.</p>

<h2>Things to do in Antalya</h2>
<p>The heart of the city is <strong>Kaleiçi</strong>, the old town: a maze of cobbled lanes, restored Ottoman mansions, boutique hotels, and a picturesque restored <strong>Roman harbor</strong>. Wander through Hadrian's Gate, browse the bazaars, and stop for tea overlooking the marina. Just outside the center, the <strong>Düden Waterfalls</strong> plunge dramatically into the sea — a memorable, easy excursion. For beaches, the city is flanked by <strong>Konyaaltı</strong> (pebble, with mountain views) and <strong>Lara</strong> (sand, with the big resorts).</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_2: Cobbled lane in Kaleiçi old town with Ottoman houses and bougainvillea -->

<h2>Day trips: ancient ruins</h2>
<p>Antalya is surrounded by some of Turkey's finest classical sites, making it a superb base for history:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Aspendos</strong> — one of the best-preserved Roman theatres anywhere, still used for performances.</li>
<li><strong>Perge</strong> — an extensive ancient city with a colonnaded street and stadium.</li>
<li><strong>Side</strong> — a resort town built around seafront temple ruins.</li>
<li><strong>Termessos</strong> — a dramatic ruined city high in the mountains, for the more adventurous.</li>
</ul>

<!-- AFFILIATE_SLOT: tours — GetYourGuide CTA for Antalya day tours to Aspendos, Perge, and Side -->

<h2>Where to stay</h2>
<p>For first-timers, base in <strong>Kaleiçi</strong> — the old town's boutique hotels put you in the most atmospheric part of the city, walkable to the harbor and bazaars. For a beach-and-resort stay, <strong>Lara</strong> and <strong>Konyaaltı</strong> have the bigger hotels, while the all-inclusive belt of <strong>Belek, Side, and Kemer</strong> spreads out on either side. Our <a data-internal="where-to-stay-mediterranean">Turkish Riviera accommodation guide</a> compares each area. Thanks to the weak lira, Antalya delivers strong value, though prices climb in the summer peak — check current rates and book early.</p>

<h2>What to eat</h2>
<p>Seafood is the coastal star — look for <strong>seafood meyhanes</strong> along the harbor for fresh fish and meze. Don't miss <strong>piyaz</strong>, the local white-bean salad that's an Antalya specialty, and round off a meal with Turkish tea overlooking the water.</p>

<h2>Getting around Antalya</h2>
<p>The old town of Kaleiçi is compact and walkable — you'll explore it on foot. A modern <strong>tram</strong> connects the center with the Konyaaltı beach and museum area, and the nostalgic old tram trundles along the cliff-top. For the ancient-site day trips, the easiest options are a rental car, a guided tour, or intercity buses from the otogar (bus station); Aspendos and Perge are common combined excursions. Taxis are metered — insist on the meter.</p>

<h2>When to go</h2>
<p>Antalya enjoys one of Turkey's longest, sunniest seasons. <strong>Spring (April–May)</strong> and <strong>fall (September–October)</strong> are the sweet spots, with warm seas and comfortable temperatures for both beach time and exploring the ruins, which offer little shade. <strong>Summer</strong> is hot and busy — ideal for a pure beach holiday, harder for sightseeing. Even <strong>winter</strong> is mild and quiet, when you can pair the old town with snow-capped mountain views just inland.</p>

<h2>How many days?</h2>
<p>Two days covers the old town, a beach afternoon, and the Düden Waterfalls; add a third for an ancient-ruins day trip to Aspendos and Perge. From here, the rest of the <a data-internal="turquoise-coast-guide">Turquoise Coast</a> opens up to the west.</p>
