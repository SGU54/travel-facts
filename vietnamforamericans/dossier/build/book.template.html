The contract

What this book is for

What this book is forThe contract

A first trip to Vietnam is not hard. It is long, and the decisions that shape it are made in the wrong order.

Vietnam is among the easier countries an American can travel in. The State Department places it at its lowest advisory level, the food is excellent at every price, the internal flights are cheap and frequent, and the people are warmer to American visitors than most first-timers expect. Almost nothing about the trip is difficult. What it is, instead, is long — a thousand miles from the first city to the last, running three climates at once — and the length turns a dozen small decisions into one sequence, where each choice quietly closes options for the next.

This book exists because that sequence is where the days go. The month you can travel decides which end of the country is in season. The international ticket decides which end you land at, and it is usually bought first. The e-visa form, filled in weeks earlier, has already fixed how long you can stay and which gate you may leave through. The overnight cruise you booked because it was on every list has claimed two days of a northern leg that had four. None of that is difficult. It is simply not answerable one article at a time, which is exactly how it is usually answered.

What we do and do not claim

Every call in here is argued in the open, with the arithmetic on the page, so that you can disagree with us and still come out ahead. That is the contract. Where we say fly, we show the hours that say fly; where the call is close, we say it is close. A recommendation you cannot audit is worth nothing to someone spending four thousand dollars, and the number of places where a confident voice is repeating a decade-old blog post is high in Vietnam — the rail pass that no longer exists, the visa exemption that was never for Americans, the overnight train to Sapa that the expressway overtook. ●

We do not tell you which hotel to book or which restaurant to eat at. Those are the two things every guide leads with and the two that age fastest. The back of this book says plainly what it refuses to do, and why.

This book is built from a hundred articles published on vietnamforamericans.com — the same desk, not an outside authority. The book is built to stay true for about two years and contains no live prices, because a price printed in a book is a lie with a long shelf life. Every volatile figure (the visa fee, the processing window, next year's Tết dates, the fares) lives in the Field Sheet, a short dated companion reissued every quarter. When the two disagree, the Sheet is right and the book is old. Read the book once before you book anything; carry the Sheet on the trip.

The ground

One line, three climates

One line, three climatesThe ground

The single fact that reorganises a first trip to Vietnam, and the reason the usual question has no answer.

Ask when to visit Vietnam and you will be given a month. The answer is honest for somebody and useless for you, because the question has no answer. The country runs about a thousand miles from the Chinese border to the Gulf of Thailand along a single north–south spine, and at any given moment it is running three different climates. A month that is perfect in one third is the worst of the year in another. ●

The north (Hanoi, the bay, Ninh Bình, Sapa) has four real seasons, including a winter that arrives as a persistent grey drizzle in the fifties and sixties Fahrenheit and catches out everyone who packed for Southeast Asia in the abstract. The centre (Huế, Đà Nẵng, Hội An) is driest from February to August and then spends September to November under the storms that flood Hội An's riverside streets in most years. The south (Saigon, the delta, the islands) is warm all year and divides into dry and wet rather than hot and cold, the wet arriving as a heavy afternoon hour rather than a washed-out day. ●

Put the three side by side and the shape of the problem is visible at once. No month is green at both ends. March and April compromise least, so they are busy. October is the north's best month and the centre's worst, in the same week. December is bright in the south and cold in the north. Whatever month you have, one third of the line is in its difficult season, and the trip will cross it.

Why that decides the direction

The classic first trip runs the length of the line in one direction — Hanoi to Saigon or the reverse — with an internal flight or two and the Huế–Đà Nẵng coast by train. Direction feels like the first creative decision, and it is mostly not a decision at all: it is the residue of three things settled elsewhere. {{claim:nonstop}} So a West Coast traveller who wants the nonstop starts in the south whether they intended to or not. Open-jaw tickets, into one end and out of the other, are widely available and cost little more than a return, so the backtrack is never necessary. And the month has an opinion: the weather-sensitive end should come first, while there are still days to move. ●

By the time anyone asks themselves which way to go, the airline map and the calendar have usually answered it. The useful move is to notice that earlier, and to treat the international booking as the moment the route is chosen rather than a separate errand done in a lunch hour.

What else the line decides

Once the country is a line rather than a map, most of the remaining decisions get easier, and several of them stop being decisions at all. The long legs sort themselves by distance: fly the thousand-kilometre hops, take the train on the one stretch of coast where the train is the point, and never fly the hundred kilometres between Huế and Đà Nẵng. The excursions off the line (the bay, Sapa, the delta, the islands) each cost a known number of days, and the days come out of somewhere. And the e-visa, which names the gate you will enter and the gate you will leave by, has to be filled in for the line you will travel, because it cannot be corrected once you are on it. ◇

Everything that follows is that single fact, worked out. Eleven decisions, each with the arithmetic on the page; the operations a first trip runs; and the failures, sorted by what they cost. The country is better than any plan you can make of it. The plan's job is to leave you free for that.

Vietnam asks more of an American traveller in advance than Japan or Western Europe do, and the items with the longest horizons are the ones that look most like details. {{claim:visa-required}} That single fact puts a government working-day clock in front of everything else, and the clock stops for a week at Tết.

The visa deserves the first slot because it is the only item that can refuse you. {{claim:visa-processing}} The counter-evidence is real — Vietnam's own embassy in India publishes a far wider range — so the lower figure is a floor, not a promise, and non-refundable flights should not be bought against it. When the document arrives, read every field against the passport character by character; a mismatched passport number or date of birth can mean being refused on arrival, with an expensive airport sponsorship as the only remedy. The passport, specifically

{{claim:passport-6m}} The State Department's own page is internally inconsistent on the page count, saying one blank page per stamp in one section and at least two in another, so treat two as the working minimum. One trap worth knowing if things go wrong mid-trip: Vietnam may not accept the twelve-page US emergency passport for e-visa entry, so a passport replaced at an embassy elsewhere in the region may not get you in. ◇

The clinic

{{claim:vaccines}} Hepatitis A and typhoid are both food-and-water borne, which is the relevant route in a country where the best eating is done at street stalls. Most visitors do not need antimalarials, which contradicts the blanket advice given about Southeast Asia: {{claim:malaria}} Sapa, Hà Giang and the central highlands are not covered by that list, and a trip built around trekking changes the calculation — a question for a clinic with the actual route in front of it, not for this page. Dengue is the mosquito risk with no vaccine; it is urban, seasonal, and bites by day, so repellent is not a jungle concern.

The first rule is about documents, not nationality: {{claim:same-passport}} A family that enters on Vietnamese passports and tries to leave on American ones, or the reverse, has created the problem at the booking stage. Decide which passport the trip runs on before a single flight is bought, and use it for every domestic sector too, because the airlines check. ◇

The children are where families are caught. {{claim:child-exit}} {{claim:minor-consent}} A grandparent taking a grandchild to the village, or one parent travelling alone with the children, carries the other parent's notarised consent or proof of sole custody; the question of what a particular child needs in order to leave goes to the Vietnamese consulate before booking, not to the US Embassy, which cannot answer it. For families who will go back more than once there is a standing alternative to the e-visa: {{claim:exemption-paper}} It replaces a ninety-day visa bought trip by trip with something closer to a permission, and it is applied for at a Vietnamese consulate in the United States. ◇

What does not need doing

You do not need an International Driving Permit, which Vietnam does not recognise. You do not need cash in advance beyond a modest reserve of clean dollars for emergencies; the airport exchange counter is the worst rate of the trip. You do not need day tours, cooking classes, drivers or food walks booked from abroad — they are arranged in the country a day or two ahead, usually more cheaply and with better information about the weather. And you do not need a packing list for one climate: a trip that touches Hanoi or Sapa between November and March wants real layers, a shell, and shoes that come off easily at temple thresholds. ●

Day zero

The first six hours

The first six hoursDay zero

Nobody writes the arrival day, which is the one where a plan is most easily lost.

You land after twenty-two to twenty-six hours in transit, a full twelve hours out of step with your body in winter and eleven in summer, on a calendar day that is not the one you left on. What happens next is decided almost entirely by three things chosen months earlier: which airport, which direction, and what time the wheels touch. None of them felt like an itinerary decision when the ticket was bought. All of them are.

The arrival day is a write-off, and the trips that go wrong are the ones that refuse to admit it. A six a.m. domestic flight or a cruise transfer on the morning after landing is how two days are lost instead of one. Plan a slow afternoon, daylight, Vietnamese meal times from the first meal, a nap of no more than half an hour, and an early night. Hanoi's Old Quarter absorbs a jet-lagged wander well; Saigon in the heat is harder going in that state, which is a small argument for landing north that most people never weigh. ●

At the counter

Have the printed e-visa with the passport, the first night's address written down, and the pre-arrival declaration filed within the three days before. Then the one check that is still fixable at home and not at the desk: the gate named on the visa against the airport on the ticket. {{claim:visa-port}} Saigon's immigration hall is the busiest in the country and its peak queues are long; the smaller international airports (Đà Nẵng, Cam Ranh, Phú Quốc) are quick and far less stressful, which is a reason to route through one of them if the trip is regional rather than country-wide. ◇

Into the city

Grab is the simplest answer at all three main airports and the fare is fixed before you accept, which removes the inflated-meter conversation that airports everywhere are famous for. At Saigon the pickup is on the upper departures level rather than at arrivals, which catches people out; follow the signs up and walk past whoever offers a car at the kerb. At Hanoi the pickup is signposted a short walk from the hall, and the 86 express bus runs to the Old Quarter for a fraction of the price in about an hour. Đà Nẵng is minutes from its own centre and forty-five minutes to an hour from Hội An, and at that distance a car arranged by the hotel is worth the small premium after a long flight. ●

Change only enough at the airport counter to reach the hotel — the rate is the worst of the trip — and use the ATM in the arrivals hall for one large withdrawal rather than several small ones. A SIM counter in the hall needs the passport for registration; airport wifi is enough to call the first Grab if the connectivity can wait until town.

If you are connecting onward

An international arrival followed by a domestic flight means clearing immigration, collecting the bags, and checking in again at a separate domestic terminal. Budget three hours between the two at either Hanoi or Saigon, more around the holidays. The same asymmetry runs in reverse at the end of the trip: {{claim:exit-control}} The last night of the trip belongs near the departure airport, not two hours away behind a mountain road or on a boat whose sailing the port authority decides. ◇

The rhythm

How a day in Vietnam runs

How a day in Vietnam runsThe rhythm

The day has a shape here, and fighting it costs you the two hours that matter most — and lunch.

Vietnam gets up early and it gets up to eat. Food markets do their real business between six and nine; the famous phở shop is out of broth by mid-morning and shut; bún chả is lunch and gone by early afternoon; the bia hơi corners fill from late afternoon and have sold the day's supply by nine or ten. A visitor who keeps Western hours meets the country in its dead middle and concludes that the famous place must have closed. ●

The sights run on the same clock, for a different reason. Mỹ Sơn, the Marble Mountains, the Imperial City, the Golden Bridge in its cloud, the Ancient Town of Hội An — each is a different place at opening and at eleven, because the coaches from the resorts all arrive on the same schedule. The gap between seven and nine in the morning is the difference between a site you will remember and a queue you will photograph, and it costs nothing but a decision made the night before.

The heat is the other half of the rhythm. Saigon in July regularly makes the mid-nineties feel like well over a hundred, and midday sightseeing stops being enjoyable. The local day adjusts for it — out early, a long flat middle indoors, out again after four — and copying that rhythm makes summer travel far more pleasant than fighting it. A hotel with a pool stops being a luxury in the south and becomes the thing that makes the afternoon work. ●

The two hours worth protecting

Build each day around one early anchor and let the rest be loose. The first-timer's instinct is four sights moved between efficiently; the better shape is one thing at opening, an unhurried middle, and an evening allowed to be unremarkable. Vietnam rewards the loose afternoon more than most countries, because the density of small good things — a market alley, a pagoda with nobody in it, a counter that sells one dish — means walking produces finds that planning cannot. The alarm that matters is the five o'clock one, two or three times in a trip: for the floating market, the Ancient Town before the sweepers, the bay before the day boats return.

A first trip converges on a small number of unavoidable set pieces, and they are unavoidable for good reasons. The bay

The day boats work the busiest water in the middle of the day. The overnight buys the two windows they miss — the late afternoon after they have gone and the early morning before they are back — plus a kayak from the back of the boat, which is the only vantage at which the scale of the limestone registers. Ask which bay the itinerary covers, because operators sell all three under one name: Hạ Long is the busiest, Bái Tử Long to the north-east sees fewer permits, and Lan Hạ off Cát Bà carries the same scenery with a fraction of the boats. Drinks are almost never included; cabin count changes the trip more than the decor does; and the cancellation decision belongs to the port authority. ●

The pass

It is worth saying what the train is like, because it is the first thing that tells a visitor the country can be seen slowly. The line leaves Huế through paddies and fishing villages, runs along the lagoon at Lăng Cô, and climbs the spur with the sea on one side and the mountain on the other while the road above it is sitting in cloud. It costs very little, takes about three hours, and replaces a transfer you were making anyway. A car over the pass with stops is a half-day and the choice for luggage and company; an easy-rider pillion trip is the day itself. The tunnel beneath sees none of it and is the default for through traffic, so say which you want when you book. ●

The town

Hội An is described as magical and as a tourist trap, and both descriptions are of the same streets at different hours. Before seven the Ancient Town belongs to people sweeping and buying breakfast; through the middle of the day and into the evening it is a near-continuous retail frontage; after nine the day visitors have thinned and the lanterns are the light. On the fourteenth day of the lunar month the electric lighting goes off altogether. The base that puts you inside the town for those hours is the whole of Decision 07; the tailor, with its two fittings, is the reason the town wants two full days. ●

The war

For most American visitors these are the places where the war stops being history and becomes a room you are standing in, and the order they arrive in matters. Travelling north to south you meet Hanoi's sites first (Hỏa Lò, the B-52 in the lake), then the DMZ out of Huế, where Vịnh Mốc's tunnels were dug for a village to live in and are close to their original dimensions, and only at the end the War Remnants Museum and Củ Chi in Saigon. That progression puts the heaviest material last rather than on the second day. The museum presents a national account and does not attempt American balance; the reception, by most accounts, is ordinary courtesy. Go early, go first, and schedule nothing demanding after it. It is called the American War here, and listening serves better than explaining. ●

Hanoi

The Old Quarter is the right default for a first visit: the lake a few minutes away, the food at the door, the thing you came to see all around you. The cost is noise — scooters from before six, the bar strip on Tạ Hiện until late — and the fix is a room at the back, above the second floor, after checking on a map how many streets separate the hotel from that corner. The French Quarter is ten to fifteen minutes on foot, close enough to walk in and far enough to sleep, and the right answer for a second visit or anyone noise-sensitive. Tây Hồ is leafy and pleasant and genuinely far: every outing is a fifteen-minute Grab, which on three nights of sightseeing eats the trip. In winter, check the room has heating as well as air conditioning; Hanoi's damp cold catches hotels out too. ●

Saigon

District 1 is the centre and the default, and which part of it matters: Đồng Khởi for the grander hotels, Bến Thành for the street food, and the Phạm Ngũ Lão backpacker quarter at the western end, where Bùi Viện runs as a bar street until well past midnight and should be chosen deliberately rather than on price. District 3 is immediately north-west, leafier and better value, and the sensible choice for four nights or more. Most north-to-south trips end here, which argues for a comfortable room: a good bed at the end of two weeks is worth more than the same bed at the start. ●

Two checks everywhere

Ask which floor the room is on and whether there is a lift (many Old Quarter buildings are tall, narrow and stair-only), and whether the room has a window, which a surprising number of cheap rooms do not and the price does not signal. And remember that the hotel will take your passport at check-in: {{claim:police-reg}} That is the rule working, not a scam — and the statute is narrower than the desk's habit: {{claim:host-reg}} A photocopy and a look are what the declaration needs; if a hotel wants to keep the passport overnight, ask for it back once the form is filed.

The plan

The days, as operations

The days, as operationsThe plan

Not an itinerary. Four shapes a day can take, the fourteen-day spine most first trips run on, and the joints where it is won or lost.

An itinerary tells you where to be at eleven o'clock. An operation tells you what kind of day you are running, which is more useful, because the specific pagoda matters far less than whether the day has one anchor or four. Four shapes work, and the mistake is almost always treating a transfer day as a free one.

The fourteen-day spine

Fly into Hanoi, out of Saigon, open-jaw; two internal flights, Hanoi to Huế or Đà Nẵng and Đà Nẵng to Saigon, with the Huế–Đà Nẵng leg by train. Three nights in Hanoi, one of them written off to arrival. The northern excursion — Ninh Bình for most first visits, Sapa if the mountains are the point. One night on the bay, then the evening flight south. Two nights in Huế for the Imperial City, two tombs and, if the war is the interest, the DMZ. The train over the pass. Two or three nights in Hội An, ordering clothes on the first morning. Fly down for two nights in Saigon, the museum first and early, Chợ Lớn in the late afternoon, and the last morning near the airport. Run it backwards if the flights point south-first; at this length the direction matters less than at seven days, because two weeks absorbs a mistake. ●

Ten days is the same spine with Sapa, Huế as a stop and the delta cut; seven days is two adjacent regions and one internal flight, and the question is only which region to give up. Three weeks is the fortnight plus what it cuts (both northern excursions, Huế properly, the Cần Thơ night), and not a fourth region or a second country.

The joints

Look at the joints rather than the days. The cruise's return time decides whether the flight south is that evening or the next morning, which decides whether the Huế day starts in Huế or in an airport. Huế before Hội An turns the pass into a scenic train ride; after it, the same journey is backtracking. A Hội An stay that ends with a morning flight from Đà Nẵng wants the last night in Đà Nẵng, not an hour away. Each choice is small and each sets the terms of the next, which is what distinguishes a fortnight that works from one with the same list of places in a different order. ●

Jet lag, honestly

Twelve hours is the maximum offset there is, so there is no gentle version of the adjustment and the first two days are compromised whatever you do. Plan them as neighbourhood days with nothing that cannot be abandoned, get daylight, eat at Vietnamese hours from the first meal, and do not schedule the thing you most want to see on day one. A trip that front-loads its highlights spends them on a version of itself that is not fully present.

What one full day covers is the Imperial City before the heat and two of the three tombs that tours circuit — Tự Đức and Khải Định are the usual pair, Minh Mạng the formal third — with Thiên Mụ on the river between them. It is brisk and it is not rushed. The single night that most templates give Huế buys an afternoon, which means the citadel or the tombs done properly and not both; it works, and it is why so many people report Huế as fine, which is the sound of a place seen at half strength. The second full day is for the DMZ or for the food, and rarely both. ●

The pool itself is simple to state. On a fortnight the usual split is two nights in Huế and three in Hội An; on ten days, one and three or two and two; on a week the centre has three nights in total and the choice is Hội An plus a Huế day from Đà Nẵng, or Huế plus the train and a single Hội An night. Huế before Hội An turns the pass into a scenic train ride; after it, the same journey is backtracking (Decision 05). ●

Phong Nha

Phong Nha-Ke Bàng, the cave park north of Huế, has gone from a backpacker secret to a fixture, and it draws on the same pool. Its caves fall into three bands that the tour boards do not separate: the show caves — the river boat into Phong Nha cave, the lit walkways of Paradise Cave — which need shoes and nothing else; the adventure caves, a day or two of walking, swimming and kayaking with a licensed operator, Hang Én's sandy camp inside the cave the best of them; and Sơn Đoòng, a six-day expedition in a category of its own that is a trip to plan a holiday around, not a stop to add to one. Its price lives on the Field Sheet. The cost to a first itinerary is about four days — a day in from Huế or Hanoi, two in the park, a day out — and on a fortnight those days come out of the central coast or the delta, which is the trade to make deliberately. The cheap day trips sold out of Huế reach the show caves, rushed, and spend most of the day on the road. The park's rivers rise in the central coast's autumn and the adventure caves close when they flood, so the dry months are the window. ●

Eating

The rule that outlives the list

The rule that outlives the listEating

Vietnam is one of the easiest countries in the world to eat well in and one of the easiest to eat badly in — by going to the wrong kind of place at the wrong hour.

Every guide to Vietnam opens its food chapter with names. The names are the part that expires. What does not expire is the structure of eating here — which kinds of places exist, which dish belongs to which hour and which region, and how you get into them — and that structure is unusually legible once someone draws it for you.

Three regions, three cuisines

The single most useful thing to understand is that the country cooks differently as you travel it. The north (Hanoi and the Red River delta) cooks with restraint: less sugar, less chilli, more black pepper and herbs, clear broths; phở and bún chả come from here, and northern phở is the plainer original, with no basil plate and no hoisin. The centre (Huế, Hội An, Đà Nẵng) is the spiciest and most elaborate, with a court cuisine behind it: bún bò Huế, the little steamed cakes, cao lầu, mì Quảng. The south is sweeter, uses coconut milk, and has the greatest abundance of fresh herbs; southern phở arrives with a plate of them that a northerner would call excessive. If the route covers the country, eat each dish where it belongs. ●

The hour each dish keeps
DishWhen, and the tell of a good one
Phở ●breakfast; clear broth, a shop that sells nothing else, a large pot at the front, closed when the pot is finished
Bún chả ●Hanoi, lunch; charcoal you can smell from the street, not a flat-top
Bánh mì ●any hour; bread audibly crisp and light, pickles and herbs not optional
Cơm tấm, cơm bình dân ●lunch and late night; the point-and-choose canteen is how the country eats at noon
Bia hơi ●late afternoon to nine; brewed that morning, sold out by night

The rule

Specialisation beats range. A place with one dish written on the sign, a queue at the right hour, and a cook who has made the same thing for years will almost always be better than a restaurant with a laminated menu of forty items and air conditioning. Plastic stools on the pavement are not a lower tier; they are often the top one. The sit-down places with English menus are generally aimed at visitors and priced for them. And eat what the place sells: asking a bún chả shop for phở is how you get the worst version of both. ●

The hour is the second half of the rule, and the half most often broken. Many specialist places sell one meal and close. Turning up at two in the afternoon expecting the famous place to be open is the commonest way to miss it, and eating on the Vietnamese rhythm — a hot savoury breakfast out of the house, rice at noon, coffee in the afternoon, the grilled food and the beer late — is the single easiest way to find the good places open.

Safety, without the fear

Street food carries a real risk of stomach illness, which is the commonest thing that goes wrong on a trip here, and avoiding the category means missing most of the country's best eating. The sensible position sits between. The vaccinations are decided before you travel — Hepatitis A and typhoid are both food-and-water borne, and they are the part within your control. On the ground the principles are the ones Vietnamese people use: turnover, cooked to order in front of you, raw and cooked kept apart. A queue is evidence, not proof. Tap water is not for drinking anywhere; the uniform cylindrical ice with a hole through it is the factory kind, and irregular chipped ice is the kind to be wary of. Raw herbs are the genuine grey area, washed in whatever water the stall has, and they are also a structural part of most dishes; higher-turnover places are the lower-risk end of a range, not a safe one. Carry rehydration salts, which assume the plan can fail. ●

What to book

One thing, early: a street-food walk on the first evening in whichever city you reach first, in a small group, with a guide who eats at the stops. A tour at the end of a trip is a nice meal; a tour at the start is a set of tools you then use on every meal after it. A cooking class is a different half-day, and the test of a good one is the market visit — forty-five minutes buying what you will cook, or ten minutes for photographs before the minibus. Everything else is better found than scheduled, because the upside sits in exactly the places that take no reservation from abroad.

Coffee is its own chapter and needs four words: đá iced, nóng hot, sữa with condensed milk, đen black. The default is cà phê sữa đá; egg coffee is a Hanoi invention that tastes like liquid tiramisu; the robusta carries roughly twice the caffeine of what you are used to, so two in an afternoon is more than it sounds. The free glass of weak iced tea beside it is there to drink between sips. ●

The one that puts people in hospital

Road traffic, not crime, is what injures visitors, and the gap between the two is not close. The roads are crowded, most people are on motorbikes, and a tourist on a rented scooter with no local riding experience is the highest-exposure person on them. Two things compound it, and they are the whole of Decision 04: the licence you hold is invalid, and without a valid one the insurer may decline the bill. Crossing the street on foot, by contrast, is a skill acquired in a day — step off at a slow, constant, predictable pace and keep looking, because the technique improves the odds and does not guarantee that every rider has seen you; wait out anything with four wheels. ●

The one that takes your phone

Violent crime against foreigners is rare. Losing a phone is not. It leaves your hand while you stand on a pavement reading a map, and the bike is gone before you have registered it; Saigon is where this is most common. The defence is unglamorous: do not walk and scroll, stop with your back to a wall to look at anything, keep bags on the side away from the traffic, and keep the camera out of sight on the street. In nightlife districts the ordinary precautions apply — watch the drink being poured, keep it with you. {{claim:emergency}} They are frequently answered only in Vietnamese; for anything serious in Hanoi or Saigon, the international hospitals are the route, and they expect payment up front. ◇

The one nobody expects

{{claim:drugs}} For a traveller coming from a state where cannabis is legal that is the mechanism to understand, because it does not require possession. What makes it weigh more than it otherwise would is the aftermath: consular access to detained Americans is routinely delayed, often by several weeks, so the embassy may not reach you quickly. Carry any prescription in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the prescription, and check anything you depend on before you fly rather than at the border. ◇

The one in the glass

Most of the drinking in Vietnam is beer, and beer is not the problem. {{claim:methanol}} The mechanism is ordinary fraud — a known label refilled with cheap distilled alcohol, or a batch of local rice liquor distilled badly — and the exposure is the open bottle: cocktails, shots and free-pour measures in places whose supply chain is nobody's reputation, which is why the cases cluster in the backpacker streets and the beach resorts. The rule that follows costs nothing: beer or sealed wine by default; spirits only in an established hotel bar or restaurant; a bottle for a party from a supermarket, never a corner shop; and nothing that arrives in a bucket or at a price that could not have bought a genuine measure. The symptoms start the next day and look like a hangover, and the sign that separates them is the eyes — blurred or snowy vision is a hospital, not sleep and water. ◇

Air

The State Department flags air pollution as a significant problem in the major cities, Hanoi above all, and the worst stretch overlaps the cool dry months that are otherwise the north's best. That is not a reason to avoid Hanoi in winter, but if you are asthmatic or travelling with someone who is, it belongs in the season decision, and the reading to use is a live one on the day rather than any figure printed in an article or in this book. ◇

How many days do you really have, and how many regions do they buy?

Days — How many days do you really have, and how many regions do they buy?Decision 01 of 11

A fortnight off work is not a fortnight in Vietnam. The crossing from the East Coast runs twenty-two to twenty-six hours each way, the arrival day is lost to a body twelve hours out of step, and every change of region costs half a day in transfers. By the time those are subtracted, the itinerary most people draft (three regions, two excursions, a beach) belongs to a trip about a week longer than the one they booked.

The subtraction · counted from the day you leave home

10 days of leave ●2 regions, 1 internal flight, no overnight excursion

The arithmetic is unforgiving because none of its terms is optional. {{claim:timezone}} The westbound flight adds to that offset, so a Monday evening departure lands on Wednesday morning; the day between went into the crossing and does not come back until the return. Two weeks of leave is twelve days of Vietnam, and the first of those twelve is written off before it starts.

Within what remains, the unit that matters is the move. Each region is about an hour's flight from the next, which sounds like nothing and is in fact a hotel checkout, a transfer, a domestic terminal, the flight, and a transfer at the far end — most of a day gone, with one easy thing possible at the other end of it. Three regions in ten days is two of those days. Add an overnight excursion in the north, which claims two days of its own, and the centre has shrunk to a single night before anyone has argued about it. ●

The call

Count the days on the ground first, then pick adjacent regions, never the two ends. Seven days on the ground is two regions done well or three done badly; ten is the first length at which all three fit; fourteen is the length the country is built for. North plus centre, or centre plus south — the north-and-south pairing skips the middle, which is where Huế and Hội An are, and it is the combination people most often regret. Write the plan out and count the half-days lost to movement: if more than a quarter of the trip is transfers, remove a region rather than compress the stays.

When the other call is right
  • then take three → thirteen days of leave or more, one northern excursion, Huế as a day
  • then take the ends → a returning visitor who has already done the centre
  • then stretch to Cambodia → three weeks, not two: Angkor needs three days plus a day at each end
When ours is wrong for you
  • then take two → ten days of leave; the third region is the one you will remember as airports
  • then take two → travelling with children, where fewer places and longer stays is the whole adjustment
  • then subtract one → the list already has Sapa and the delta on it and the leave is under three weeks

Ask when to visit Vietnam and the internet answers with a month, which only works if the trip stays in one region. It will not. The classic first trip runs the length of a country that is running three climates at once, so the month you choose decides which end is in season, which end carries the risk, and therefore which direction the trip should run. Most people settle the direction first, by buying the flight, and consult the weather afterwards.

The reason this is a decision and not a lookup is that the regions do not share a calendar. The north has four seasons including a winter that surprises everyone who packed for Southeast Asia in the abstract; the centre is driest from February to August and then spends September to November under the storms that flood Hội An's riverside streets in most years; the south is warm all year and divides into dry and wet rather than hot and cold. October is the clearest month on Hạ Long Bay and one of the worst on the Hải Vân Pass, in the same week. ●

Direction then follows from three things that are usually settled before anyone asks the question. The only US nonstop lands in Saigon, so a traveller who needs it starts south. Open-jaw tickets into one end and out of the other cost little more than a return and remove the backtrack entirely. And jet lag argues for landing somewhere that absorbs a slow first day — Hanoi's Old Quarter does; Saigon in the heat does not. When those three cancel out, start in the harder city with fresh energy and finish in the one with the most flights home.

The call

Let the month choose the direction, and put the slack where the storm is. If the dates are yours, take March or April and the direction barely matters. If they are not, build around the region your month favours and run the line so that the weather-sensitive end comes first, while you still have days to move. An autumn trip meets the central coast's storm season whichever way it runs, so the answer there is not a direction but a spare half-day kept in the centre and refundable rooms near the river. The one hard constraint is the airline map: {{claim:nonstop}} A West Coast traveller who wants the nonstop is starting in the south whether they intended to or not.

When the other call is right
  • then go south-first → the San Francisco nonstop is what makes the fare work
  • then go south-first → December to February, when the south is dry and the north is cold; end in Hanoi's mist, do not start in it
  • then go north-first → September to November, taking the bay and Sapa while they are clear and keeping slack for the centre
When ours is wrong for you
  • then forget direction → March or April, when every region is workable and the order is a tiebreaker
  • then forget direction → the trip is the centre only; fly in and out of Đà Nẵng and there is no line to run
  • then move the dates → the plan is beach-led and the only month available is September or October

{{claim:visa-90}} The premise most people arrive with is that a visa can be adjusted later, because in most countries it can. {{claim:visa-noext}} The Immigration Department allows in-person extensions for other categories (sponsored business visas, work-linked visas, residence cards), and the electronic tourist visa is not among them. Agencies advertising an e-visa extension are selling an exit and re-entry, or a different visa through a sponsor, under a name the government says does not exist.

The gate rules are two rules wearing one coat, and both must be satisfied. {{claim:visa-gates}} Flying in is therefore never the problem. The second rule is the binding one. {{claim:visa-port}} Overland and on the river is where both bite, because only some land crossings accept an e-visa at all, and the one you intend to use has to be the one you named. The Châu Đốc boat to Phnom Penh, the Mộc Bài bus, a return through either — each is a line on the form, filled in before the route existed. ◇

The river exit has a second form behind it, and it is Cambodia's. {{claim:cambodia-evisa}} The boat still runs and the post still stamps; the document it stamps has to have come from a consulate or the arrival counter rather than the Cambodian website, and that is settled in the same week as the Vietnamese form, not on the jetty at Châu Đốc.

The call

Settle the shape of the trip, then apply — never the other way round. Decide first whether Cambodia or Laos is an exit, a loop, or absent; whether you land north or south; and how many days the ground will hold with a buffer at both ends. Only then fill in the form, on the official site, asking for the duration you need rather than the three weeks you first guessed. {{claim:visa-fee}} {{claim:visa-processing}} Apply several weeks out and do not hold non-refundable flights against the lower figure.

When the other call is right
  • then take multiple entry → Vietnam, then Cambodia, then Vietnam again, usually because of a return flight that cannot be changed
  • then name a land gate → the Mekong boat or the Mộc Bài bus is the exit, and the visa must say so; the river exit also needs a Cambodian visa that is not the Cambodian e-visa
  • then skip the visa → Phú Quốc only, thirty days or fewer, arriving and leaving the island directly and never clearing the mainland
When ours is wrong for you
  • then keep it single → one entry, one exit, both by air, which is most first trips
  • then ask for more days → the plan is loose and the multiple-entry premium is the cheapest flexibility in the trip
  • then do it earlier → the dates fall near Tết, when the working-day clock stops for a week

Do not rent.   • then take the easy-rider → Hà Giang or the pass is the reason you came; the road is the day, not the transfer
  • then take a Grab → it is a city, it is hot, and the distance is more than a mile

The distance rule holds because the airports eat the short hops. Add two transfers, check-in and security to a one-hour flight and a leg like Huế to Nha Trang is four hours door to door against a train that leaves from the middle of town. At the other end the arithmetic inverts: {{claim:han-sgn-rail}} The same journey is a little over two hours in the air, and the fare gap between the cheapest and dearest carrier on a route is often smaller than the baggage fee that separates them. ●

The overnight legs are a genuine planning tool and a safety decision at once. A sleeper saves a hotel night and moves a long journey out of daylight; the train does that with a flat berth and a door, the bus with a berth built to a shorter frame and an operator you cannot verify from the seat. Reviews describe legroom, not maintenance. The position is that the bus goes places the railway does not, and that where both exist the train is the one you can assess. Sapa is the exception that needs slack: every route there is overland, so a return booked for the day of an international flight has no fallback at all. ●

The call

Fly above five hundred miles, never below a hundred and fifty, and take the train on the coast. Between those lines it is a toss-up decided by departure times and whether you would rather sleep through a journey or see it. {{claim:hue-dn-rail}} It costs very little and replaces a transfer you were making anyway, which makes it the best-value hour in the country. Book the internal flights early in the day: the low-cost carriers accumulate delay as the schedule runs, and the leg that connects to a cruise check-in or an international departure is the one that cannot slip.

When the other call is right
  • then take the bus → Đà Lạt, Mũi Né, Phong Nha, Cát Bà and the delta towns, where the railway does not go; daytime where possible
  • then take the overnight train → Hanoi to Huế or to Lào Cai for Sapa, when daylight hours matter more than the fare
  • then fly short → Côn Đảo, Phú Quốc from the north, Điện Biên: there is no practical alternative
When ours is wrong for you
  • then do not fly → Huế to Đà Nẵng, ever; and Hội An has no airport or station, so every version of that leg goes through Đà Nẵng by road
  • then do not take the sleeper bus → with children of any age, or on mountain roads at night if the train exists
  • then leave a spare day → any thin-schedule route, where a cancelled flight means the next day's, not the afternoon's

North — The bay, Ninh Bình, or Sapa — which one does the northern leg carry?Decision 06 of 11

Hanoi sits at the hub of three excursions that every first-timer wants and almost no northern leg can hold. A one-night cruise does not occupy one night; the transfer out takes most of a day and the return most of a morning, so it claims two. Sapa is five to six hours each way by road, which makes a day there two travel days for one in the valley. Ninh Bình is two hours. The question is not which is best. It is what the leg has room for once the first choice has taken its share.

The reason the day trip to the bay disappoints is structural rather than bad luck. It is a long road transfer each way for a few hours on the busiest water, alongside the other day boats, with a shopping stop on the way; what it cannot deliver is the late afternoon after the day boats have gone and the early morning before they return, which is what the photographs are made of. An overnight buys those two windows, kayaking from the back of the boat, and an anchorage. Cancellations are decided by the port authority, not the operator, so a cruise never goes on the night before an international flight. ●

The two-day case is the common one and it has an asymmetry the brochures do not print. {{claim:bay-suspension}} Ninh Bình's rowboats go out in rain, and a wet day there is a worse day rather than a lost one. {{claim:halong-road}} So the two places are substitutes and not merely alternatives: the same transfer, the same limestone, one of them at the mercy of a forecast and the other not. A cancelled sailing on the morning of departure turns into a Ninh Bình day from the same hotel if the fallback was already in your head; it turns into a day in Hanoi if it was not.

Sapa's cost is the road, and the road has no substitute. There is no airport, the railway stops an hour short at Lào Cai, and the expressway van is the fastest version at five to six hours. Budget four days for a two-day trek and you are close to what the leg really costs. The decision underneath is whether the mountains or the water are the anchor of the north; the leg rarely holds both without the centre paying for it. ●

The call

On a short northern leg take Ninh Bình; on a standard one, the overnight bay; take Sapa only when the mountains are the reason you came. Ninh Bình returns a full day of extraordinary landscape for two hours of travel. The bay is worth the overnight and not worth the day trip, and it is worth asking for Lan Hạ by name, where the same limestone carries a fraction of the boats. Sapa is five hours of mountain road in each direction with no flight behind it, and if the trek is the point it earns that; if it is on the list because it was on a list, it is the addition most people regret. With two days and no more, read the forecast before you read the brochures: calm and clear, the boat; cloud, wind or a warning, the rowboat — and sleep in Ninh Bình rather than come back.

When the other call is right
  • then take Sapa → mountains and trekking are the reason for the trip; go in September–October or March–May and sleep in the valley, not the town
  • then take both → three weeks, when Ninh Bình and Sapa stop being a fork
  • then take Cát Bà instead → independent travellers who would rather sleep on land and take day boats into Lan Hạ
When ours is wrong for you
  • then skip the bay → it is winter and the mist that photographs well makes the karsts invisible in person; Ninh Bình is the better bet
  • then take the rowboat → the two-day forecast shows cloud, wind or a storm warning; the port authority grounds the fleet at once and a two-day window has nowhere to move to
  • then skip Sapa → the leg is four days or fewer; two of them on a mountain road is the wrong trade
  • then skip the day trip → if the bay matters, do the overnight; if the day is all there is, Ninh Bình gives comparable scenery for half the travel
● Ask any operator which bay the itinerary covers — Hạ Long, Bái Tử Long or Lan Hạ — how many cabins the boat has, and what happens when the port authority cancels. The answers, not the price, separate the tiers.

Three places inside an hour of each other produce three different trips, and they are sold under one label. The distances look trivial on a map. They are the entire decision.

The tailor sets a floor under the number of nights, and it is the detail most often discovered too late. A well-made garment needs two fittings, which in practice means ordering on the first morning and allowing two full days, three for a suit that is meant to fit. A one-night stop in Hội An is not the trip to have clothes made on, however persuasive the overnight turnaround sounds. ●

Đà Nẵng is a legitimate choice for what it is — a working coastal city with a long beach, the Chăm sculpture museum, the Sơn Trà peninsula and an airport minutes from the centre — and a poor substitute for what it is not. The call

Hội An is described as magical and as a tourist trap, and those are the same streets before seven and at noon; a base inside the town is the only one that puts you there at the right hours without a plan. The season overrules all of it: from September to November the beach is the part of the proposition that fails first, and the streets nearest the river flood in some years.

When the other call is right
  • then take Đà Nẵng → a late arrival or early departure by air, a family wanting more than a beach, or a stay of a week
  • then take An Bàng → three nights or more, the sea is in season, and dinner in town is a bicycle ride you are happy to make in the dark
  • then take the split → four nights, and you want the town's quiet hours and an airport hotel on the last night
When ours is wrong for you
  • then take the town → two nights, the Ancient Town is why you came, or clothes are being made

  • then check the river → any Hội An room between September and November; ask how far from the water it sits

Which beach — and does the month leave you a choice?

Beach — Which beach — and does the month leave you a choice?Decision 09 of 11

Vietnam's two beach destinations are opposite kinds of place with opposite weather, and the month settles the question more often than any preference does. Phú Quốc is dry roughly from November to April and rough from May; Nha Trang is dry from January to August and wettest in October and November. Picking the wrong one for the month is how people end up on a grey beach wondering what the fuss was.

Five coasts · from the site's own seasonal grid
CoastIts calm months
Phú Quốc ●November – April; the best sand, the most building
Nha Trang ●January – August; a city with a beach, on the railway
An Bàng and Đà Nẵng ●February – August; the beach afternoon inside a trip about something else
Côn Đảo ●March – September; empty, a prison island, four days of transport
Mũi Né ●wind November – April, swimming May – October; never both

The character difference is the second decision, and it only opens in the months when both are dry. Phú Quốc is an island with resorts strung along its coast and a small town in the middle: you use the property's beach and take taxis to anything else, and the south has a theme park, a cable car and a built-up resort town that is nothing like the photographs. Nha Trang is a city of several hundred thousand with a six-kilometre municipal beach along its front, cheaper, busier, with a large package-tourism layer and a real place to eat behind it. ●

Access decides the rest. Nha Trang is on the railway and the highway, so it drops into a north-to-south route without a detour; Phú Quốc is a flight and a dedicated leg, which is why it works as an ending rather than a stop. The island also carries the country's only visa exemption for Americans — thirty days or fewer, arriving and leaving the island directly, which helps only if the island is the whole trip. ◇

The call

November or December, Phú Quốc. May to August, Nha Trang. January to April, either, so decide on character. September and October, no coast is reliable, and the real question is whether a beach belongs in that itinerary at all. A first north-to-south fortnight needs no detour: An Bàng for the afternoons, and Nha Trang if the route and the month allow. A trip that should end on sand is a flight to Phú Quốc. Both places disappoint the people who booked the brochure — the unspoilt island that is now a resort coast, the beach town that is a high-rise city — and that is an expectation problem the month table prevents.

When the other call is right
  • then take Phú Quốc → the beach is the point, it is November to April, and three nights are available
  • then take Nha Trang → a beach day inside a longer overland route, in summer, when the central coast is hot and humid
  • then take Côn Đảo → remoteness and the prison history, with four days and a tolerance for cancelled ferries
When ours is wrong for you
  • then drop the beach → September or October, when the answer is a city or the north instead
  • then use An Bàng → the trip already passes Hội An and the only beach you need is an afternoon
  • then check the building → any Phú Quốc property: recent reviews mentioning construction next door are worth more than the brochure
● Flags and local advice, not how the water looks, decide whether to swim; rip currents are a real hazard on the open coast in the windy months, and lifeguards are a given only on the municipal beaches at Đà Nẵng and Nha Trang.

Your dates touch Tết. Go through it, or around it?

Calendar — Your dates touch Tết. Go through it, or around it?Decision 10 of 11

Tết is not a festival a visitor attends; it is the entire country travelling home. Articles treat it as a week to avoid, which misses the mechanism. It does not make one week inconvenient. It pushes the transport bookings months earlier than you would otherwise make them, inverts the usual price relationship between the cities and the coast, empties the ATMs in small towns, and raises the cost of any plan that leans on a single mid-trip flight — because that is the link most likely to fail.

The four things Tết does to a plan
What Tết doesWhich way it pushes
Seats on popular routes ●book out well ahead and fares rise; on the wanted days the seat may not exist
Family-run food, tailors, small operators ●close for several days, sometimes a week
City hotels versus the coast ●cities can get cheaper as business stops; resorts fill with domestic holidaymakers
Banks and small-town ATMs ●close, run empty, and go unrefilled for days

The date moves with the lunar calendar, usually landing between late January and mid-February, and it is widely misreported in travel articles written well ahead. The official holiday is the smaller number; the practical window is wider, and one national carrier treats a span running into the third week of February as its return-surge period on routes from Saigon to the north. A plan that depends on a Hanoi–Đà Nẵng flight in that week has put its weakest link exactly where the pressure is. ◇

Two other dates do a smaller version of the same thing and are fixed on the solar calendar: Reunification Day and Labour Day on 30 April and 1 May, which fill the beaches and hill towns for a long weekend whose exact shape is announced each year, and National Day in early September. {{claim:natday-2027}} If the only difference between two possible weeks is that one contains a national holiday, that is a real reason to pick the other. ◇

The call

Go around it unless you have a reason to be there, and if you go through it, fix the transport the day the dates are known. {{claim:tet-2027}} The disruption runs about a week either side of the official days, because people travel home before and back after. A trip that must overlap it should base in a larger city or a resort area rather than a small town, carry more cash than seems sensible, withdrawn in a city before the holiday starts, and expect its favourite-looking kitchen to be shuttered. What it gets in return is the stillest Hanoi you will ever see, temples full and alive, and the flower markets in the week before, which are a genuine sight.

When the other call is right
  • then go through it → a Vietnamese connection, an invitation into a home, or a taste for a capital at its quietest
  • then go through it → a package or a resort stay, which feels the closures far less than independent travel
  • then use the inversion → a city hotel can be the bargain of the trip in that week; the coast is the opposite
When ours is wrong for you
  • then move the dates → the plan is independent, food-led, and runs through small towns
  • then move the dates → the trip has one internal flight on which the whole sequence depends
  • then move the dates → the e-visa is still to be applied for and the working-day clock stops for the holiday
◆ The dates for your own year, and the government's announced span, are on the Field Sheet and must be checked there; a Tết date printed in a book is the kind of fact that goes wrong quietly. Learn chúc mừng năm mới; it is received warmly.

No fee figure is printed here, and that is deliberate: no bank's own foreign-card tariff could be verified against a primary source, and every number in circulation traces to a travel blog. The structure is what is true and what lasts — a flat charge, a per-withdrawal cap, and a US-side fee on top — and the rule follows from the structure without needing the number. ●

Tipping is the other half of the cash question and it is mostly a matter of unlearning. It is not customary, leaving nothing is normal, and the places it is anticipated are a short list: private guides and drivers hired by the day, spa therapists, porters, and the pooled envelope on an overnight cruise. The scams that exist are price-ambiguity rather than theft — a service begun before a number is named — and one habit prevents nearly all of them: agree the figure, in đồng, before anything starts. Crossing a border is the one place the cash itself is regulated: {{claim:cash-declare}} ●

The đồng is not a courtesy to the country; it is the law. {{claim:dong-only}} A price quoted in dollars is therefore the seller's convenience rather than a rate you are bound to, and settling it in đồng is both the cheaper and the regular course.

The call

Withdraw the maximum the machine and your bank allow, as rarely as you can stand, and carry into the countryside what the countryside cannot dispense. The ATM fee is flat and the cap is low, so the cost per dollar is set entirely by how much you take at once: five small withdrawals are five fees. Your own bank's foreign charge stacks on top and is often the larger half, which is the part within your control before you fly. Decline the machine's offer to bill in dollars — dynamic currency conversion is a worse rate every time — change nothing at the airport beyond the ride to the hotel, and keep a small reserve of clean dollars for emergencies rather than for spending.

When the other call is right
  • then carry more → the far north, the delta backroads, or any dates inside the Tết window; refill in the last city
  • then rely on the card → a trip that stays in the four cities and sleeps in chain hotels
  • then change at a gold shop → it is legal, ordinary, and usually a better rate than the bank; never with someone who approaches you
When ours is wrong for you
  • then carry less → you are leaving the country into Cambodia, where small dollar notes are useful and đồng is not
  • then stop tipping → an ordinary phở shop, a market, a cab; rounding up is the gesture, a percentage is a misunderstanding
  • then count twice → any stack of similar notes, in both directions; it is the zeros, not dishonesty

What the book does instead is tell you which part of a city to sleep in and why (the Old Quarter against the French Quarter, the town against the beach), which is the decision that survives a change of ownership.
  • It does not name restaurants. A list of restaurants is wrong within a season: the cook moves, the queue finds it, the lease ends. The rule that generated the list is still right in five years — one dish on the sign, a queue at the right hour, charcoal you can smell — so the book gives you the rule. The eating chapter is that argument in full.
  • It does not print current prices. Prices belong in the Field Sheet, which is dated and re-issued. A price set in a book is a claim that stops being true quietly, and the reader has no way to tell when it did. Where a number is structural — the visa fee set by decree, the customs allowance in đồng — the Sheet carries it with the date it was read.
  • It does not republish anyone's ratings. Aggregate scores from the booking platforms are contractually and editorially radioactive, and reprinting them would add nothing you cannot see for yourself.

There is a fifth refusal that shaped this edition. Where no primary source could be found for a number that every blog prints — the ATM fee, the per-withdrawal cap, the typical sleeper-bus fare — we wrote the structure and left the figure out, rather than copy a number whose origin nobody could name. A chapter assembled to fill a page is detectable, and it devalues the chapters that were earned.

Closing

The country, not the plan

The country, not the planClosing

There is a moment, usually on the second or third morning, when the planning stops mattering. It tends to arrive somewhere ordinary — a lake at six in the morning, full of badminton and tai chi and old men doing slow exercises in their vests; a pavement at seven, where a woman who has made one soup for thirty years hands you a bowl and a plastic stool; a river before dawn, with a boat selling coffee alongside. Nothing about it is a sight. It is a country working, at an hour most visitors are still asleep.

That is the thing the arithmetic is protecting. Every decision in this book exists to subtract a small anxiety from the fortnight — the gate that matches, the leg that flies, the night that was not spent on a coach — so that more of it is available for the parts that cannot be decided in advance: the lane you turned down because the lanterns were lit, the cafe you climbed to through what looked like somebody's hallway, the pass seen from a train window with the sea on one side and the cloud lifting.

Work the sums out before you go. Then put the book down and stop optimising. The country is better than any plan you can make of it, and it rewards the loose afternoon more than almost anywhere.
