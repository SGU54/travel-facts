Vietnamese Americans are the largest group of US travellers to Vietnam with a reason beyond tourism: a first trip back, a family visit, a parent's grave, a cousin's wedding, a child being shown where the family came from. The trip has an emotional layer this site cannot plan for you. It also has a paperwork layer that is different from an ordinary American's and that trips up a surprising number of families at the airport, and that is what this article covers.

Which visa, or none

A Vietnamese American travelling on a US passport needs a visa to enter Vietnam like any other American, with one route the tourist articles mention only in passing. Under Decree 82/2015, overseas Vietnamese and their foreign spouses and children can apply for a visa-exemption paper valid for up to five years, with a six-month stay on each entry, instead of a visa for each trip.

For a family that will go back more than once, it replaces the e-visa and its ninety-day limit with something closer to a standing permission.

Eligibility is proved with a document showing Vietnamese origin: a Vietnamese birth certificate, a decision renouncing Vietnamese nationality, a certificate of loss of nationality, or another paper stating former nationality; a spouse or child of an overseas Vietnamese proves the relationship instead. The passport must have at least a year left when you apply. Applications from the United States go through a Vietnamese consulate; the paper can also be applied for in Vietnam at the Immigration Department.

If you are going once

The ordinary e-visa, up to 90 days, is simpler for a single trip and needs no proof of origin. Ask for the full length you might want, because Vietnam does not allow you to renew or extend an e-visa while you are in the country. Which of the two routes fits your family's pattern of trips is one of the visa decisions the complete Vietnam decision manual lays out side by side.

Which passport

If you or a family member holds both a US and a Vietnamese passport, the rule is fixed: you must enter and exit Vietnam on the same passport, and travel domestically on the passport you entered with. Entering on the Vietnamese passport and flying home on the American one, or the reverse, is the error that strands people at departure.

Decide which passport the trip runs on before you book, and use it for every domestic flight too, because airlines check.

Vietnamese nationality is not lost simply by becoming an American citizen. The decree's own list of proofs includes a formal decision renouncing it, which tells you that renunciation is a process rather than a side effect. Someone who left as a child and never renounced may still be a Vietnamese national in Vietnamese law, with consequences for their children that the next section covers.

Your children

Children of Vietnamese parentage are the part of the paperwork most families do not see coming. The US Embassy states that in general a Vietnamese passport or a Vietnamese exit visa is required by the Vietnamese government in the event your child needs to exit Vietnam. The requirement attaches to parentage, not to the passport the child entered on. In practice this is raised at departure rather than arrival, which is the worst place to discover it.

The State Department adds a second rule for children travelling without both parents: notarised consent is required for every Vietnamese minor, including dual US citizens, to travel without a parent or guardian, and Vietnamese authorities have sometimes stopped US-citizen minors from leaving without it. A grandparent taking a grandchild to the village, or one parent travelling alone with the children, should carry the other parent's notarised consent letter or proof of sole custody.

What to do before you fly

Ask the Vietnamese consulate, before booking, how your child's parentage is treated and what document they want at exit. The US Embassy cannot help with this; it is a Vietnamese government question, and the consulate's answer is the one that counts at the airport.

Staying with family

Staying with relatives rather than in a hotel is normal and it has one administrative step. every visitor must have their overnight address registered with the local police; hotels do it automatically, and in a family home the duty is the host's: by law the host must declare a foreign guest's temporary residence to the local police within 12 hours of arrival, or 24 hours in remote areas, usually online.

Mention it to your relatives on the first evening. Most know the drill, and the ward police office is a familiar place to them.

Relatives will also expect gifts, and will have views on what you should bring: American vitamins, cosmetics and branded goods remain the classic requests, and cash in envelopes for the older generation is the custom. Prices inside Vietnam are set in dong by law, so change or withdraw a sensible amount early rather than arriving with a wallet of small US notes.

The altar and the graves

A first visit usually includes the family altar and the family tombs, and relatives will guide you through the incense and the bows. The anniversary of a death, not a birthday, is the family gathering, and if your trip can be timed to a parent's or grandparent's giỗ, the family will be assembled for you.

Timing and flights

The weeks before Tet are the peak for flights from the United States, because so many families fly back for the new year, and the fares and the crowds reflect it. Tet falls on 6 February 2027, and a trip timed to it means booking months ahead and accepting that the country closes for the holiday week. A trip in the dry, cool months either side of it gets the family without the squeeze.

The only nonstop flight is Vietnam Airlines' San Francisco to Ho Chi Minh City flight; from Orange County, San Jose, Houston or Seattle the choice is that flight via the Bay Area or a one-stop through an Asian hub, and the one-stops are often cheaper and land in Hanoi or Da Nang directly if the family is in the north or the centre.

Being Việt kiều

Overseas Vietnamese were once resented at home and are now courted, officially and by the market, and both attitudes survive. You may be charged the local price or the foreigner's price depending on your accent; relatives may assume wealth; a younger cousin may know more of the music you grew up with, through the diaspora variety shows, than you expect. The language is the other surprise: a Vietnamese learned at home in California is often a southern Vietnamese of the 1970s, and relatives will hear it. None of this spoils the trip, and most returning families say the thing they did not expect was how ordinary, and how welcoming, the country was.
