TITLE: Do Americans Need a Visa for Vietnam?
EXCERPT: Yes — US passport holders need a visa for Vietnam, and the widely repeated 45-day visa-free rule does not apply to you. Here is what you actually need.

Yes. If you hold a US passport, you need a visa to enter Vietnam. There is one narrow exception, and there is a lot of confident misinformation online telling you otherwise — so it is worth being precise about what applies to an American passport and what does not.

The short answer for US passport holders
American travelers need a visa for Vietnam, and almost all of them use the electronic visa. The State Department states the rule without qualification: a visa is required, and you must apply before you travel. The single exception is a stay of 30 days or less on the island of Phu Quoc, which you can do without a visa if you fly in and out of Phu Quoc directly.
The mechanics of applying are covered step by step in our Vietnam e-visa walkthrough. That exception is narrower than it sounds. Fly to Phu Quoc via Ho Chi Minh City and clear immigration on the mainland, and you have entered Vietnam — the exemption no longer covers you.

Why you keep reading that Vietnam is visa-free
Vietnam does run a unilateral visa-exemption scheme, most recently widened to twelve more European countries in August 2025. It covers a list of mostly European and Asian nationalities for stays of up to 45 days. United States, Canadian and Australian passports are not on that list. Articles that say "Vietnam is visa-free for 45 days" are usually accurate for somebody — just not for you.
There is one group of Americans who are treated differently. Overseas Vietnamese holding the relevant Vietnamese documentation can obtain a five-year visa-exemption certificate instead of applying trip by trip. If you have Vietnamese parentage, that route is worth asking about before you pay for an e-visa.

What the e-visa actually gives you
The Vietnamese e-visa is issued online by the Immigration Department and costs $25 for single entry or $50 for multiple entry, payable by bank transfer. Validity runs up to 90 days, and processing usually takes 3 to 5 working days once the department has your completed application and payment.
Treat the processing window as a floor rather than a promise. Vietnam's own embassy in India publishes a far wider range, and the figure stretches during holidays and application surges. Apply weeks ahead, and do not buy non-refundable flights against an assumption that it will land in three days.

Ninety days is a ceiling, not a default
Vietnam usually grants exactly the period you request, so the 90 days is the maximum available rather than what you automatically receive. Ask for less than you need and you cannot top it up later.
There is also a passport rule that almost nothing online mentions. Your e-visa's validity must be at least 30 days shorter than your passport's remaining validity. For most people this never bites — a 90-day visa needs about four months of validity, and Vietnam already asks for six. It matters if you are travelling close to your passport's expiry, which is a reason to renew before a long trip rather than after.

Your passport: how much validity you need
Vietnam requires your passport to be valid at entry with six months' validity beyond your arrival date, plus blank pages for stamps. The State Department's own page is internally inconsistent on the page count, saying one blank page per stamp in one section and at least two in another, so treat two as the working minimum.
One more trap worth knowing if things go wrong mid-trip: Vietnam may not accept the 12-page US emergency passport for e-visa entry. If you lose your passport elsewhere in the region and replace it at a US embassy, the replacement may not get you into Vietnam.

The second form nobody tells you about
Separately from the visa, Vietnam operates an online pre-arrival declaration at the Immigration Department's own portal. Filing is free, takes about ten minutes, and is done no earlier than three days before you arrive. The two governments describe its status differently: the State Department says air travelers on a non-Vietnamese passport must complete it, while Vietnam's portal presents it as advice that reduces waiting time at immigration.
We are not going to resolve a disagreement between two governments for you. The practical answer is to file it: it is free, it takes about ten minutes, and on Vietnam's own account it shortens the queue you would otherwise stand in.

Where your e-visa is actually valid
An e-visa works at 83 designated border gates — 17 airports, 27 land crossings and 39 seaports — after Vietnam added 41 of them in December 2025. That covers every international airport in Vietnam, so Noi Bai, Tan Son Nhat, Da Nang, Cam Ranh and Phu Quoc are all eligible. Note that eligibility is a separate question from the port you select on the form, which is dealt with below.
Where it does bite is overland. If you plan to cross into Cambodia or Laos and come back, or enter by road from either, the crossing has to be on the approved list — and plenty are not.

Two ways a valid visa still fails
Your entry and exit points must match what you selected on the application, so a visa naming Hanoi will not get you through Ho Chi Minh City. And you must print the thing: the official instruction is to carry a paper copy with your passport. Both failures happen before you leave home, and both are free to avoid.

TITLE: Vietnam E-Visa: How to Apply Step by Step
EXCERPT: The official Vietnamese e-visa costs $25, takes 3 to 5 working days, and has four ways to fail at the border. Here is the whole process.

The Vietnamese e-visa is genuinely straightforward to apply for, and the application takes perhaps twenty minutes. A few of its fields are more binding than they look, though, and the mistakes worth knowing about are the ones you cannot correct once you have landed.

Use the official site and nothing else
Vietnam's e-visa is issued by the Immigration Department, and the fee is $25 for single entry or $50 for multiple entry, paid by bank transfer. The department runs it on two official domains, evisa.gov.vn and thithucdientu.gov.vn; evisa.gov.vn is the one to use. Beyond those, you are dealing with an intermediary, and the market around this particular visa is unusually aggressive.
Plenty of those intermediaries are legitimate agents doing real work. The problem is that their markup is rarely presented as a markup — sites styled to look governmental will charge several times the state fee for submitting the same form you could submit yourself. Paying an agent is a choice; paying one without realising you had the option is not.

Whether you need a visa at all, and the one narrow exception for US passports, is covered in our guide to Vietnam visa requirements for Americans.

What you need before you start
Have your passport scan, a passport-style photograph, your travel dates, your intended port of entry and your first night's address ready. The form will not let you save your way around a missing field, and sessions time out.

Choosing your dates and duration
Vietnam usually grants exactly the period you ask for rather than defaulting to the maximum, so the duration field deserves more thought than it looks like it does. Validity runs up to 90 days, but asking for three weeks gets you three weeks even if you later wish you had more.
Build in a buffer at both ends. Arriving a day or two later than planned is fine as long as you are inside the validity window — what you cannot do is arrive before it opens or leave after it closes, and neither is fixable from the airport.

The passport rule that can cap your request
Your e-visa's validity has to be at least 30 days shorter than your passport's remaining validity. Since a 90-day visa needs roughly four months of validity and Vietnam separately requires six, most travellers never meet this limit. Check your expiry date anyway before choosing a duration — it only bites if you are close to renewal.

Picking your port of entry
The application asks which border gate you will use, and that answer is binding. Your entry and exit locations must match what you selected, so an e-visa naming Noi Bai in Hanoi will not get you through Tan Son Nhat in Ho Chi Minh City.
Two different rules are at work here and they are easy to confuse. The first is which gates accept an e-visa at all: 83 of them since December 2025, including every international airport in Vietnam, so flying in is never the problem. The second is the one above — your selected port is binding whichever gate you choose. Overland crossings into Cambodia and Laos are where the first rule still bites, because many crossings are not on the list.

Paying and waiting
Processing normally runs 3 to 5 working days from the point the Immigration Department has both your completed application and your payment. Weekends and Vietnamese public holidays are not working days, and Tet shuts the calendar down for a week or more.
Do not treat that window as a guarantee. Vietnam's own embassy in India publishes a substantially longer range, and the honest planning advice is to apply several weeks out. The US Embassy is explicit that it cannot change your e-visa information or speed your application up — there is no escalation path.

Check the document before you celebrate
When your e-visa arrives, read every field against your passport, character by character. A mismatch in your name, passport number or date of birth can mean being refused entry on arrival, and the State Department warns you may then have to pay a travel agency at the airport for an expensive visa sponsorship — a remedy that is neither guaranteed nor cheap.
Then print it. The official instruction is to carry a printed copy alongside your passport, and a sheet of paper is a cheap hedge against a flat phone battery in an immigration queue.

The separate form you also need
Vietnam runs an online pre-arrival declaration independently of the visa, filed at the Immigration Department's portal no earlier than three days before you arrive. It is free and takes about ten minutes. The State Department describes it as required for air arrivals on a non-Vietnamese passport; Vietnam's own portal frames it as advisory. Do it regardless.

What you cannot do later
An e-visa cannot be renewed or extended from inside Vietnam. The Immigration Department allows in-person extensions for other visa categories, but the electronic visa is not one of them, which makes the duration you choose at application the final word.
Anyone selling you an e-visa extension is therefore selling a service the government says does not exist. If you genuinely need longer, the answer is a different visa type arranged before you travel, not a fix applied once you are there. That is worth knowing before you choose a duration, because it is one of the places where the order you plan things in matters — something the complete Vietnam decision manual works through in full.

TITLE: Is Vietnam Safe for Americans?
EXCERPT: Vietnam sits at the State Department's lowest advisory level. The risk Americans worry about and the risk that actually hurts them are not the same thing.

Vietnam is a safe country to travel in, and the US government says so in the plainest terms it has. The more useful question is not whether it is safe but which risks are real — because the thing most American visitors worry about is not the thing that actually sends them home early.

What the official advisory says
The State Department places Vietnam at Level 1 — Exercise Normal Precautions, the lowest of its four levels. That is a lower advisory than the Department currently assigns to France or Germany, both of which sit at Level 2. For an American audience whose mental picture of Vietnam was shaped by a war that ended fifty years ago, that single data point is worth sitting with.
Level 1 is not a promise that nothing goes wrong. It means the baseline caution you would use in any unfamiliar city is the appropriate level of caution here. The advisory itself is short and worth two minutes of your time before you fly.

How Americans are actually received
Warmly, and more warmly than most first-time visitors expect. The median Vietnamese citizen was born long after 1975, Vietnam and the United States are significant trading partners, and American visitors are commonplace. In Vietnam it is called the American War, museums present it from the Vietnamese side without much softening, and that is a national account of a national trauma rather than a comment on you standing in the room.

The crime risk: petty, persistent, avoidable
Petty theft is the dominant crime risk, and the State Department is direct about it: Vietnam is generally safe for travelers, but petty crime is common, especially in popular tourist locations. Violent crime against foreigners is rare. What is not rare is losing a phone.
The signature method is a snatch from a moving motorbike — your phone leaves your hand while you are standing on a sidewalk reading a map, and the bike is gone before you have registered what happened. Ho Chi Minh City is where this is most common. The defense is unglamorous and effective: do not walk and scroll, keep your phone in your hand only when you have stopped and have your back to a wall, and wear bags on the traffic-away side.

Scams are price-ambiguity, not theft
Nearly every common Vietnamese tourist scam is the same trick wearing different clothes: a service begins before a price is named, then the price arrives. Taxi meters that run fast, cyclo rides quoted per person rather than per trip, an unsolicited shoe repair in Hanoi's Old Quarter, a motorbike rental with pre-existing scratches charged at return. One habit prevents most of them — agree the number, in dong, before anything starts. Grab and Xanh SM remove the taxi version entirely by fixing the fare in the app. The exception is the motorbike-rental damage claim, where the price was never the issue: that one needs photographs of the bike before you ride off.

The risk nobody writes about
Road traffic is what actually injures visitors to Vietnam, not crime, and the gap between those two risks is not close. The country's roads are crowded and chaotic, most people are on motorbikes, and a tourist on a rented scooter with no local riding experience is the highest-exposure person on the road.
Two things compound it. US driver's licences and International Driving Permits are both invalid in Vietnam, and riding without a Vietnamese licence can void the travel insurance that would otherwise pay your medical bills. We have written up what that means in practice in our guide to renting a motorbike in Vietnam, and it is the single most consequential safety decision on a Vietnam trip.

Crossing the street is a skill
Walk at a slow, constant, predictable pace rather than darting, and keep watching the traffic as you go. Riders read your trajectory and steer around it, so sudden changes are what cause collisions — the rider has to re-solve the problem mid-manoeuvre. Predictability is the technique, not a guarantee: cars and buses do not swerve the way scooters do, so wait those out, and crossing alongside a local is the easiest way to learn the rhythm.

Legal risks worth taking seriously
Drug offences carry severe penalties, and the mechanics are worth understanding if you are coming from a state where cannabis is legal. Police sometimes test blood for drugs, and substances used legally in another country can still lead to arrest in Vietnam if they are detectable in your system on arrival. Ingredients in some ordinary prescription medicines, including common ADHD treatments, may also be illegal — a reason to travel with documentation for anything you are carrying.
What makes this weigh more than it otherwise would is the aftermath. Consular access to detained Americans is routinely delayed, often by several weeks, so the embassy may not reach you quickly. Overstaying your visa compounds similarly: fines, then delays obtaining permission to leave.

Unexploded ordnance, in proportion
Wartime ordnance remains in the ground in parts of Vietnam, and the risk is real but narrow. The greatest exposure is at construction sites, and landmine risk is low in urban and populated areas, including tourist destinations. On a DMZ tour or a rural trek, stay on marked paths and do not pick up metal objects. In Hanoi, this is not something to think about.

Health, air and emergencies
Tap water is not drinkable anywhere in Vietnam, bottled water is cheap and universal, and ice in established restaurants is commercially produced and generally fine. Air pollution is a genuine consideration — the State Department flags it as a significant problem in major cities, Hanoi above all, and advises travelers with respiratory conditions to speak to a doctor first. Vaccination questions are covered in our Vietnam health and vaccines guide.
Emergency numbers: 113 for traffic police, 115 for an ambulance. Be aware that these are frequently answered only in Vietnamese, and for a serious medical event in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City you are usually better off going directly to an international hospital than waiting for dispatch.

Taken together, the pattern is that Vietnam's real risks are ordinary ones handled badly rather than exotic ones — and most of them are settled by transport choices made weeks earlier, when you decide how you will move around the country. TITLE: Renting a Motorbike in Vietnam: What to Know
EXCERPT: Rental shops will hand you a bike without checking anything. That does not make it legal, and your travel insurance may not cover what follows.

Renting a motorbike is the classic Vietnam experience, and for a lot of travelers it is the whole point of the trip. It is also the one decision on a Vietnam itinerary where the gap between what is easy and what is legal is wide enough to end badly. Here is the honest version.

The legal position, stated plainly
US driver's licences and International Driving Permits do not work in Vietnam. The State Department puts it without hedging: driving without a Vietnamese licence can mean a fine, arrest, or jail, and renting a moped or motorbike without one is illegal. That is the US government describing the law to its own citizens, not a cautious lawyer's reading.
You will not feel any of this at the rental shop. Shops routinely hand over a bike against a passport photocopy and a cash deposit, ask nothing, and check nothing. The absence of a check is not permission — it only means the consequences arrive later, if they arrive at all.

Why the IDP you bought does not help
Americans often arrive holding an International Driving Permit from AAA, reasonably assuming it is the universal solution. Vietnam does not recognise it. The permit the United States issues is not the standard Vietnam accepts, and the State Department simply lists both your licence and your IDP as not working. Buying one before you fly is money spent on a document that changes nothing here.

What this does to your insurance
Travel medical insurance is where the legal question turns into a financial one, and it is the reason this article exists. If you are in an accident while riding without a valid licence, the State Department warns that your travel insurance may not pay your medical bills.
Sit with what that means. Road traffic is the leading cause of serious injury to visitors in Vietnam, and a bad motorbike accident can mean a medical evacuation costing well into six figures. Riding without a licence means finding out whether your policy pays at the worst possible moment — the State Department's wording is that it may not, which is a risk you are accepting rather than a certainty you are buying.

Can you get a Vietnamese licence as a visitor?
Realistically, no. Converting a foreign licence requires residency documentation — a temporary residence card issued by the Immigration Department — which a tourist on an e-visa does not hold and cannot obtain for a two-week trip. The lawful route exists for people who live in Vietnam, not for people visiting it.

Traffic law, and how much it is enforced
Vietnamese law requires helmets, speed-limit compliance and zero alcohol while driving — zero, not a blood-alcohol threshold like the 0.08 you are used to. One beer with dinner puts you over the limit. You must also carry your licence, the vehicle registration and identification while riding.
Enforcement is tightening rather than relaxing. The government is deploying cameras and raising fines in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, and traffic police increasingly have speed detectors and some English. The State Department also notes that informal payments are sometimes solicited for minor violations, and advises insisting on an official receipt.

If you ride anyway
Plenty of travelers make this choice with their eyes open, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. If you are going to, reduce the things that are actually within your control.
Photograph the entire bike with the renter present before you ride off, including every existing scratch, or expect to be charged for them at return. Never surrender your actual passport as a deposit — offer a photocopy and cash. Wear a real helmet rather than the plastic shell supplied, which is not designed to survive an impact. Ride outside the cities before you ride inside them, and do not start in Ho Chi Minh City traffic on day one.

The alternatives that are not a compromise
Riding pillion with an experienced local — the easy-rider model — is how a large share of the Ha Giang loop is actually done, and it removes the licence problem entirely while improving the experience: you get a guide, and you get to look at the scenery instead of the road. A car with a driver costs roughly what bare car rental costs in the US and covers the long legs comfortably. Grab's motorbike taxis handle short city hops for a couple of dollars.

What you are really deciding
Look at what just happened across all of that. This reads like a question about renting a vehicle, but every answer above was actually about something else — which parts of Vietnam you can reach, whether Ha Giang and the mountain passes are on your itinerary at all, how your long north-to-south legs get covered, and what your insurance is worth on the day you need it. The bike was never the decision. The shape of the trip was.
Travelers who sort transport first and route second tend to end up with an itinerary built around a vehicle instead of a vehicle chosen for the itinerary. Which way those choices should run, and what each one forecloses, is worked out in order in the complete Vietnam decision manual. If you are still weighing the broader risk picture, our honest assessment of safety in Vietnam covers the rest.

--- vietnam-health-and-vaccines | handoff=none | swamp=none ---
TITLE: Vietnam Health and Vaccines for Travelers
EXCERPT: No vaccines are required to enter Vietnam, several are recommended, and most travelers do not need the antimalarials they are told to buy.

Vietnam asks nothing of you medically at the border, which is a different statement from saying you need nothing. The distinction between what is required to enter and what a doctor would recommend is the whole of this subject, and conflating the two is how travelers end up either under-prepared or paying for medication they will never need.

Required versus recommended
No vaccinations are required to enter Vietnam. The State Department states it in exactly those words, and no proof of vaccination is needed at the border for a standard tourist arrival from the United States.
That is an immigration fact, not medical advice, and it is where most articles stop. The CDC maintains a separate and longer list of vaccines it recommends for Vietnam — recommendations that carry no border consequence whatsoever but exist because the diseases do. Nobody will stop you boarding without them.

What the CDC actually recommends
For Vietnam the CDC recommends Hepatitis A, Hepatitis B, and typhoid for most travelers, alongside keeping routine vaccinations current. Hepatitis A and typhoid are both food-and-water transmitted, which is the relevant route in a country where the best eating is done at street stalls. Hepatitis B is recommended for unvaccinated travelers of all ages.
Several others are conditional rather than universal, and the distinction saves money. Japanese encephalitis is aimed mainly at people moving to affected rural areas or spending long periods there, though CDC also says to consider it for shorter trips with significant rural or outdoor exposure, or accommodation without good mosquito protection. Rabies is weighed on animal contact and remoteness. The CDC's Vietnam page lists the full set with its reasoning.

Malaria: probably not your problem
Most visitors to Vietnam do not need antimalarial medication, which contradicts the blanket advice people are given about Southeast Asia. The CDC is specific: transmission is rural only, with rare cases in the Mekong and Red River deltas, and there is no transmission in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang, Hai Phong, Nha Trang or Quy Nhon.
Read that list against a normal itinerary. Hanoi, Da Nang and Ho Chi Minh City are named on it outright, and the rest of a standard first trip — Ha Long Bay, Hoi An — is coastal and urban rather than the rural forest where transmission sits. For most city-and-coast itineraries that makes prophylaxis a question to raise with a travel clinic rather than an automatic purchase.

When it does apply
Rural and forested areas are a different matter, and the far north is rural. Sapa, Ha Giang and extended time in the central highlands are not covered by the city exemption above, and if your trip is built around trekking rather than cities the calculation changes. Chloroquine and mefloquine resistance is present in a named set of provinces — Binh Phuoc, Dak Lak, Dak Nong, Gia Lai, Khanh Hoa, Kon Tum, Lam Dong, Ninh Thuan and Tay Ninh — where the CDC points to atovaquone-proguanil, doxycycline or tafenoquine instead. Elsewhere its options are wider. Which applies depends on exactly where you are going, which is a conversation for a travel clinic with your actual route in front of them.

The mosquito risk with no vaccine
Dengue is the mosquito-borne illness most likely to affect a visitor to Vietnam, and unlike malaria it is urban, seasonal and not preventable by medication. Cases rise during and after the rainy season, and the Aedes mosquitoes that carry it bite during the day rather than at dusk.
Bite avoidance is therefore the whole strategy, and it is the same strategy everywhere: repellent with DEET or picaridin applied to exposed skin, long sleeves in the evening, and air conditioning or a screened room. This matters more in the south and during the May-to-November wet months.

Food, water and the stomach question
Tap water is not drinkable anywhere in Vietnam, including the major cities. Bottled water is cheap and sold everywhere, and most hotels and many cafes will refill a bottle. Brush your teeth with bottled water if you are being careful, though many travelers do not and are fine.
Ice is the judgement call. The CDC's advice is to avoid ice, on the basis that it may be made from untreated water. In practice most cafes and restaurants in Vietnamese cities use commercially produced ice — the cylindrical kind with a hole through the middle — and iced coffee is close to unavoidable if you want to drink what the country drinks. The honest position is that the industrial product is widely used and widely consumed without incident, that neither its shape nor its popularity proves how it was handled, and that the cautious choice remains the CDC's. Be stricter in rural areas and anywhere the ice is loose chipped rather than uniform.

How to choose a street stall
Turnover is the most useful signal available to you. A stall with a queue of locals is selling food that has not been sitting around, which removes one of the commonest problems — though it says nothing about water, storage temperature or cross-contamination, so treat it as evidence rather than proof. Beyond that: prefer food cooked to order in front of you, and be more cautious with pre-cut fruit and anything lukewarm that ought to be hot.

Medical care and what to bring
International-standard hospitals exist in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City and Da Nang, and they expect payment up front — frequently by card before treatment begins, with reimbursement your problem afterwards. Outside those cities, facilities drop off sharply, which is the practical reason travel insurance with medical evacuation cover matters on a Vietnam trip.
Bring any prescription medication in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the prescription. Some ingredients in common prescription drugs, including certain ADHD treatments, may be illegal in Vietnam, so check anything you depend on before you fly rather than at customs. A basic kit of rehydration salts, loperamide and plasters covers the overwhelming majority of what actually goes wrong.
