----- ARTICLE: Vegetarian and Vegan Food in Vietnam  (slug vegetarian-vegan-food-vietnam)
EXCERPT: Harder than it looks and easier than you fear. The com chay network is the answer, and fish sauce is the problem.

Vietnam looks like an easy country to eat vegetarian in — the herbs, the vegetables, the rice — and then you discover that fish sauce is in almost everything, including dishes with no visible animal protein. It is still very manageable, but it needs a strategy rather than optimism.

The problem, stated plainly

Nuoc mam, fish sauce, is the base seasoning of the cuisine in the way salt is elsewhere. It is in dipping sauces, in marinades, in broths, in dressings for vegetable dishes. Shrimp paste, mam tom, appears in northern and central cooking. Pork or chicken stock underlies many broths that arrive looking vegetable-based.

A dish of stir-fried morning glory is not vegetarian by default. Neither is a bowl of noodles with tofu in it. This is not a matter of carelessness on the cook's part; it is how the food is seasoned.

Com chay: the real answer

Vietnam has a large Buddhist vegetarian tradition and, with it, a whole category of restaurant: com chay, literally vegetarian rice. These places are entirely plant-based — no fish sauce, no meat stock — and they are inexpensive, common, and often clustered near pagodas.

Look for the word "chay" on a sign, or the Buddhist swastika symbol, which in this context marks a vegetarian kitchen. Many are canteen-style: you point at what you want from a tray of dishes and pay by the plate.

The lunar calendar

Many Vietnamese Buddhists eat vegetarian on the first and fifteenth days of the lunar month. On those days, com chay places are at their busiest and best stocked, and ordinary restaurants often add vegetarian options. It is a good time to be eating this way.

Regional differences

The south is the easiest. Buddhist practice is more widespread there, com chay places are more numerous, and the southern cooking's heavier use of coconut milk and vegetables translates well. Ho Chi Minh City in particular has a dense network of both canteens and modern vegan restaurants.

The centre is harder, because Hue and Hoi An's signature dishes are built around pork, shrimp and fish — though Hue, as a Buddhist centre with a long monastic tradition, has excellent vegetarian cooking if you look for it. The north sits between the two, with shrimp paste appearing more often than elsewhere.

The useful phrases

Toi an chay — I eat vegetarian. This is the single most useful thing to be able to say, and in a Buddhist context it is well understood to mean no meat, no fish, no fish sauce.

Khong nuoc mam — no fish sauce. Say it explicitly, because "vegetarian" in a non-Buddhist restaurant may not be heard as excluding it.

Khong thit, khong ca, khong trung, khong sua — no meat, no fish, no egg, no milk, for vegan requirements. Writing these down and showing them works better than pronunciation.

Dishes that work

Many standard dishes have genuine chay versions: pho chay, bun rieu chay, banh mi chay, goi cuon chay with tofu and herbs, com tam chay. These are not compromises invented for visitors — Buddhist cooking has made mock-meat versions of the national dishes for a long time, and some of them are very good.

Beyond that: fresh spring rolls without the shrimp, banh xeo made without pork, rau muong xao toi if you confirm no fish sauce, and the enormous variety of vegetable, tofu and mushroom dishes in a com chay canteen.

Vegan specifically

Com chay cooking is largely vegan already, because Buddhist dietary practice in Vietnam generally excludes dairy and often egg. That makes Vietnam considerably easier for vegans than many countries, once you are inside the chay network.

Outside it, egg is the common hidden ingredient — in banh mi, in some noodle dishes, in desserts. Dairy barely features in traditional Vietnamese cooking, but condensed milk is everywhere in drinks. Ask for coffee black, or with coconut milk where it is offered.

Where it gets hard

Street food, ironically, because stalls make one dish one way and have no vegetarian version. Smaller towns, where com chay places are fewer. And group situations, where the shared dishes are the point.

Finding places

The mapping apps list com chay restaurants and the vegetarian-specific apps cover the cities well. In Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi there is also a growing number of modern vegan restaurants aimed at a younger Vietnamese and international clientele, which are a different and more expensive proposition from the canteens.

Near any significant pagoda, there will be vegetarian food close by. That is a reliable rule and it works in small towns as well as cities.

Breakfast

The hardest meal, because the standard Vietnamese breakfast is a meat-based noodle soup. The workable options are xoi, sticky rice, sold from baskets in the morning in both sweet and savoury versions; banh mi ordered without the pork and pate; and the com chay places that open early, which many do.

How it actually goes

Most vegetarian visitors to Vietnam eat extremely well, and the ones who struggle are usually the ones who tried to make the general restaurant scene work rather than using the chay network. Switch your default from "what can I remove from this dish" to "where is the nearest chay place", and the country becomes straightforward.


----- ARTICLE: Vietnamese Desserts and Sweet Things  (slug vietnamese-dessert-guide)
EXCERPT: Che, sticky rice, fruit and yoghurt coffee. Dessert here is mostly a street snack rather than a course.

Vietnamese cooking does not really do dessert as a final course at the end of a meal. What it has instead is a large category of sweet things eaten through the day — from stalls, from carts, from dedicated shops — and they are among the easiest and cheapest pleasures of the country.

Che

The central category. Che is a family of sweet soups and puddings, served hot or cold, built from beans, pulses, jellies, tapioca, fruit and coconut milk. A che shop will have a dozen or more on the counter, often in glass jars, and you point.

The common ones: che dau xanh, mung bean; che ba mau, the three-colour one with red bean, mung bean and pandan jelly over crushed ice and coconut milk; che chuoi, banana with tapioca and coconut; che troi nuoc, glutinous rice balls with mung bean filling in a ginger syrup, served warm.

It is sweet, it is texturally busy, and it is worth going in without strong preconceptions. Che is not trying to be a Western dessert and it does not taste like one.

Sticky rice and cakes

Xoi is glutinous rice, and it runs both savoury and sweet. The sweet versions — xoi xeo with mung bean, xoi gac with its orange gac fruit colouring, xoi la dua with pandan — are breakfast and snack food sold from baskets on the pavement.

Banh chung and banh tet, the square and cylindrical glutinous rice cakes wrapped in leaves, are lunar new year food and are savoury rather than sweet, filled with pork and mung bean. You will meet them around Tet and not much otherwise.

Banh flan

Crème caramel, inherited from the French and completely absorbed into Vietnamese food. Sold everywhere, often with coffee poured over it or with crushed ice, and usually very good.

Banh and the leaf-wrapped things

A whole family of small cakes steamed in banana or pandan leaf, sold in markets and at pagodas. Banh da lon is a layered steamed cake of mung bean and pandan with a distinctive chewy texture. Banh bo is a honeycomb-textured rice cake. Banh ran and banh cam are the sesame-coated fried glutinous rice balls with a mung bean filling.

These are snack food rather than dessert, eaten through the afternoon, and they are a few thousand dong each.

Fruit

The thing most visitors underuse. Vietnam grows an enormous range, and the markets sell it cut and ready. Mangosteen, rambutan, longan, dragon fruit, jackfruit, custard apple, pomelo and several varieties of mango, depending on the season.

Durian divides people exactly as advertised; it is banned in many hotels for its smell, and that is not a joke. Try it from a stall rather than bringing it to your room.

Sinh to is the fruit smoothie, blended with ice and condensed milk. Nuoc mia is fresh sugarcane juice, pressed in front of you, and is the best thing to drink in the heat.

The coffee-adjacent sweets

Sua chua — yoghurt — is a serious category here, and not a health food. Sua chua nep cam is yoghurt with fermented black sticky rice. Sua chua ca phe is yoghurt with coffee, which sounds wrong and is excellent. Frozen yoghurt stalls sell it as a block to be eaten with a spoon.

Egg coffee, the Hanoi invention, sits somewhere between a drink and a dessert and belongs in both conversations.

Regional specialities

Hue has its own family of che, including one made with candied lotus seeds wrapped in longan, which was court food. Hoi An has banh it la gai, a dark sticky cake wrapped in leaves. The south has the widest range of coconut-based sweets, and the Mekong delta is where the coconut candy workshops are.

Kem

Ice cream, and Vietnam has a long history with it. Trang Tien in Hanoi is the institution, sold from a window to a permanent crowd, in flavours including green bean and coconut sticky rice.

What to expect from the sweetness

Vietnamese sweets are sweet in a different register from European ones — less fat, less flour, more texture from beans, jellies and tapioca, and coconut milk doing the work that cream does elsewhere. There is very little baking in the tradition; almost everything is steamed, boiled or fried.

That means a plate of che is lighter than it looks, and also that someone expecting cake will not find it. The French-derived things — the flan, the baguette, the pastries in the old patisseries of Hanoi and Da Lat — are the exception.

Where to find all this

Che shops are their own category of business and are usually open from the afternoon into the evening. Markets sell cut fruit and sticky rice in the morning. The yoghurt and ice cream places run late.

Almost none of it is expensive, and the whole category is best approached by pointing at something you do not recognise and finding out what it is.


----- ARTICLE: Street Food Tours in Hanoi and Saigon: Worth It?  (slug best-street-food-tours-hanoi-saigon)
EXCERPT: Yes, on the first evening, and no later than that. What a good one does that you cannot do alone.

Street food tours are one of the most heavily sold activities in both Vietnamese cities, and the honest answer about whether to take one depends almost entirely on when you take it.

What a tour actually gives you

Three things you cannot easily get on your own, at least not on day one.

Navigation. The good street food is sold by places with no English, no menu, one dish, and a specific hour. A guide takes you to five or six of them in an order that makes sense, and you eat things you would have walked past.

Explanation. What is in it, why it is made that way, which region it comes from, how to eat it. That turns a meal into a framework you then use for the rest of the trip.

Confidence. This is the underrated one. After two hours of being shown how to read a stall, you stop being nervous about street food, and the rest of the trip is better for it.

So: the first evening

That is the argument for taking one early rather than late. A tour on your last night is a nice meal. A tour on your first night changes how you eat for two weeks.

Walking or on the back of a scooter

Two formats. Walking tours cover a neighbourhood — usually the Old Quarter in Hanoi, District 1 or District 4 in Ho Chi Minh City — and move between stops on foot.

Scooter tours put you on the back of a bike with a driver-guide and cover much more ground, including the districts a walking tour cannot reach. They are the more memorable of the two and they are also a motorbike ride through Vietnamese city traffic, which some people will not want.

As a passenger, you do not need a licence. Riding yourself is a different matter and not what these tours involve.

How much food is involved

More than people expect. A six-stop tour is a full dinner and then some, and the better guides pace it deliberately — a half portion here, a shared plate there. Do not eat beforehand, and say early if you are slowing down; a good guide adjusts rather than marching you through the remaining stops.

Most run two to four hours in the evening, though morning tours exist and are the better option if you want to see pho and the breakfast culture rather than the evening food.

What separates a good tour

Ask these before booking. How many people are in the group — four to eight is workable, fifteen is a procession that changes how the stalls treat you. How many stops, and is each one a dish or a taste. Is the guide a local resident of that neighbourhood. Are drinks included. Does it finish with a beer somewhere, which the better ones do.

Ask also whether the stops are places the guide eats at or places that have an arrangement with the company. The answer tells you a lot, and a guide who answers it straightforwardly is usually the right guide.

Dietary requirements

Tell them when you book, not on the night. Vegetarian street food tours exist in both cities and are a better option than a standard tour with substitutions, because the stops themselves change.

Hanoi versus Saigon

The two cities give quite different tours. Hanoi's Old Quarter is dense and walkable, the food is northern — bun cha, pho, cha ca, bun rieu — and a walking tour covers a lot in a small area. It usually ends with bia hoi.

Ho Chi Minh City is spread out, which is why the scooter format originated here and works so well. The food is southern and more varied, with more Chinese influence in Cholon and the seafood and snail restaurants in the outer districts.

If you are doing both cities, taking a tour in each is not redundant. The food is genuinely different.

What it costs, and the alternative

A tour costs many times what the food itself does. You are paying for the guide, and on the first night that is good value. For the rest of the trip, the alternative is to use what you learned: find the specialist places, go at the right hour, eat where the queue is.

A cheaper version of the same thing is a food-focused free walking tour, which several student groups run in both cities, where you pay for your own food and tip the guide.

Safety and hygiene on a tour

A guide takes you to places they use, which is a reasonable filter, but it is not a guarantee against an upset stomach and no tour can offer one. The usual precautions apply the same as anywhere: the vaccinations are the part decided before you travel, and carrying rehydration salts is the part that assumes it might happen anyway.

The verdict

Take one, early, in whichever city you reach first. Choose a small group. Ask about the stops. Then spend the rest of the trip eating on your own, which is what the tour is for.


----- ARTICLE: Cooking Classes in Vietnam: What to Look For  (slug best-cooking-classes-vietnam)
EXCERPT: The market visit is the part that matters, and most classes rush it. How to choose one worth the half day.

Cooking classes are offered in every town a visitor passes through, and they range from genuinely instructive half-days to a token chopping session attached to a lunch. The difference is visible in the itinerary before you book.

The market visit is the test

Almost every class advertises a market visit. The question is whether it is forty-five minutes of actually buying the ingredients you will cook, with the teacher explaining what each one is and how to choose it, or a ten-minute walk-through for photographs before the minibus.

The market is where you learn the things that stay with you: what the herbs are called, how to tell good fish sauce, what rice paper thickness is for, why there are four kinds of basil. The cooking itself is mostly following instructions.

Ask how long is spent at the market and whether you buy the ingredients there. A class that cannot answer is telling you the answer.

Group size and stations

The second question. A class where everyone has their own station and cooks every dish is a different thing from a demonstration where the group collectively makes one dish and watches the rest.

Eight to twelve is a workable group with individual stations. Twenty-plus is a demonstration regardless of what it is called. Ask explicitly: do I cook each dish myself, or do we share?

What you will actually cook

The standard menu across most classes is three to five dishes: fresh spring rolls, a salad such as green papaya or banana flower, a main — banh xeo, clay pot fish, or a grilled dish — and sometimes a soup. These are chosen because they are quick, forgiving and photograph well.

Nothing wrong with that, but if you want to learn pho you will need to look specifically, because a proper broth takes longer than a class does. Classes that teach it generally start the stock the day before and show you the finishing.

Where to take one

Hoi An is the most established place for it, with classes on farms outside town, on boats, and in restaurant kitchens. Many include a bicycle ride or a basket-boat trip to the herb village at Tra Que, which is a genuine working market garden rather than a set piece.

Hanoi teaches northern food — bun cha, cha ca, pho — and the classes are more often city-based, built around a market trip to one of the Old Quarter markets.

Hue is the interesting one if you already cook. The court cuisine is more technical, the dishes are smaller and more precise, and a Hue class will teach you things the others do not.

Ho Chi Minh City covers southern cooking and has the widest range of formats, from hotel schools to home kitchens.

Home kitchens

Smaller operations run classes in a family home, usually for four to six people, and often cook the family's own dishes rather than the standard tourist menu. These are the ones people remember, and they are harder to find because they advertise less.

What you should come away with

A good class sends you home with the recipes in writing, in quantities you can actually buy, with notes on substitutions for ingredients you will not find. Ask whether recipes are provided.

It should also teach you the two or three things that are structural rather than dish-specific: how nuoc cham is balanced, how to handle rice paper, how a Vietnamese plate of herbs is put together. Those transfer to anything you cook afterwards.

What it costs relative to other activities

A half-day class is among the more expensive single activities in a Vietnamese town, several times the cost of a street food tour per hour, because it includes ingredients, a teacher and a meal. The home-kitchen operations are usually the cheapest and often the best, and the hotel schools the most expensive.

Practical points

Most classes run a morning or an afternoon and include the meal you cook, which means you do not need to plan lunch or dinner around it. Say in advance if you are vegetarian — most will adapt the menu properly if given notice.

Wear clothes you can cook in and shoes you can walk a market in. Markets are wet underfoot. Book a day or two ahead in Hoi An, where the good classes fill.

With children

Several classes take families and are good at it — the spring-roll rolling and the pancake flipping are the parts children engage with. Check whether knives and hot woks are part of what they will be doing and whether that suits.

Is it worth the half day

If you cook at home, yes, and the Hue or home-kitchen versions especially. If you do not cook, a class is still a good way to spend a morning in a market with someone who can explain it, which is most of the value anyway.

If your time is tight, a street food tour gives you more of the eating and less of the technique in half the time. They are different activities and the choice between them is about whether you want to understand the food or to find it.


----- ARTICLE: What to Bring Home from a Vietnamese Kitchen  (slug vietnamese-cooking-ingredients-to-bring-home)
EXCERPT: What is worth the suitcase space, where to buy it, and what customs on both ends will say.

Vietnam is a good country to shop in for a kitchen, because the things worth buying are cheap, light and hard to get at home. Here is what actually earns its space, and what to know about getting it back.

Fish sauce

The one to buy properly. Good nuoc mam is made from anchovies and salt and nothing else, fermented in wooden vats for a year or more. Phu Quoc has a protected designation for it and the island's warehouses sell it directly; Cat Hai in the north is the other named producer.

Look at the label for the degree of protein, usually given as a number of degrees N. Higher is stronger and more expensive, and the best is drunk-tasted the way olive oil is. Buy glass if you can and bubble-wrap it; a bottle that breaks in a suitcase is a memorable problem.

Coffee and a phin

Vietnamese coffee is cheap and good, and the small metal phin filter costs almost nothing and is the right way to brew it. Buy both together.

Check whether the coffee is pure or roasted with butter or other additives — much mass-market Vietnamese coffee is, which gives the characteristic flavour but behaves differently in a home machine. Specialty roasters in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City and Da Lat sell single-origin Vietnamese arabica if that is what you want.

Pepper

Vietnam is one of the world's largest pepper producers and the stuff sold in the markets is fresher than most of what reaches a Western supermarket. Phu Quoc pepper has a reputation of its own. Buy whole peppercorns, not ground.

Dried and shelf-stable things

Rice paper for fresh rolls, which is light, flat and almost indestructible in a bag.

Dried rice noodles in the widths you cannot buy at home — the flat pho noodles and the thin bun vermicelli.

Shrimp paste and fermented bean curd if you are serious; both are pungent and need sealing well.

Dried shiitake, wood ear and lily buds, which are light and keep for years.

Cinnamon and star anise, both grown in Vietnam, for pho broth.

Sauces beyond fish sauce

Hoisin and chilli sauce are made everywhere and the Vietnamese versions differ from what is sold abroad. Tuong ot, the Vietnamese chilli sauce, is thinner and sharper than the better-known Thai kind. Mam nem, fermented anchovy sauce, and mam ruoc, shrimp paste, are the strong ones and need serious sealing.

Sate, the chilli-and-lemongrass oil served with bun bo Hue, is underrated as a thing to bring back and transforms a bowl of noodles at home.

Tea

Vietnamese green tea, lotus tea and the artichoke tea from Da Lat all travel well. Lotus tea in particular is specific to here and makes a good present. The tea shops in the markets will vacuum-seal it.

Kitchen equipment

A claypot, a rice paper mould, a wooden mortar — all cheap, all heavy. The phin is the one piece of equipment that is genuinely worth the weight.

Lacquerware, textiles and the non-edible

Outside the kitchen cupboard, the things that travel well are lacquerware bowls and trays, which are light and genuinely made here; embroidered and block-printed textiles; and the conical hat, which is impossible to pack and which people buy anyway.

Ceramics from Bat Trang, the pottery village outside Hanoi, are the other category — heavy, breakable, and worth it if you are checking a bag and pack carefully.

Where to buy

Supermarkets are the easiest and the prices are fixed: the large Vietnamese chains stock everything above, often better packaged for travel than the markets. Markets are cheaper and more interesting but you are buying loose and bargaining.

Airport shops sell most of this at a premium, which is worth knowing as a fallback rather than a plan. Buy the liquids in town and the last-minute things at the airport.

What not to bother with

Fresh herbs, which will not survive and will not clear customs. Fresh lemongrass and galangal, for the same reason — the frozen versions at an Asian supermarket at home are better than what arrives after a long flight. Anything requiring refrigeration. And the branded instant noodles, which are sold at home for roughly the same price.

Getting it home

Two separate sets of rules. On the way out, Vietnam's own allowances apply to what you are carrying, and goods beyond a total customs value of 10,000,000 dong per entry must be declared.

On the way in, your own country's agricultural and customs rules are the binding ones, and they are stricter about food than most people expect. Processed, commercially packaged, shelf-stable goods are generally the easy category; fresh produce, meat and some seeds are generally not. Check your own border agency's current list rather than assuming, and declare food when asked — declaring something that turns out to be permitted costs you nothing, and not declaring something prohibited can cost a great deal.

Liquids and flights

Fish sauce, chilli sauce and any liquid go in checked baggage, double-bagged. The smell survives a loose cap, and so does the stain.
