----- ARTICLE: Vietnam and Cambodia Together: How to Combine Them  (slug vietnam-and-cambodia-combined-itinerary)
EXCERPT: Two countries that fit well on one trip, and one visa decision that has to be right before you book anything.

Vietnam and Cambodia are neighbours, the flights between them are short and cheap, and Angkor is close enough to the southern end of Vietnam that combining the two is one of the most common long trips in the region. The combination is straightforward. The visa sequencing is where people come unstuck.

The visa decision, first

This has to be settled before the tickets, not after. The entry and exit location on an e-visa must match the port chosen in the application. An e-visa naming Tan Son Nhat does not work at a land or river border, and an error on it can mean refused entry.

So the shape of the trip decides the visa. If you enter Vietnam by air and leave by air, name the airports. If you plan to cross overland or by river to Cambodia, the visa has to name that crossing. And Vietnam does not allow you to renew or extend an e-visa while you are in the country — so this is a thing to get right at the application stage.

If you return to Vietnam

A single-entry visa is spent when you leave. Vietnam, then Cambodia, then Vietnam again needs either a valid multiple-entry visa covering the return, or a second visa approved before you come back, applied for from outside Vietnam. An application in progress is not permission to enter.

Cambodia's own rules are set by Cambodia and change independently; check them with the Cambodian authorities rather than with a tour operator.

The three shapes that work

Vietnam north to south, then Cambodia, out of Phnom Penh or Siem Reap. The cleanest version. Fly into Hanoi, work down the country, cross to Cambodia from Ho Chi Minh City or the delta, and fly home from Siem Reap. One Vietnamese entry, one exit, no backtracking.

Cambodia first, then Vietnam south to north, out of Hanoi. The same thing reversed, and the better choice if the cheap flight lands you in Cambodia.

Vietnam, Cambodia, Vietnam. Only if you have a specific reason, because it is the version that needs the multiple-entry visa and a second border crossing. Usually the reason is a return flight you cannot change.

How to cross

By air is the simplest: Ho Chi Minh City to Phnom Penh is about an hour in the air, and to Siem Reap not much more. Hanoi to Siem Reap and Phnom Penh also run. An airport entry is the straightforward case for both countries' paperwork.

By road through the Moc Bai crossing, buses run from Ho Chi Minh City to Phnom Penh in six to seven hours. By river, boats run from Chau Doc in the Mekong Delta up to Phnom Penh, which takes most of a day and turns the transfer into part of the trip.

How many days each

Cambodia's essential list is shorter than Vietnam's. Siem Reap and Angkor need three days to be done properly — two is possible and rushed. Phnom Penh needs two, including Tuol Sleng and Choeung Ek, which are heavy and should not be compressed into a morning.

So a minimum useful Cambodia leg is five or six days on the ground. Against Vietnam's ten to fourteen, that makes a combined trip three weeks rather than two. Two weeks across both countries means doing Vietnam in a week, which is two regions, and Cambodia in five days.

The common mistake

Adding Angkor to a two-week Vietnam trip as a three-day excursion. The flights and the transfers eat a day at each end, Angkor itself wants three, and the week that remains cannot hold Vietnam's three regions. Something has to be cut and it is better to decide which than to discover it.

What Cambodia adds that Vietnam does not have

Angkor, principally. There is nothing in Vietnam at that scale, and the Cham towers at My Son — which are the closest relatives architecturally — are a fraction of the size and much more ruined. Seeing My Son after Angkor makes it feel small; seeing it before makes Angkor overwhelming. Either order works, and the order you choose changes both.

Phnom Penh's Khmer Rouge sites are the other thing. They are heavier than Vietnam's war sites and they are closer in time, and a trip that includes both needs some space between them rather than four days of difficult material in a row.

Practical points

Currencies differ: Cambodia uses the riel alongside US dollars, and small dollar notes are genuinely useful there in a way they are not in Vietnam. Carry some.

The climates are similar and the seasons broadly align — the southern Vietnamese wet season from roughly May to October overlaps Cambodia's, and Angkor in the wet months is greener, quieter and punctuated by afternoon downpours.

Booking order

Work out the crossing first, because it determines the visa. Then apply for the Vietnamese e-visa naming the right ports. Then the international flights, open-jaw into one country and out of the other. Then the internal flights, then accommodation.

How Vietnam fits into a longer Southeast Asia route, with Laos or Thailand on it as well, is a question the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: Cai Rang Floating Market: How to See It Properly  (slug can-tho-floating-market-guide)
EXCERPT: A working wholesale market on the water that is finishing by the time most visitors arrive. Everything depends on the hour.

Cai Rang, on the Hau river just outside Can Tho, is the largest floating market in the Mekong Delta and the reason most people stay the night there. It is a wholesale market — boats selling produce to other boats — and it runs early. Almost everything about seeing it well comes down to what time you are on the water.

What it actually is

Larger boats moor mid-river with their produce hung from a tall pole at the bow, so buyers can see from a distance what each one is selling: pineapples, watermelons, pumpkins, cabbages. Smaller boats work between them, buying, loading and ferrying. Among them are the ones selling coffee and noodle soup to the traders.

It is a working market rather than a performance. Nobody is there for the visitors, which is what makes it worth the early alarm — and also means it keeps the hours of a wholesale market rather than a tourist attraction.

The hour

Trading starts before dawn and thins through the morning. By mid-morning the larger boats are leaving and what remains is a handful of vessels and other tourist boats.

So: on the water by five or soon after, at Cai Rang for first light, and back for breakfast. A boat leaving Can Tho at eight arrives at the end of it. This is the single reason a day trip from Ho Chi Minh City cannot work — the city is several hours away and the market is over before a morning departure could reach it.

Booking a boat

Boats are arranged the evening before, through your accommodation or directly at the Ninh Kieu quay in Can Tho. A private boat for two or three people is inexpensive and is the better option, because you set the departure time and the route rather than taking the operator's.

Agree three things: the departure time, how long you are on the water, and whether the trip continues to Phong Dien, the smaller and less visited market further upstream, or to the fruit orchards and the rice noodle workshops along the canals.

What to ask for

Ask the boatman to cut the engine among the moored boats and drift rather than circling. Ask to stop at one of the coffee boats, which come alongside and pass across a glass. And ask for the narrower canals on the way back, which are the part people remember as much as the market.

What to bring on the boat

A layer, because the river before dawn is cooler than the delta at any other hour. Small notes in dong for the coffee boats and anything you buy from a vendor. Something waterproof for a camera, since the boats sit low and the wash from passing vessels comes over the side.

And a seat cushion if your boat is a plain wooden one, which most of the small private ones are.

Where to stay

Can Tho is a city with the full range of accommodation, from riverside hotels on Ninh Kieu to guesthouses in town. Homestays on the surrounding canals are the other option and are quieter, though they add travel time to the pre-dawn start.

For the market specifically, being close to the quay is worth more than being somewhere pretty, because you are leaving in the dark.

Getting to Can Tho

Buses run frequently from Ho Chi Minh City's western bus station and take three to four hours; private cars are quicker. There is also an airport with domestic flights, including from Hanoi and Phu Quoc, which is useful if you are fitting the delta between two other regions.

The rest of Can Tho

Worth the afternoon before. The riverfront at Ninh Kieu, the night market, Binh Thuy ancient house, and the Khmer and Chinese temples in the centre. The city is pleasant and almost entirely unvisited by foreign tourists outside the market run.

Is the market changing

Worth knowing: floating markets across the delta have been reported as shrinking for years as road transport replaces river freight. Cai Rang is the largest and the most resilient, and it is still a working market, but it is smaller than it was and the direction of travel is one way.

That is a reason to see it rather than a reason not to, and it is also a reason not to expect the scale in the older photographs.

And the other markets

Phong Dien, about forty minutes further on, is smaller, more local and visited by far fewer boats of tourists. Cai Be, in Tien Giang province closer to Ho Chi Minh City, is the one many day trips use and is much diminished.

Whether to come back up to Ho Chi Minh City afterwards or carry on west towards the Cambodian border is a question the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: Vietnam's Beaches and Islands: Which One for Which Trip  (slug best-beaches-islands-vietnam)
EXCERPT: Three thousand kilometres of coast and no single best beach. A map of which one suits which trip, and which month.

Vietnam has a very long coastline and a large number of islands, and the beaches are genuinely different from each other — in development, in season, and in how hard they are to reach. There is no single answer to which is best. There is a reasonably clear answer to which suits a particular trip.

Start with the month

This is the part that decides more than preference does, because the coast does not have one season. The central coast, which includes Hoi An, Da Nang and Nha Trang's northern neighbours, has a storm season from roughly September to November. Nha Trang's own dry stretch runs roughly January to August. Phu Quoc's dry season is roughly November to April, the opposite end of the year.

So the useful question is not which beach is best but which beach is dry when you are travelling.

The ones on the main route

An Bang and the Hoi An beaches. The convenient choice: ten to fifteen minutes from the Ancient Town by bicycle, low-rise, with beach restaurants. It is not the best sand in the country and it does not need to be, because it is a beach afternoon inside a trip about something else.

My Khe, Da Nang. A long city beach with a wall of hotels behind it, clean and used heavily by locals early and late. Best treated as a city with a beach rather than a beach destination.

Nha Trang. Six kilometres of municipal beach in front of a working city, with islands and diving offshore. On the main overland route and the easiest beach day to drop into a north-south itinerary.

The islands worth a detour

Phu Quoc. The largest island, the best sand, an international airport, and the most development. Sao Beach is the postcard; the south is heavily built; the north is quieter. It is a beach holiday rather than a stop.

Con Dao. The quiet one. Empty beaches, a national park, limited flights and a prison complex that is a significant historical site. Four or five days minimum because of the transport, and the reason to go is that almost nobody does.

Cat Ba and Lan Ha. Northern, and the beaches are small — the point here is the limestone bay rather than the sand. The best beach-adjacent experience in the north is kayaking, not lying down.

The ones people miss

Quy Nhon, halfway between Hoi An and Nha Trang, is an undeveloped coastal city on the railway with good beaches and very few foreign visitors. Mui Ne has wind and dunes rather than swimming. Doc Let, north of Nha Trang, is a long white beach an hour from the city. The Cham Islands off Hoi An are a day trip in calm weather.

What the development looks like

Worth saying plainly, because the photographs do not. Da Nang and Nha Trang are walls of high-rise hotels behind a public beach. Phu Quoc's south has a large resort and entertainment complex and its north is being built on. Mui Ne has lost sand to erosion in places and several resorts have sea walls where beach used to be.

The undeveloped coast exists — Con Dao, Quy Nhon, stretches north of Nha Trang — and it is undeveloped because it is harder to reach. That trade is the whole geography of Vietnamese beaches in one sentence.

Matching the beach to the trip

A first two-week trip north to south: An Bang for the afternoons, and Nha Trang if the route and the month allow. No detour needed.

A trip that should end on sand: Phu Quoc, which has the flights and the sand, or Con Dao if you have the days and want nobody there.

A trip with children: An Bang or Da Nang, both of which have the infrastructure and the food within reach, or Phu Quoc for a longer stay.

A trip where the water is the point: Nha Trang or Con Dao for diving, Lan Ha for kayaking, Mui Ne for wind.

What to expect from the sea

Conditions change with the season everywhere on this coast. Flags and local advice are the thing to follow rather than how the water looks, and rip currents are a real hazard on the open-coast beaches in the windy months.

Lifeguards are present on the municipal beaches at Da Nang and Nha Trang and are not a given elsewhere.

Getting to each one

An Bang and My Khe need no detour at all. Nha Trang is on the railway and the highway. Phu Quoc and Con Dao are flights. Quy Nhon is on the railway and is the easiest of the quiet ones to reach. Cat Ba is a bus and a ferry from Hanoi.

Development

Vietnam's coast is building fast, and a beach that was quiet five years ago may not be now. Recent reviews and recent photographs are worth more here than anywhere else in the country when you are choosing.


----- ARTICLE: Vietnam's Markets: Which Ones Are Worth Your Morning  (slug best-markets-in-vietnam)
EXCERPT: The famous ones are for visitors and the good ones are not. Where each kind is, and how to use them.

Markets in Vietnam divide cleanly into two kinds: the ones a city sends visitors to, and the ones where people buy their food. Both are worth seeing and they want different things from you.

The visitor markets

Ben Thanh, Ho Chi Minh City. The best known in the country, under a landmark clock tower in District 1. Clothing, souvenirs, coffee and spices, and a food court at the back that is the best part of it. Prices are not marked and bargaining is expected. An hour, not an afternoon.

Dong Xuan, Hanoi. The Old Quarter's big covered market, three floors, more wholesale than Ben Thanh and less aimed at foreigners despite its fame. The surrounding streets at night become a market of their own at the weekend.

Hoi An central market. Small, photogenic, on the river at the edge of the Ancient Town, with a food hall and a lot of fabric. It is both a working market and a visitor attraction at the same time, which is unusual.

The working markets

Binh Tay, Cholon. The wholesale market for the western half of Ho Chi Minh City, in the Chinese quarter, and a completely different experience from Ben Thanh — bulk goods, almost no foreign visitors, and a scale that takes a while to absorb.

Dong Ba, Hue. The city's main market on the river by the Citadel, with the conical hats the city is known for, and a food section that is where to eat in Hue away from the restaurants.

Han Market and Con Market, Da Nang. Han is the central one and sees visitors; Con is bigger, scruffier and is where the city actually shops and eats.

Tan Dinh, Ho Chi Minh City. The fabric market, near the pink church, and the place to go if you want cloth rather than clothes.

The ones that are a destination in themselves

Cai Rang, Can Tho. The floating wholesale market on the Hau river, which runs before dawn and is finishing by mid-morning. It needs an overnight in Can Tho and an early boat.

Bac Ha, Lao Cai province. A Sunday highland market near Sapa where Hmong and other communities come in from the surrounding valleys. It is several hours from Sapa and it runs one day a week, which means planning a visit around it.

The valley markets around Ha Giang and Sapa. Smaller versions on fixed weekdays, and the reason to check which day you will be where.

What a Vietnamese market is organised like

By trade rather than by stall. Fresh produce and meat at one end, usually at the back or on the ground floor; dry goods, spices and rice in the middle; fabric, clothing and household goods above or along one side; and the food section, which is a row of counters selling one dish each, generally near an entrance.

That layout repeats across the country, which means once you can read one market you can read all of them. The food section is the part to find first.

How to use a market well

Go early. Food markets do their real business between about six and nine in the morning, and by midday the fresh produce has gone and the sellers are packing up. This is the single biggest difference between a market that feels alive and one that feels like a shed.

Eat there. Almost every market of any size has a food section and it is generally cheaper and better than the restaurants nearby.

Settle prices before anything is wrapped, in dong, and count your change. Where goods have tickets on them, the price is usually fixed; where they do not, it is not.

Photography

Ask before photographing a stallholder. Many are happy and some are not, and buying something first changes the exchange entirely. Avoid blocking a narrow aisle for a shot during the busy hours; people are working.

What is actually worth buying

Coffee, pepper, cinnamon, dried fruit and cashews — compact, good value and they travel. Lacquerware and ceramics if you can pack them. Fabric by the metre at Tan Dinh or Dong Ba.

Less so: branded clothing and electronics, where the gap between what you are told and what you are buying is widest, and anything you have not seen the price of elsewhere first.

A note on the meat and fish sections

They are not sanitised and they are not hidden. Live fish, poultry and whole animals are handled in the open, which is normal here and startling to some visitors. If that is not something you want to walk through, the food and dry-goods sections are usually reachable from a different entrance — ask, or walk the perimeter first.

The night markets

A separate category, aimed at visitors and at young Vietnamese: Hoi An's lantern-lit riverside, the streets around Ben Thanh after the hall closes, Hanoi's weekend Old Quarter market, and Da Lat's. They are food and souvenirs rather than produce, and they are an evening out rather than a shopping trip.


----- ARTICLE: Photographing Vietnam: Where to Be, and When  (slug best-photography-spots-in-vietnam)
EXCERPT: The light and the hour matter more than the location. Twelve places and the time of day each one wants.

Vietnam photographs well almost everywhere, which is the problem — it is easy to come home with a thousand pictures and no good ones. The locations below are worth planning around, and in every case the time of day does more work than the spot.

The north

Ha Long and Lan Ha bays. First light from the deck, before the other boats move. An overnight cruise is the only way to be on the water at that hour. Kayak level gives you the scale that a boat deck does not.

The Sapa terraces. Gold in late September and October before the harvest, mirrored water in April and May after planting. Early morning for the mist sitting in the valleys; late afternoon for the side light across the terraces.

Ha Giang. The most dramatic landscape in the country, and the road itself is the subject. Mid-morning once the valley cloud lifts.

Hanoi's Old Quarter. Early, between five and seven, when the market is setting up and the streets are empty enough to see. The Long Bien bridge at the same hour, with the trains and the bicycles.

Ninh Binh. From the top of the Hang Mua steps, late afternoon, looking down the river between the karsts.

The centre

Hoi An Ancient Town. Two windows and nothing in between. Before seven, when the streets are empty and the light is low along the river. And after nine at night, when the lanterns are the only light and the day visitors have gone. The fourteenth night of the lunar month, when the electric lights go off, is the one to plan for.

The Hai Van Pass and the coastal railway. From the train, sea side, in the morning. From the road, at the summit, whenever the cloud allows — which is the whole difficulty.

My Son. At opening, when the light comes in low through the trees and the coaches have not arrived.

Hue's tombs. Mid-morning at Khai Dinh for the interior mosaics, late afternoon at Tu Duc for the overgrown pavilions and the lake.

The south

Cai Rang floating market. First light, from a small boat with the engine off. The coffee boats coming alongside are the picture, not the produce boats from a distance.

Ho Chi Minh City from above. The observation decks and rooftop bars at sunset, when the lights come on and the sky still has colour. Thirty minutes before sunset, not after dark.

The delta canals. Early morning, from water level, with the palms closing overhead.

What to carry

Less than you think. A single body and one zoom covers almost all of this; the situations that reward a second lens are the bay at dawn and the markets, where something fast and wide earns its place. A polariser for the water and the haze.

What matters more than glass here is a strap you are comfortable keeping the camera close with, and something to keep it dry — the afternoon rain in the wet season arrives quickly and the boats are wet.

What makes the difference

Being somewhere at the right hour, which almost always means being there before the people who are not thinking about it. In practice that is a 5am alarm two or three times in a trip, and it is worth every one of them.

Vietnam's light is hard and flat in the middle of the day for most of the year, and the haze on the central and northern coasts flattens distance. Early and late are not stylistic preferences here; they are the only hours the landscape has depth.

The weather as a subject

The grey days are not lost days. Mist in the northern valleys, cloud sitting on the Hai Van Pass, rain on the Hoi An river at night — these produce better photographs than a flat blue sky does, and they are the conditions most visitors wait out indoors. The one thing genuinely ruined by weather is a long view from height.

Photographing people

Ask. A gesture at the camera and a smile is enough, and the answer is usually yes. Buying something from a stallholder first changes the exchange completely. If someone declines, that is the end of it.

Be careful with the street-vendor-with-conical-hat shot in the tourist areas; in several places it has become a paid arrangement, and some people will expect payment after the fact. Agreeing it first avoids the awkwardness.

Drones

Treat them as restricted rather than permitted. Flying a drone in Vietnam is regulated and permission is required; military sites, airports and border areas are sensitive, and several of the most photographed landscapes sit near one of those. Check the current rules before travelling rather than assuming, and do not launch at a heritage site without asking the site first.

Practical

Humidity fogs lenses when you come out of air conditioning — let the camera adjust before shooting. A polariser helps with the haze and the water. And keep your camera out of sight on the street in the cities, because snatch theft from passing motorbikes is the common petty crime and a camera on a strap is a visible target.
