----- ARTICLE: Vietnamese Food: A Guide for First-Timers  (slug vietnamese-food-guide-first-timers)
EXCERPT: How the cuisine is organised, why it changes as you travel, and the twenty dishes that cover most of it.

Vietnamese food is one of the main reasons people come, and it is easier to eat well here than almost anywhere. The difficulty is not quality, it is navigation: the country's regions cook differently, the best food is often sold by places that make one dish, and the menus are not written for you.

The three regions

The single most useful thing to understand. The north — Hanoi and the Red River delta — cooks with restraint: less sugar, less chilli, more black pepper and herbs, clear broths. This is where pho and bun cha come from.

The centre — Hue, Hoi An, Da Nang — is the spiciest and the most elaborate, with a court cuisine tradition behind it and a fondness for small, complicated dishes. Bun bo Hue, the little steamed cakes, cao lau.

The south — Ho Chi Minh City and the Mekong — is sweeter, uses coconut milk and more sugar, and has the greatest abundance of fresh herbs and produce. Southern pho is sweeter and comes with a plate of herbs that a northerner would find excessive.

How a meal is built

Most Vietnamese dishes are assembled by the eater rather than served finished. A bowl of noodles arrives with a plate of herbs, bean sprouts, lime and chilli beside it, and you add what you want. Grilled meat comes with rice paper, lettuce and herbs to wrap it in.

That is not a garnish you can skip. The herbs are a structural part of the dish, and a bowl eaten without them is a different and lesser thing.

The dipping sauce

Nuoc cham — fish sauce thinned with water, lime, sugar, garlic and chilli — is the thread running through the whole cuisine. Almost everything is dipped in it or dressed with it. The balance varies by region and by cook, and it is worth paying attention to how different it tastes in different places.

The dishes to know

Noodle soups: pho, the beef or chicken noodle soup; bun bo Hue, spicier and with lemongrass; hu tieu, the southern pork noodle soup with a clearer, sweeter broth; mi quang, the central turmeric noodle dish with almost no broth.

Rice dishes: com tam, broken rice with grilled pork chop, egg and pickles; com ga, chicken rice; com binh dan, the point-and-choose canteen rice with several dishes on one plate, which is how most Vietnamese people eat lunch.

Rolled and wrapped: goi cuon, the fresh summer roll; nem ran or cha gio, the fried spring roll; banh cuon, steamed rice sheets rolled around pork and mushroom.

Grilled: bun cha, grilled pork with noodles and dipping broth; nem nuong, pork sausage skewers; banh xeo, the crisp folded pancake you cut and wrap.

The sandwich: banh mi, which needs no introduction and varies enormously from cart to cart.

The things people find difficult

Three honest warnings. Fish sauce is in almost everything and its smell is stronger than its taste in a finished dish — most people who think they dislike it are reacting to the bottle rather than the food. Offal and less familiar cuts appear widely, particularly in noodle soups, and are usually listed, so ask if it matters. And coriander is everywhere; if you are one of the people for whom it tastes of soap, learn to say "khong rau mui".

Chilli is almost always served on the side rather than cooked in, outside the centre. You control the heat.

Where to eat

The general rule is that specialisation beats range. A place with one dish written on the sign, a queue at the right hour, and a cook who has made the same thing for years will almost always be better than a restaurant with a laminated menu of forty items.

Plastic stools on the pavement are not a lower tier of restaurant; they are often the top tier. The sit-down places with air conditioning and English menus are generally aimed at visitors and priced for them.

Timing

Many specialist places sell one meal and close. Pho is a breakfast dish and the good shops are out by mid-morning. Bun cha is lunch. Com tam is often late-night. Turning up at two in the afternoon expecting the famous place to be open is the most common way to miss it.

Vegetarian eating

Harder than it looks, because fish sauce is in almost everything, including dishes with no visible animal protein. The solution is the com chay places — Buddhist vegetarian canteens, common across the country and often near pagodas — which are entirely plant-based and inexpensive.

What a day of eating looks like

Breakfast is a hot savoury meal, usually a noodle soup or sticky rice, eaten early and out of the house. Lunch is rice with several dishes, often from a canteen-style place, eaten around eleven or twelve. The afternoon is coffee. Dinner is later and more social, and is where the grilled food, the hotpot and the beer happen.

Eating on that rhythm rather than a Western one is the single easiest way to find the good places open.

Ordering

Pointing works. So does the name of the dish, which is usually also the name of the shop. Prices at specialist street places are generally fixed and posted; where they are not, ask before you sit down.

And eat what the place sells. Asking a bun cha shop for pho is how you get the worst version of both.


----- ARTICLE: Pho, Bun Cha and Banh Mi: What to Order  (slug pho-bun-cha-banh-mi-what-to-order)
EXCERPT: Three dishes you already know by name, and what actually distinguishes a good one from a bad one.

These are the three Vietnamese dishes with international recognition, and all three are easy to eat badly in Vietnam by going to the wrong kind of place. Here is what each one is, where to eat it, and what to look for.

Pho

A rice noodle soup built on a long-simmered broth, served with beef or chicken. Everything is the broth: it is simmered for hours with charred onion and ginger, star anise, cassia and cloves, and a good one is clear, deeply savoury and only faintly sweet.

Northern pho is the original and the plainer version — clear broth, few accompaniments, maybe some spring onion and a wedge of lime. Southern pho is sweeter, usually comes with hoisin and sriracha on the table, and arrives with a plate of bean sprouts, Thai basil, sawtooth herb and lime.

The cuts

Pho bo tai is rare beef, sliced thin and cooked by the hot broth at the table. Chin is well-done brisket. Gau is fatty flank, nam is flank, gan is tendon, bo vien is beef ball. Tai nam is the common default and a good first order. Pho ga is the chicken version, lighter and underrated.

What a good one looks like

Clear broth, not cloudy. Noodles that are soft but still separate. A smell of spice and beef rather than of stock powder. And a shop that sells pho and essentially nothing else, is busy at breakfast, and has a large pot at the front.

Pho is a breakfast and early-lunch dish in Vietnam. A pho shop at eight in the morning and the same shop at four in the afternoon are not the same proposition, and many of the best simply close when the pot is finished.

Bun cha

A Hanoi dish, and arguably the one that rewards a visit to the north most. Grilled pork — both patties and slices of belly — arrives in a bowl of warm, sweet-sour, diluted fish sauce with slices of pickled green papaya and carrot floating in it. Alongside: a plate of cold rice vermicelli and a mountain of herbs.

You assemble it. Noodles and herbs into the bowl of broth and pork, a few at a time, and eat with chopsticks. It is not a soup and the broth is not meant to be drunk, though nobody will stop you.

What to look for

Charcoal. The pork should be grilled over coals, and in Hanoi you can usually smell a bun cha shop before you see it — the smoke from the grills on the pavement at lunchtime is the signal. Pork that has been cooked on a flat-top is a different and much duller dish.

Bun cha is lunch. Most shops open late morning and are done by early afternoon.

Banh mi

A Vietnamese baguette, light and crisp-crusted from the rice flour in the dough, split and filled. The standard build is pate, some form of pork, pickled carrot and daikon, cucumber, coriander, chilli and a sauce.

The variations are endless: banh mi thit nuong with grilled pork, banh mi xiu mai with pork meatballs in tomato sauce, banh mi op la with fried eggs, banh mi cha ca with fish cake. Hoi An has a particularly strong reputation for it.

What separates a good one

The bread, first. It should be audibly crisp and light enough to compress, warmed or toasted before filling. A dense, bready roll is the most common failure.

Then the balance: the pickles and the herbs are not optional, and a banh mi that is mostly meat and mayonnaise has missed the point. A cart with a queue and a charcoal brazier is a better bet than a shop with a photo menu.

The accompaniments, and what to do with them

With pho, in the south: tear the basil and sawtooth herb rather than dropping leaves in whole, add bean sprouts a few at a time so the broth stays hot, and squeeze the lime at the end. Hoisin and sriracha are for the side dish, not for the bowl, if you want to taste the broth you came for — though plenty of Vietnamese people put them straight in.

With bun cha, work in small batches rather than tipping everything in at once; the noodles go soft and the herbs wilt. With banh mi, there is nothing to assemble, but ask for extra chilli if you want it, because the default is mild for a foreign customer.

Prices and how to order

All three are inexpensive at the places that specialise in them, and several times the price at restaurants aimed at visitors. Prices are generally posted at street stalls; where they are not, ask first.

Ordering is usually a matter of saying the dish name and holding up fingers for how many. At a pho shop, saying "tai nam" after "pho" gets you the standard mixed-beef bowl.

Where to eat each one

Pho is everywhere, but the northern version in Hanoi is the one to seek out. Bun cha is a Hanoi dish and is best there. Banh mi is at its best in the south and in Hoi An. If your route covers the whole country, eat each one where it belongs.


----- ARTICLE: Street Food Safety in Vietnam  (slug street-food-safety-vietnam)
EXCERPT: The cautious advice is to avoid street food. The better advice is to choose it well, and to get the right vaccinations first.

Street food is a large part of why Vietnam is worth visiting, and avoiding it means missing most of the country's best eating. It also carries a real risk of stomach illness, which is the most common thing that goes wrong on a trip here. Both of those are true, and the sensible approach sits between them.

Start before you travel

The vaccinations are the part you can actually control, and they are decided weeks before you arrive. The CDC recommends Hepatitis A, Hepatitis B, and typhoid for most travelers travelling to Vietnam, among others. Hepatitis A and typhoid are both food- and water-borne, which is precisely the relevant category here.

Talk to a travel clinic well in advance — some of these need more than one dose, spaced out. This is not something to arrange the week before the flight.

How to choose a stall

The principles are the same ones Vietnamese people use, and they are about turnover rather than appearance.

Busy is the single best indicator. A stall with a queue is selling its stock fast, which means the ingredients have not been sitting out. An empty stall at a busy hour has a reason for being empty.

Watch the food being cooked. Grilled, fried and boiled to order, in front of you, is the lowest-risk category. Food that has been cooked and is sitting at room temperature is the highest.

Look at where the raw and the cooked sit. Separate surfaces and separate tongs are a good sign. One board and one pair of tongs for everything is not.

Specialist beats general

A stall that makes one dish all day gets through its ingredients and knows exactly how to handle them. A cart offering fifteen things is keeping fourteen of them waiting.

Water, ice and salads

Drink bottled or filtered water and check the seal. Tap water is not for drinking anywhere in Vietnam, and that includes brushing your teeth if you want to be careful in the first few days.

Ice is less of a worry than its reputation suggests at established places: commercial ice in Vietnam is generally factory-made from treated water, and the uniform cylinders with a hole through the middle are the industrial kind. Irregular hand-chipped ice is the one to be wary of.

Raw herbs and salads are the genuine grey area, because they are washed in whatever water the stall has. They are also an integral part of most dishes. Busy places with high turnover are the reasonable compromise; if your stomach is already unhappy, that is the week to skip them.

The things most likely to cause trouble

If you want a short list rather than principles: shellfish that has been sitting out, cut fruit prepared hours earlier and kept at room temperature, anything with raw egg or mayonnaise in the heat, and the leftover accompaniments on a table that have clearly been there a while. Rice that has been held warm for a long time is another.

None of these is an absolute rule — a busy stall selling cut fruit all afternoon is turning it over constantly. The underlying question is always the same: how long has this been sitting there?

Easing in

A common and sensible approach is to start with the cooked-to-order things in the first day or two — grilled meat, fried rice, noodle soups where the broth is boiling — and add the raw accompaniments once you have settled. Most people adjust within a few days.

This is about giving yourself a gentler start, not about any one food being dangerous and another safe.

What to carry

Oral rehydration salts are the most useful thing in the bag, and they are cheap and available in any Vietnamese pharmacy. An anti-diarrhoeal is worth having for travel days specifically. Hand sanitiser matters more than most people expect, because you eat with your hands a lot here and many stalls have no washing facilities.

When to see a doctor

Most stomach upsets resolve in a day or two with fluids and rest. Seek medical care for a high fever, blood in the stool, severe or persistent symptoms, or signs of dehydration — and sooner rather than later if you are travelling with children. International clinics operate in the main cities.

Travel insurance

Worth having and worth reading before you need it. Check that it covers medical treatment abroad and, if relevant, evacuation. The international clinics in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City are good and expect payment or a direct insurance arrangement at the point of treatment.

What this all adds up to

Vietnamese street food is not a risk to be endured for the sake of the experience. It is a food culture with its own hygiene logic, and the way to eat it well is to read the signals — turnover, cooking to order, separation of raw and cooked — rather than to avoid the category.

Getting vaccinated, drinking bottled water and choosing busy stalls covers most of it. The rest is the ordinary risk of eating anywhere.


----- ARTICLE: Vietnamese Coffee: What to Order  (slug vietnamese-coffee-guide)
EXCERPT: The second-largest coffee producer in the world drinks it differently from anyone else. What the drinks are, and where to have them.

Vietnam grows an enormous amount of coffee and has built a drinking culture around it that owes very little to European or American habits. Ordering it well is one of the quickest ways to get something genuinely local for almost nothing.

The bean

Most Vietnamese coffee is robusta rather than arabica — stronger, more bitter, higher in caffeine and with a heavier body. That is not an accident of quality; the drinks are built around it. Robusta is why condensed milk works, and why a Vietnamese coffee can take that much sweetness without disappearing.

Arabica and specialty-grade coffee are grown in Vietnam too, mostly around Da Lat, and the third-wave cafes in the cities serve it. That is a parallel scene rather than the mainstream one.

The classic drinks

Ca phe sua da — iced coffee with condensed milk. The default, and the one to start with. Strong coffee dripped through a small metal filter onto a layer of sweetened condensed milk, stirred, poured over ice.

Ca phe den da — iced black coffee, usually with sugar unless you say otherwise. Bracing.

Ca phe sua nong and ca phe den nong are the hot versions of both, more common in the cooler north.

The phin

The small stacked metal filter that sits on top of the glass. It drips slowly, which is part of the experience — ordering coffee here means sitting for ten minutes while it brews. Plenty of places now pre-brew for speed, but the phin is still the standard in the north and at traditional cafes.

The ones worth seeking out

Ca phe trung — egg coffee, a Hanoi invention. Egg yolk whipped with condensed milk into a thick sweet foam, poured over strong coffee and served hot in a bowl of warm water. It tastes like liquid tiramisu and it is better than it sounds.

Ca phe muoi — salt coffee, from Hue. A lightly salted cream on top of iced coffee. The salt cuts the sweetness and it is extremely drinkable.

Ca phe cot dua — coconut coffee, blended with coconut cream and ice into something close to a dessert. Common in Hanoi.

Bac xiu — a southern drink, much more condensed milk and much less coffee. The gentle option.

How to order it the way you want it

Four words cover most of it. "Da" means iced, "nong" means hot, "sua" means with condensed milk, "den" means black. So ca phe sua da is iced with milk, ca phe den nong is hot and black. If you do not want sugar in a black coffee, say "khong duong" — black coffee usually arrives sweetened by default.

Where to drink it

Three distinct kinds of place. The pavement cafes — low plastic stools, a glass of free iced tea alongside, the cheapest coffee in the country and the one most embedded in daily life. The Vietnamese chains, air-conditioned and ubiquitous, which are reliable and a reasonable place to work. And the independent specialty cafes, which are serving arabica and pour-overs to a more international standard.

All three are worth using for different things. The stool on the pavement is the one that is specifically Vietnamese.

The sitting

Coffee in Vietnam is not a takeaway habit. The phin takes its time, the stools face the street, and the expectation is that you stay. Nobody will hurry you out of a pavement cafe after one drink, and an hour over a single glass is the normal rate.

The iced tea

Almost every cafe gives you a glass of weak iced tea, tra da, alongside the coffee, free. It is there to drink between sips; the coffee is strong and the tea resets your palate.

Buying beans to take home

Vietnamese coffee is cheap and travels well, and the supermarkets and specialty roasters both sell it ground or whole. A few things to know: a lot of mass-market Vietnamese coffee is roasted with butter, oil or other additives, which gives the characteristic flavour but can clog a home machine. Check whether what you are buying is pure coffee if that matters to you.

A phin filter costs almost nothing and is the right way to make it at home. Buy one with the drink.

Da Lat and where the coffee comes from

Most of Vietnam's coffee is grown in the Central Highlands around Buon Ma Thuot and Da Lat, at altitude and inland from the coast. Da Lat in particular has become a destination for farm visits and cupping, and the town's own cafe scene is among the best in the country.

If the coffee is a serious interest rather than a daily habit, that is where to go for it, and it fits into a route between the coast and Ho Chi Minh City.

A note on strength

Robusta carries roughly twice the caffeine of arabica, and Vietnamese coffee is brewed strong. Two iced coffees in an afternoon is more caffeine than most visitors are used to, and the effect is noticeable. Pace it, especially if you are drinking it in the heat.


----- ARTICLE: Bia Hoi: Vietnam's Fresh Beer, Explained  (slug bia-hoi-guide-vietnam)
EXCERPT: Draught beer brewed that morning, sold on the pavement for less than a dollar. What it is, where it is, and why it only exists in the north.

Bia hoi is fresh, unpasteurised draught beer, brewed daily and delivered to the bars that sell it the same day. It has no preservatives and does not keep, which is why it is sold and drunk within hours and why it costs almost nothing. It is one of the most distinctive things to drink in Vietnam.

What it actually is

A light lager, low in alcohol, served from kegs or plastic barrels into glasses on the pavement. Because it is not pasteurised and not bottled, the economics are completely different from packaged beer — no shelf life, no distribution, no packaging — and the price reflects that.

It is mild. Nobody drinks bia hoi for the strength; they drink it because it is cold, cheap and endless, and because of where it is drunk.

Where it is

Bia hoi is a northern institution, concentrated in Hanoi and the surrounding provinces. It exists in the south, but it is not part of daily life there in the way it is in the north — the southern equivalent is bottled Saigon or 333 on the same plastic stools.

In Hanoi, the junction known as Bia Hoi Corner in the Old Quarter is the famous one and is heavily touristed. The better experience is at any of the hundreds of neighbourhood bia hoi places away from the centre, where the clientele is local and the price is lower still.

How it works

You sit on a low plastic stool at a low table, usually on the pavement. Beer arrives in glasses and keeps arriving; you are generally charged at the end for what you drank. Food is ordered alongside and is the other half of the attraction.

The beer is often poured ahead into rows of glasses, which is normal. It is delivered fresh each day and the places that sell a lot get through it.

What to eat with it

Bia hoi places are drinking-food places. The standard range is nem chua ran, fried fermented pork; lac rang, salted peanuts; nom, a shredded green papaya salad with beef jerky; grilled skewers; and fried tofu. It is salty, sharp food designed to keep you drinking.

Ordering a few plates between a group and letting the beer keep coming is the whole format.

How to find a good one

Away from the Old Quarter, look for the signs: a corner with low tables spilling onto the pavement, a steel keg or a plastic barrel with a tap, and a crowd that is mostly local and mostly after work. The word "bia hoi" on a sign is literal — these places are not trying to be discovered.

There is no need to book, there is rarely a menu in English, and pointing at what the next table is eating works. If the place is full at six in the evening, that is the recommendation.

The hours

Bia hoi is an after-work drink. Places fill up from late afternoon, peak in the early evening, and many have sold their day's supply and closed by nine or ten. This is not a late-night activity, and arriving at eleven expecting to find it in full swing is how people conclude it does not exist.

The price

It is the cheapest beer you will encounter, and considerably cheaper than bottled beer in the same establishment. Prices are posted at most places; at the tourist-facing corners in the Old Quarter they are higher and sometimes not posted, so ask.

Practical points

The glasses are often rinsed rather than washed, which is worth knowing. The beer itself is a brewed, alcoholic product delivered daily and is not the risky part of the evening.

Do not drive afterwards. Vietnam enforces zero alcohol while driving, and that is not a graduated limit — any alcohol at all is an offence. Take a Grab.

The etiquette

Glasses are raised together rather than drunk individually — "mot, hai, ba, vo" is the count and the toast, and you will hear it across the room all evening. Topping up other people's glasses before your own is normal courtesy. None of it is demanding, and nobody expects a visitor to know it.

Beyond bia hoi

Vietnam has a serious craft beer scene now, concentrated in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, brewing with local ingredients. It is a different evening and a different price, and it is genuinely good. Bia hoi is not competing with it.

Bottled Vietnamese lagers — Bia Hanoi in the north, Saigon and 333 in the south, Huda in the centre — are the everyday alternative and are sold everywhere.

Taking beer home

Bia hoi cannot travel; it has no shelf life. Bottled Vietnamese beer can, within the customs allowance you are flying into. Vietnam's own inbound allowance is 1.5 litres of spirits at 20% ABV or above, or 2 litres under 20%, or 3 litres of beer, and your own country's rules apply on the way back.
