--- north-to-south-or-south-to-north-vietnam | handoff=turn | swamp=north-south-routing ---
TITLE: North to South or South to North: Which Way to Travel Vietnam
EXCERPT: The direction you travel Vietnam is decided by your flights, your season and your jet lag — not by preference. Here is how each one pulls.

Almost every first Vietnam trip runs the length of the country in one direction, and most people pick that direction by instinct. It is worth a few minutes of thought instead, because three separate forces pull on the answer and they do not always pull the same way.

The short version
North to south — Hanoi first, Ho Chi Minh City last — is the more common choice and the one that suits most American itineraries. South to north works better if the nonstop from the West Coast is what makes your trip affordable, or if you are travelling in a season that favours finishing in the north.
Neither is wrong. What makes the decision real is that it is effectively locked the moment you buy your international tickets, which is usually before you have finished planning anything else.

Why most people go north first
Hanoi is the harder city, and arriving there with fresh energy rather than two weeks of travel fatigue tends to work better. The north also carries the trip's most weather-sensitive items — Ha Long Bay, Sapa — so doing them early leaves room to adjust if conditions are poor. And finishing in Ho Chi Minh City means ending somewhere with the widest choice of onward flights.

What your flights decide for you
There is no nonstop from the United States to Hanoi. The only nonstop routes are Vietnam Airlines from San Francisco and Los Angeles to Ho Chi Minh City, which means a West Coast traveller who wants to avoid a connection is starting in the south whether they intended to or not.
If you are connecting anyway — which anyone flying from the East Coast or the Midwest will be — the direction is genuinely open. Open-jaw tickets into one city and out of the other are widely available and usually cost little more than a return, so there is rarely a price penalty for not backtracking.

Da Nang as a third door
If your trip is centred on Hoi An, Hue and the central beaches rather than the whole country, flying in and out of Da Nang removes the direction question entirely. It has a reasonable set of connections through the Asian hubs and sits in the middle, which turns a country-length march into a regional trip with far less transit.

Backtracking is the thing to avoid
Flying into and out of the same city on a country-length trip means either a long internal flight at the end or a rushed return up the spine. An open-jaw booking removes that entirely, and it is the single most useful thing to get right early.

What the season decides
The regions do not share a calendar, so the direction can be used to put each region in its better window. An autumn trip runs into the central coast's storm season, so travellers going that way often put the centre early and keep slack around it. A trip in the northern winter faces cool, grey, drizzly weather in Hanoi and Sapa, which some people would rather take at the start while they are fresh and others would rather skip entirely.
March and April are forgiving in every region, which is one reason they make the direction question much less consequential. Outside that window the season has an opinion.

What jet lag decides
Arriving from the United States means landing twelve hours out of step in winter, eleven in summer, after a journey that commonly runs twenty-two to twenty-six hours door to door from the East Coast. Whatever city you land in, the first day is largely a write-off.
That argues for starting somewhere you are happy to spend a slow day. Hanoi's Old Quarter absorbs a jet-lagged wander well. Ho Chi Minh City is hotter and harder going in that state. It is a modest consideration, but it is the one most people ignore entirely and then regret on day two.

What the two ends are actually like
They are different cities in temperament, not just geography. Hanoi is older, denser and cooler in winter, with a street plan that rewards walking and a food culture built on a handful of dishes done exactly. Ho Chi Minh City is bigger, hotter, flatter and more commercial, with wider boulevards, better nightlife and the country's most significant war museums.
Ending in Ho Chi Minh City means finishing on the easier city with the most flights out. Ending in Hanoi means finishing on the one most people find more atmospheric. Neither is a strong argument on its own, but if the other three factors cancel out, this is a reasonable tiebreaker.

What this actually settles
Look at what has just happened. A question that sounds like a preference turned out to be answered by three things you decide at different moments: which airport you can fly into, which month you can travel, and how much of the first day you are willing to lose. Those decisions are usually made in the wrong order — flights booked first on price, season chosen around work, direction assumed afterwards — and by the time the direction is obvious, two of the three are already fixed.
Getting them into the right order is the whole trick, and it is what the complete Vietnam decision manual works through: which commitments foreclose which, and what to settle before you buy anything.

TITLE: Vietnam Itinerary: One Week
EXCERPT: Seven days on the ground is enough for two regions done well, or three done badly. Here is the week that works, and what it costs you to try for more.

A week in Vietnam is a real trip, provided you accept the arithmetic early: the country is about a thousand miles long, and seven days on the ground is enough to do two regions properly. Trying for three is the mistake that turns a good week into a transit log.

What a week actually means from the US
Seven days in Vietnam is not seven days away from home. The outbound crossing eats a calendar day and the return takes most of another, so count the travel separately when you work out your leave. Door to door from the East Coast is commonly twenty-two to twenty-six hours each way with one connection.
The arrival day should be treated as a write-off. Plan a slow first afternoon and an early night rather than a sunrise departure the next morning.

The two-region rule
Pick north plus centre, or centre plus south. The north plus south combination skips the middle of the country, which is where Hoi An and Hue are, and tends to be the one people regret. Within a week, each additional region costs most of a day in transfers.

The week that works: north and centre
Day 1 — Land in Hanoi. Transfer to the Old Quarter, stay awake, walk Hoan Kiem slowly, eat bun cha, sleep early.
Day 2 — Hanoi properly. Temple of Literature, the Ho Chi Minh complex in the morning while it is open, and one of the museums in the afternoon. Egg coffee somewhere with a balcony.
Day 3 — Transfer to Ha Long or Lan Ha for an overnight cruise. Kayaking, a cave, dinner on the water.
Day 4 — Cruise morning, disembark, return to Hanoi, and take an evening flight to Da Nang. Transfer to Hoi An, about forty-five minutes.
Day 5 — Hoi An. The Ancient Town early before the crowds, a tailor fitting if you want one, An Bang beach or a cycle through the paddies in the afternoon, lanterns after nine.
Day 6 — My Son Sanctuary at opening, back to Hoi An for a second fitting, Marble Mountains if there is time.
Day 7 — A slow morning, then Da Nang airport and out.

What this version gives up
Ho Chi Minh City, the Mekong Delta and Sapa. That is a real loss and worth being honest about: you are not seeing the south at all. The compensation is that nothing in the week is rushed and only one internal flight is needed.

The alternative: centre and south
If you want Ho Chi Minh City and the war history, invert it. Two days in Ho Chi Minh City with the War Remnants Museum and Reunification Palace, a Mekong day, then fly to Da Nang for three days in Hoi An and Hue. Same shape, same single internal flight, different half of the country.
This version is warmer year-round and less weather-dependent, which makes it the safer choice for a trip in the northern winter.

The internal flight is not optional
Hanoi to Da Nang is about an hour and a quarter in the air against fifteen to seventeen hours by rail. On a seven-day trip the train is a day you do not have. Book the flight early in the day rather than late, because low-cost carriers accumulate delay as the schedule runs on.
Indicatively, as of October 2026, a one-way internal hop on a main route commonly falls somewhere around $30 to $80 booked a few weeks out. Check live fares rather than planning around that.

If you only have a week, what to skip
Sapa is the most commonly attempted and most commonly regretted addition. It is five to six hours each way from Hanoi, which on a seven-day trip means two travel days for one day in the mountains. Ninh Binh, two hours away, delivers a comparable landscape for a fraction of the time.
The Mekong Delta has the same problem from the other end. The packaged day trip out of Ho Chi Minh City is the weakest day most people have in Vietnam, and the version worth doing needs an overnight in Can Tho for the dawn floating market. On a week, leave it.

And what to protect
Keep the slow morning in Hoi An and the slow evening in Hanoi. The complaint people tend to have about a one-week Vietnam trip is rarely that it missed a city — it is that every day started with an alarm.

Why one week is the hardest itinerary to build
Notice what happened across all of that. Every day you add to one region comes out of another, and every region you add costs a transfer day that produces nothing. A week has no slack, which means the sequence has to be right first time: the order of the regions sets which internal flight you need, which sets what time you land, which sets whether day four is a travel day or a half-day of Hoi An.
On a two-week trip those mistakes are absorbed. On a one-week trip they compound, which is why it is the itinerary that most rewards getting the order right before booking anything — the problem the complete Vietnam decision manual is built around.

TITLE: Vietnam Itinerary: Two Weeks
EXCERPT: Two weeks is the length Vietnam is built for. Here is the north-to-south run, what it includes, and the two places most people wrongly cut.

Fourteen days on the ground is the length at which Vietnam stops being a series of compromises. You can run the country north to south, spend real time in three regions, and still have slack for the weather to misbehave. Here is the shape that works and the decisions inside it.

The frame
Fly into Hanoi, out of Ho Chi Minh City, open-jaw. Three internal flights. Two overnight legs at most. Count the travel days separately from the fourteen: the crossing eats a calendar day each way.
The direction matters less at this length than at seven days, because two weeks absorbs a mistake. If your flights point you south-to-north, run the same plan backwards.

Days 1 to 3 — Hanoi
Arrive, write off the afternoon, and give the city two full days. Hoan Kiem and the Old Quarter on foot, the Temple of Literature, the Ho Chi Minh complex in a morning, and one of the Vietnamese Women's Museum or Hoa Lo Prison. Eat bun cha at lunch and pho at breakfast, not the other way round.

Days 4 to 5 — the northern choice
This is the first real fork. Sapa, five to six hours by road, gives you terraced mountains and a valley homestay. Ninh Binh, two hours away, gives you the same karst landscape as Ha Long but on rivers, and costs you half the travel. Doing both inside two weeks is possible and leaves the rest of the trip tight.
Ninh Binh is the better choice for most first visits because the travel is short and the landscape is extraordinary. Sapa is the better choice if mountains and trekking are why you are coming.

Days 6 to 7 — Ha Long or Lan Ha
An overnight cruise from Hanoi, back the following afternoon. Lan Ha Bay, reached via Cat Ba, has the same karst scenery with a fraction of the boats and is worth asking for by name. Then an evening flight south.

Days 8 to 9 — Hue
Fly Hanoi to Hue, or to Da Nang and transfer. The Imperial City takes a morning, two of the royal tombs take an afternoon, and the DMZ — Vinh Moc tunnels, Khe Sanh, the Ben Hai river — takes a full and sobering day. Hue is also where the food changes: bun bo Hue, and the steamed rice-cake plates.

Day 10 — the Hai Van Pass
Hue to Da Nang by train, about two and a half to three hours along the coast and over the pass. It is one of the best rail stretches in Southeast Asia, costs very little, and replaces a transfer you would make anyway. Sit on the right heading south.

Days 11 to 12 — Hoi An
The Ancient Town before eight and after nine, My Son Sanctuary at opening, An Bang beach in between, and a tailor if you want one — allow two fittings, which means arriving with time.

Days 13 to 14 — Ho Chi Minh City
Fly down. The War Remnants Museum first and early; it will shape the rest of the day. Reunification Palace, Cho Lon in the late afternoon, street seafood in District 4 at night. Use the last morning for Cu Chi or the markets, then fly out.

The two things people cut that they should not
The Mekong Delta usually gets dropped, and the version that survives — a packaged day trip from Ho Chi Minh City — is the weakest day most people have in Vietnam. If the Delta matters to you, it needs an overnight in Can Tho for the dawn floating market, which means taking two days from somewhere else.
The DMZ day out of Hue is the other. It is the only place the war is on the ground rather than in a museum, and for an American audience it is frequently the day people remember longest.

Where the slack lives
Keep a spare half-day in the centre if you are travelling between September and November, because that is the central coast's storm season and boat trips and beach days are the first things cancelled. Keep the last night near your departure airport rather than two hours from it.

What makes this plan hold together
Look at the joints rather than the days. The Ninh Binh or Sapa fork decides whether you have time for an overnight cruise. The cruise's return time decides whether the flight south is that evening or the next morning. Hue's position decides whether the Hai Van train is a transfer or a lost half-day. Each choice is small; each one sets up the next.
Two weeks is forgiving enough that a single wrong call is absorbed, and tight enough that three wrong calls in sequence produce a trip spent in transit. Working out which joint depends on which, before anything is booked, is what the complete Vietnam decision manual is for.

--- crossing-the-street-in-vietnam | handoff=none | swamp=none ---
TITLE: Crossing the Street in Vietnam (Hanoi/HCMC Traffic)
EXCERPT: Vietnamese traffic looks impassable and is not. The technique is simple, and the mistakes that cause accidents are the instinctive ones.

The first time you stand at a kerb in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City and look at an unbroken river of motorbikes, the gap you are waiting for is never going to come. That is the first thing to understand: you are not waiting for a gap. You are joining the flow.

The technique
Step off at a slow, steady, predictable pace and keep going. Riders are reading your trajectory and steering around it, and they are extremely good at it — they do this hundreds of times a day. What breaks the system is unpredictability: stopping halfway, darting forward, or jumping backwards forces every rider who had already solved for your path to re-solve it at close range.
Keep watching the traffic as you go. Predictability is the technique, not a guarantee, and the point of watching is to see the one rider who has not seen you.

What does not apply
Cars, buses and trucks do not flow around you the way scooters do. They are heavier, slower to change line, and often cannot see you. Wait those out. The technique is for the two-wheeled river, not for everything on the road.

Learning it in the first hour
Cross alongside a local. Stand near someone who is about to cross, go when they go, and stay on their downstream side. Two or three crossings of that and the rhythm makes sense. It is the fastest way to acquire the nerve, and nobody minds.
Pick your first crossing deliberately: a wide street with a median gives you a halfway refuge, and a quieter side street in the morning is gentler than a six-lane junction at six in the evening.

Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City are not the same problem
Hanoi's Old Quarter has narrow streets, slower speeds and a very high density of two-wheelers, which makes it intimidating but forgiving: everything is moving at a pace you can read. Crossing there is mostly a question of nerve.
Ho Chi Minh City's boulevards are wider and faster, with more cars mixed into the flow and longer distances to cover in the open. The technique is the same but the margin for error is smaller, and a median refuge is worth walking to find. If you are nervous, learn the skill in Hanoi and arrive in the south already fluent.

Where the rules differ from home
Green pedestrian signals exist and are not a guarantee of an empty road — right-turning traffic generally continues, and motorbikes may cross regardless. Treat a green light as improved odds rather than permission.
Pavements are frequently parked solid with motorbikes, so pedestrians walk in the road as a matter of course. Walk with the traffic beside you rather than against it where you can, and expect scooters on the pavement too; this is normal rather than aggressive.

Phones
Do not cross reading your phone, and more importantly do not stand near the kerb reading it. Snatch thefts from moving motorbikes are the most common crime against visitors in Vietnamese cities, and a stationary person holding a phone at the roadside is the specific target. Stop with your back to a wall if you need to look at a map.

Grab, and when walking is the wrong answer
Distances in both cities are longer than a map suggests, and the heat makes a two-mile walk in July considerably less pleasant than the same walk in a temperate climate. Ride-hailing fares are low enough that taking a car or a motorbike taxi for anything over about a mile is a sensible default rather than a luxury, particularly in the middle of the day.
A GrabBike — riding pillion behind a driver — is also the fastest way across a congested city and the cheapest. You are handed a helmet, and the driver is navigating traffic he reads fluently. It feels alarming for about four minutes and then becomes the obvious way to get around.

At night, and in the rain
Both make you harder to see and make riders less willing to slow. Wet roads mean longer stopping distances on bald tyres, and a tropical downpour cuts visibility to very little. Wear something light-coloured at night if you can, and be more conservative about which crossings you take on.

Walking with children or limited mobility
The flow technique assumes you can maintain a steady pace and change nothing. With a small child, hold them on the downstream side and cross with a group rather than alone. With limited mobility or anything that makes your pace unpredictable, use signalled crossings where they exist, cross with locals, and do not be shy about using a taxi or Grab for a distance you would otherwise walk — fares are low enough that it is a reasonable default rather than an indulgence.

The wider point
Traffic is the part of Vietnam that most alarms American visitors on arrival and the part they adapt to fastest. Within three days the river stops looking like chaos and starts looking like a system, because it is one.
What is worth keeping in proportion is which road risks actually matter. Crossing on foot, done sensibly, is a skill you acquire in a day. Riding a motorbike yourself is a different question entirely — a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, and that has consequences for your insurance as well as your legality.

TITLE: Overland Border Crossings: Vietnam to Cambodia and Laos
EXCERPT: Crossing into Cambodia or Laos by land is straightforward if your visa is valid at that gate — and impossible if it is not.

Vietnam shares long land borders with Cambodia, Laos and China, and the crossings into the first two are a normal part of a Southeast Asia trip. The thing that goes wrong is almost never the journey. It is the paperwork, and specifically which gate your visa is valid at.

The rule that decides everything
A Vietnamese e-visa works only at designated border gates. There are 83 of them since December 2025 — 17 airports, 27 land crossings and 39 seaports — and that covers every international airport in Vietnam. Flying in or out is never a problem.
Land is different. Only some land crossings are on the list, so a crossing that exists and is open to foreigners is not necessarily one your e-visa works at. Check the specific gate before you commit to a route.

And a second rule people miss
Separately from gate eligibility, your e-visa names the entry point you selected on the application, and that selection is binding. Re-entering Vietnam after a trip to Cambodia means a new e-visa naming the gate you will actually use. Both rules have to be satisfied, not just one.

Vietnam to Cambodia
Moc Bai, between Ho Chi Minh City and Phnom Penh, is the busiest and easiest. Direct buses run the route in roughly six to seven hours including the border formalities, and the bus company walks passengers through the process. Cambodia issues visas on arrival to most nationalities including Americans, or you can obtain an e-visa in advance.
In the Mekong Delta, Ha Tien and the Chau Doc river route are the scenic alternatives, with the Chau Doc to Phnom Penh boat a genuinely pleasant way to travel. Both are smaller and slower.

Vietnam to Laos
The Lao Bao crossing on the old Route 9 from Dong Ha, and Nam Can from Vinh, are the usual options. These are long journeys on mountain roads, and the bus services across them have a mixed reputation for comfort and timekeeping. Laos issues visas on arrival at the main international crossings.
Be realistic about the time. Hanoi or Hue to Vientiane or Luang Prabang overland is a full day and often more, against a short flight. People do it for the scenery and the cost, not the convenience.

What the crossing itself is like
You exit one country and enter the next at two separate windows with a walk in between. Have your passport, your Vietnamese exit documentation and your onward visa or the fee for a visa on arrival. Keep small denominations of US dollars for the Cambodian and Lao fees.
Informal fees are sometimes requested at the smaller crossings. The official position is that the posted fee is the fee; asking for a receipt usually resolves it, and the organised bus services generally handle the process in a way that avoids the question.

Timing the crossing
Land borders keep their own hours and they are shorter than you expect — most close in the early evening and some take a long lunch. Arriving at a remote crossing an hour after it shuts means a night in a border town with very little in it.
Build the crossing into the middle of a day rather than the end of one, and confirm the current hours locally rather than from an article, because they change and the smaller gates change most. The organised through-buses are scheduled around the hours, which is one of the better arguments for taking one instead of assembling the journey yourself.

Leaving Vietnam needs valid status too
Vietnam requires a valid visa, a residence card or Immigration Department approval to exit as well as to enter. An overstayed visa is therefore a departure problem at a land border just as much as at an airport, and it is harder to resolve from a remote crossing.

The China border
Crossings into China exist, at Huu Nghi near Lang Son and at Lao Cai, and they are used by travellers continuing north. They are a different proposition from Cambodia and Laos: China requires a visa arranged well in advance for most nationalities including Americans, with no visa on arrival at these crossings, and the paperwork needs to be in place before you travel to the border.
If China is part of the plan, treat it as a separate project rather than an extension of a Vietnam trip, and confirm both the Chinese visa and the Vietnamese gate eligibility before fixing any dates.

Whether to do it at all
For Cambodia from Ho Chi Minh City, the overland route is genuinely competitive: six or seven hours door to door against a flight that, with two airport transfers, is not much quicker and costs more. For Laos, the flight usually wins unless the journey itself is the point.
The reason this matters beyond the day itself is that an overland leg changes your Vietnamese visa requirements — you need a multiple-entry visa or a second one, named for the right gate, arranged before you leave home. That is a decision made weeks before the border, which is the sort of ordering problem the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.
