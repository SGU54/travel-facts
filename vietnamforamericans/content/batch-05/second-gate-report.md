Task A — In-context errors only

Findings below distinguish contradictions from unsupported claims. An absent fact is not treated as proof that a claim is false.

1. North to South or South to North: Which Way to Travel Vietnam — US time-zone scope error

“Arriving from the United States means landing twelve hours out of step in winter, eleven in summer, after a journey that commonly runs twenty-two to twenty-six hours door to door from the East Coast.”

The supplied fact establishes twelve/eleven hours for Washington, DC, not the entire United States. The East Coast qualification attaches to the journey duration, leaving the time difference incorrectly generalised.

2. North to South or South to North — manufactured loss of the arrival day

“A question that sounds like a preference turned out to be answered by three things you decide at different moments: which airport you can fly into, which month you can travel, and how much of the first day you are willing to lose.”

The article already says:

“Whatever city you land in, the first day is largely a write-off.”

It does not establish that choosing direction changes how much of that day is lost. Preference for a gentler arrival city becomes an unsupported time-saving dependency in the handoff.

3. North to South or South to North — unsupported seasonal inference

“An autumn trip runs into the central coast's storm season, so travellers going that way often put the centre early and keep slack around it.”

Keeping slack follows from weather uncertainty. Putting the centre early does not follow without dates showing that earlier is a better weather window. The sentence manufactures a directional implication from a seasonal risk.

4. Vietnam Itinerary: One Week — evening-flight plan contradicts flight advice

“Day 4 — Cruise morning, disembark, return to Hanoi, and take an evening flight to Da Nang.”

Later:

“Book the flight early in the day rather than late, because low-cost carriers accumulate delay as the schedule runs on.”

The recommended itinerary requires the opposite of the subsequent instruction. No exception or alternative timing resolves this.

5. Vietnam Itinerary: One Week — recommends and rejects the same Mekong day

“Two days in Ho Chi Minh City with the War Remnants Museum and Reunification Palace, a Mekong day, then fly to Da Nang for three days in Hoi An and Hue.”

Later:

“The packaged day trip out of Ho Chi Minh City is the weakest day most people have in Vietnam, and the version worth doing needs an overnight in Can Tho for the dawn floating market.”

“On a week, leave it.”

The alternative explicitly includes the day the article then tells a one-week visitor to omit. It does not allocate the overnight said to be necessary.

6. Vietnam Itinerary: One Week — transfers become wholly wasted days

“Every day you add to one region comes out of another, and every region you add costs a transfer day that produces nothing.”

Its own Day 4 includes a cruise morning before the transfer. Across the batch, the two-week article explicitly treats the Hue–Da Nang transfer as an attraction:

“It is one of the best rail stretches in Southeast Asia, costs very little, and replaces a transfer you would make anyway.”

The universal claim that a transfer day “produces nothing” contradicts both examples and inflates the handoff’s stakes.

7. Vietnam Itinerary: One Week — unsupported first-time perfection requirement

“A week has no slack, which means the sequence has to be right first time: the order of the regions sets which internal flight you need, which sets what time you land, which sets whether day four is a travel day or a half-day of Hoi An.”

The itinerary includes a slow final morning and an expressly optional Marble Mountains visit. Limited slack is escalated into no slack, then into a requirement for first-time perfection. Moreover, its Day 4 timing follows from the cruise schedule as well as the flight; region order alone does not determine arrival time.

8. Vietnam Itinerary: Two Weeks — wrong internal-flight count

“Three internal flights.”

Only two are described: Hanoi–Hue or Hanoi–Da Nang, followed by Da Nang–Ho Chi Minh City. Hue–Da Nang is by train. No third flight appears.

9. Vietnam Itinerary: Two Weeks — promises freedom from compromises, then requires one

“Fourteen days on the ground is the length at which Vietnam stops being a series of compromises.”

Later:

“If the Delta matters to you, it needs an overnight in Can Tho for the dawn floating market, which means taking two days from somewhere else.”

The itinerary explicitly requires a substantial trade-off to include a destination presented as something people should not cut. The opening certainty is contradicted by the plan.

10. Vietnam Itinerary: Two Weeks — fabricated fork-to-cruise dependency

“The Ninh Binh or Sapa fork decides whether you have time for an overnight cruise.”

Both alternatives occupy Days 4–5, and the cruise occupies Days 6–7 regardless. The article has not shown that choosing Sapa removes the cruise. Its handoff invents a dependency absent from its own schedule.

11. Vietnam Itinerary: Two Weeks — unsupported travel-time comparison

“Ninh Binh, two hours away, gives you the same karst landscape as Ha Long but on rivers, and costs you half the travel.”

The comparison is unclear and unsupported by the supplied numbers. Against Sapa’s stated five to six hours, two hours is approximately one-third to two-fifths, not half. If the comparison is with Ha Long, no corresponding travel time is supplied.

12. Vietnam Itinerary: Two Weeks — exclusive war-site claim contradicted by its own itinerary

“It is the only place the war is on the ground rather than in a museum, and for an American audience it is frequently the day people remember longest.”

The same article offers Cu Chi on the final morning. Calling the DMZ the only such place excludes another war site already named in the itinerary. The audience-memory ranking is also unsupported by the supplied evidence.

13. Crossing the Street in Vietnam (Hanoi/HCMC Traffic) — observation escalated into universal rider competence

“Riders are reading your trajectory and steering around it, and they are extremely good at it — they do this hundreds of times a day.”

“What breaks the system is unpredictability: stopping halfway, darting forward, or jumping backwards forces every rider who had already solved for your path to re-solve it at close range.”

These sentences assume rider awareness, competence and prior avoidance calculations. The article itself subsequently acknowledges:

“Predictability is the technique, not a guarantee, and the point of watching is to see the one rider who has not seen you.”

That qualification undermines the earlier blanket description. Predictable walking does not establish that approaching riders have seen the pedestrian or will avoid them.

14. Crossing the Street in Vietnam — unsupported crime ranking

“Snatch thefts from moving motorbikes are the most common crime against visitors in Vietnamese cities, and a stationary person holding a phone at the roadside is the specific target.”

The supplied source quotation establishes that petty crime is common, especially in tourist locations. It does not establish this specific ranking or exclusive target profile. The fact layer’s broader description of a “dominant mode” does not supply the missing statistical support.

15. Overland Border Crossings: Vietnam to Cambodia and Laos — airport eligibility becomes guaranteed passage

“Flying in or out is never a problem.”

This contradicts the supplied distinction between airport eligibility and an individual visa’s selected port, validity and errors. The article itself immediately introduces binding port selection. Coverage of airports does not guarantee entry or exit.

16. Overland Border Crossings — incorrectly requires a new visa for every return

“Re-entering Vietnam after a trip to Cambodia means a new e-visa naming the gate you will actually use.”

The fact layer allows multiple-entry e-visas. The article’s own closing paragraph also recognises that option. A new visa is not established as necessary for every re-entry.

17. Overland Border Crossings — departure confused with re-entry; application deadline invented

“The reason this matters beyond the day itself is that an overland leg changes your Vietnamese visa requirements — you need a multiple-entry visa or a second one, named for the right gate, arranged before you leave home.”

Two separate errors:

Leaving Vietnam for Cambodia or Laos does not itself establish a need for another Vietnamese entry. That depends on returning.
The supplied facts allow a fresh application from outside Vietnam. They do not require it to be arranged before leaving home.

“That is a decision made weeks before the border, which is the sort of ordering problem the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.”

“Weeks before” is likewise not an established requirement. The guide connection is built partly on invented timing pressure.

Required arithmetic and consistency checks

Seven-day main itinerary: Days 1–7 add up correctly; one internal flight.
Seven-day alternative: Two city days + one Mekong day + three central-region days = six activity days. A separate flight day could make seven. This is not a demonstrated arithmetic failure, but arrival/departure allocation is unresolved.
Fourteen-day itinerary: 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 14 days. The flight-count error remains. The evening flight after the cruise is most reasonably read as the Hanoi–Hue/Da Nang flight subsequently described, not an additional flight.
Published benchmarks: Hanoi–Da Nang air/rail times, Hue–Da Nang rail time, US nonstop claims and the 83-gate breakdown agree with the supplied benchmarks. Hanoi–HCMC air/rail durations are not restated numerically, so there is no numerical disagreement to flag.
Cruise-to-flight connections: No disembarkation, airport-arrival or departure times are supplied. Their feasibility is unproven, not demonstrated impossible.
