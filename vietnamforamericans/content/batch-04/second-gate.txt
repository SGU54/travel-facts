TITLE: Getting to Vietnam: Flights from the US
EXCERPT: There are only two nonstop routes from the United States to Vietnam. Everything else connects, and where you connect shapes the whole first day.

Vietnam is a long way from the United States, and the flight is the part of the trip most people underestimate. The headline numbers matter less than two structural facts: there are very few nonstops, and the arrival date on your ticket is almost never the day you left.

The nonstop options, and who they serve
Vietnam Airlines flies nonstop from San Francisco and Los Angeles to Ho Chi Minh City, at roughly sixteen to seventeen hours westbound. Those two routes are the entire nonstop map between the United States and Vietnam. If you are starting from the West Coast and your trip begins in the south, this is the simplest way to do it.
From anywhere else — New York, Newark, Washington, Chicago, Dallas, Seattle — you will connect at least once. There is no nonstop to Hanoi from the United States at all, which is worth knowing before you plan a north-to-south route.

Where you connect, and why it matters
The realistic hubs are Seoul Incheon, Tokyo Narita or Haneda, Taipei, Hong Kong, Singapore, and the Gulf airports at Doha and Dubai. North Asian hubs are usually the shortest total routing from the East Coast and the West. Gulf routings are often cheaper and almost always longer, sometimes adding six or more hours of total journey.
Door to door, plan on twenty-two to twenty-six hours from the East Coast with one stop. That total elapsed figure is the number to plan around, not the flight time, because it determines what state you arrive in and whether the first day of your itinerary is real.

The calendar day you lose
Vietnam has a single time zone and does not observe daylight saving, so the gap to the United States shifts by an hour twice a year rather than staying fixed — roughly eleven hours ahead of the East Coast in winter and twelve in summer. Combined with the westbound flight time, a departure on Monday evening typically lands on Wednesday morning. You lose a calendar day going out and nominally gain it coming back.
The practical consequence is that a trip described as two weeks needs sixteen or seventeen days off work. Build the arrival day in as a write-off rather than scheduling anything into it.

Which city to fly into
The default assumption is to fly into one end and out of the other, which avoids backtracking the length of the country. Open-jaw tickets into Hanoi and out of Ho Chi Minh City, or the reverse, are widely available and usually cost little or nothing more than a return.
If the nonstop from the West Coast is what makes the trip affordable, that points you to starting in Ho Chi Minh City and travelling north. If you are connecting anyway, the direction is open and should be decided on other grounds — season, Tet, and what you want to see first.

Da Nang as a third option
Da Nang now has a reasonable number of international connections through the Asian hubs and sits in the middle of the country. For a trip focused on the central coast — Hoi An, Hue, the beaches — flying in and out of Da Nang removes two internal legs that would otherwise eat a day each.

When to book, and what moves the price
Fares to Vietnam move with the Asian holiday calendar more than the American one. The expensive windows are around Tet, which moves each year, and the mid-summer peak. The cheapest reliable period is the autumn shoulder outside the holidays.
Booking two to four months ahead is the usual sweet spot for long-haul to Asia, with the important exception of Tet, where the relevant window is much earlier. We are not going to print fare figures: they move constantly, and a number frozen in an article is misleading within weeks. Check live.

Arriving, and the jet lag nobody plans for
Eleven or twelve hours is close to the maximum possible time difference, which means there is no gentle version of this adjustment. Flying west is generally the easier direction, and the standard advice holds: get onto Vietnamese meal times immediately, get daylight on the first afternoon, and do not nap for more than half an hour on arrival day.
Plan the first forty-eight hours accordingly. A walking day in Hanoi's Old Quarter or a slow first morning in Ho Chi Minh City absorbs jet lag far better than a 6am domestic flight or a Ha Long cruise transfer. The temptation is to front-load the itinerary because the trip feels short; the result is usually two wasted days instead of one.

The connection that decides your first day
One thing worth weighing when you choose a routing: a connection that lands you in Vietnam at dawn gives you a usable, if groggy, first day. One that lands near midnight costs you a hotel night and the morning after. Those two itineraries can cost the same and differ by most of a day of trip — which is the sort of trade that only shows up once you line the route, the season and the internal legs against each other, and which the complete Vietnam decision manual works through in order.

TITLE: Vietnam Airport Guide: Hanoi, Saigon, Da Nang
EXCERPT: What to expect at Noi Bai, Tan Son Nhat and Da Nang — immigration, getting into town, and the one form to have ready.

Vietnam's three main international airports are modern, busy and straightforward, and the things that slow people down are mostly knowable in advance. Here is what each one is actually like and what to have in hand before you join the queue.

Before any of them: the paperwork to have ready
Have your printed e-visa with your passport — the official instruction is to carry a paper copy, which is a cheap hedge against a dead phone in an immigration hall. Have the address of your first night's accommodation written down, because the arrival form and the officer may both ask for it.
Complete the online pre-arrival declaration before you fly, within the three days before arrival. It is free and takes about ten minutes. The two governments describe its status differently — the US State Department calls it required for air arrivals, Vietnam's own portal presents it as advice that shortens the queue — so the practical answer is simply to do it.

The gate-matching trap
Your e-visa names a port of entry, and that choice is binding: arriving at an airport other than the one you selected can mean being refused. Check the document against your ticket before you leave home, while it is still fixable.

Noi Bai, Hanoi (HAN)
International arrivals use Terminal 2, domestic flights Terminal 1, and the two are a short shuttle apart — allow for that on a connection. The airport sits about sixteen miles north of the city and the transfer takes forty-five minutes to an hour, longer in the morning and evening peaks.
Getting in: Grab is the simplest option and the pickup area is signposted, though it is a short walk from arrivals. Airport taxis are metered and legitimate but this is the classic place for an inflated fare, so agreeing the app price beforehand avoids the conversation entirely. The 86 express bus runs to the Old Quarter cheaply and takes about an hour.

Tan Son Nhat, Ho Chi Minh City (SGN)
Unusually close to the city — about four miles from District 1 — but the traffic means thirty to fifty minutes regardless. Terminal 2 handles international arrivals, Terminal 1 domestic, and the newer Terminal 3 has taken much of the domestic load.
Immigration here is the busiest in the country and queues at peak arrival times are long. Grab pickup is on the upper level rather than directly outside arrivals, which catches people out; follow the signs up rather than accepting the first offer at the kerb. Long Thanh, the planned replacement airport, is not yet operating, so do not plan around it.

If you are connecting to a domestic flight
You clear immigration and customs on arrival, collect your bags, and check in again at the domestic terminal. Budget three hours between an international arrival and a domestic departure at either Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City, and more during holidays.

Da Nang (DAD)
The easiest of the three. Small, modern, about two miles from the city centre, and roughly forty-five minutes from Hoi An by car. International and domestic terminals sit side by side.
Most Hoi An hotels will arrange a transfer, and at this distance a prearranged car is worth the small premium over sorting it on arrival after a long flight. Grab also operates normally here.

Practicalities on arrival
Airport exchange counters give the worst rates you will see on the trip, so change only enough to reach your accommodation and handle the rest in the city. ATMs are available in all three arrival halls, and the flat-fee structure means one larger withdrawal beats several small ones.
Buy a SIM at the airport if you have not arranged an eSIM; counters are in the arrivals halls and registration needs your passport. Free wifi is available in all three airports, which is enough to call a Grab if you would rather sort connectivity in town.

The smaller international airports
Four others take international flights and are worth knowing if they suit your route. Cam Ranh serves Nha Trang and is about forty minutes from the town. Phu Quoc takes direct flights from several Asian hubs and is the arrival point for the island's thirty-day visa exemption. Cat Bi at Hai Phong is the closest airport to Cat Ba and Ha Long. Phu Bai serves Hue, mostly domestically.
All of them are small, quick to clear and far less stressful than Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City. If your itinerary is regional rather than country-wide, routing through one of these instead of the big two can save a couple of hours and a lot of queueing.

Leaving the country
Arrive three hours before an international departure. Vietnam requires valid status to exit as well as to enter, so an overstayed visa is a departure problem rather than something to apologise for at the gate. Duty-free purchases on the way out are unrestricted in quantity by Vietnam, but your destination's allowances still apply.

TITLE: How to Ride the Reunification Express Train
EXCERPT: Vietnam's north–south railway is slower than the bus and far slower than flying. People ride it for one stretch in particular.

The Reunification Express runs the length of Vietnam, from Hanoi to Ho Chi Minh City, and it is the most romanticised transport in the country. It is also slow. Understanding why people ride it anyway — and which part they ride — is most of what you need.

What the route actually is
A single line of roughly a thousand miles connects Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, taking somewhere in the region of thirty-two to thirty-six hours end to end depending on the service. Trains stop at Ninh Binh, Hue, Da Nang, Nha Trang and a long list of smaller towns along the way.
Almost nobody rides it end to end, and there is no particular reason to. The train is used in segments, and the segments are what make it worthwhile.

The one leg worth planning around
Hue to Da Nang, about two and a half to three hours, runs along the coast and over the Hai Van Pass with the sea on one side and mountains on the other. It costs very little, it is one of the most scenic rail stretches in Southeast Asia, and it replaces a road transfer you would otherwise make anyway. If you take one train in Vietnam, take this one, and sit on the right-hand side heading south.

Classes, and which to book
Four broad options, in ascending order of comfort: hard seat, soft seat, hard sleeper in a six-berth compartment, and soft sleeper in a four-berth compartment. Air conditioning is standard on the better services and worth confirming when booking.
For an overnight leg, the four-berth soft sleeper is the one most visitors want. Berths are sold individually, so a couple booking two berths shares with two strangers unless they buy the compartment out — which is possible and, split between two, often still reasonable. Lower berths cost a little more and are worth it for the headroom and the window.

Private operators
Several companies attach their own refurbished carriages to the regular trains, particularly on the Hanoi to Lao Cai route for Sapa and on the central legs. They are more comfortable and more expensive, and they run on the same timetable as the train they are attached to.

The Sapa branch
The other railway worth knowing is the overnight branch from Hanoi to Lao Cai, the jumping-off point for Sapa. It leaves Hanoi in the evening and arrives around dawn, followed by an hour's road transfer up the hill. For years this was the standard way to reach Sapa.
The expressway has changed that calculation. A day bus or limousine van now covers the same ground in five to six hours, and most travellers take it. The train survives as the choice for people who would rather sleep through the journey than lose a daylight half-day to it, and the private sleeper carriages on this route are the most comfortable rail travel in the country.

Booking
Tickets are sold through Vietnam Railways and at station counters, and a number of agencies resell them with a markup for the convenience of an English-language interface. Book ahead for overnight sleepers in high season and well ahead around Tet, when the whole network fills.
Bring your passport; tickets are issued against it. Arrive at the station twenty to thirty minutes early, as platforms are announced late and the trains do not wait.

What riding it is actually like
Comfortable rather than luxurious, and sociable. Vendors come through with food and the dining options are basic, so bring snacks and water. Bathrooms are serviceable at the start of a journey and less so by the end. The ride is noisier and bumpier than European rail, and sleeping through an overnight leg is easier with earplugs.
The compensation is the window. The coastal stretches in the centre are genuinely beautiful, and the train passes through rice paddies, fishing villages and backyards that no road route shows you.

Food, and what to bring
Trolleys come through with rice boxes, instant noodles and drinks, and there is hot water on board for the noodles. It is edible rather than good. Stations on the longer stops sell better food from platform vendors, though you need to judge the stop length before getting off.
Bring water, snacks, and something to eat that does not need heating. Bring toilet paper. Bring a power bank — sockets exist in the sleeper compartments but are shared and not always working.

Train versus the alternatives
For the long hops the train loses on time to flying by a wide margin — Hanoi to Da Nang is roughly fifteen to seventeen hours by rail against about an hour and a quarter in the air. Against the sleeper bus the train wins on comfort and loses on price. The honest summary is that the train is a choice about how you want to spend the hours, not a way to save them, except on the Hue–Da Nang leg where it is simply the best option available.
Which legs to fly, ride or sleep through is one of the decisions that shapes a Vietnam itinerary most, and the complete Vietnam decision manual works that sequence through in order.

TITLE: Domestic Flights in Vietnam: Routes and Airlines
EXCERPT: Internal flights are cheap and frequent, and they are what makes a two-week Vietnam itinerary possible. Here is how the network works.

Vietnam's domestic air network is dense, cheap by American standards, and the single thing that makes a short trip through a long country workable. One flight can replace fourteen hours on the ground.

The airlines
Vietnam Airlines is the full-service national carrier and a SkyTeam member, so Delta miles earn and burn on it. VietJet Air and Bamboo Airways are the low-cost operators, with Vietravel Airlines a smaller fourth. Between them they cover essentially every pairing a visitor would want.
The trade is the familiar one. Vietnam Airlines includes checked baggage and has the better on-time record; VietJet is usually the cheapest and sells baggage, seats and everything else separately. On a short hop with hand luggage the low-cost option is often genuinely fine. On a connection where a delay would cost you something, the premium is worth it.

What the fares look like
Low, and variable enough that quoting figures here would be misleading within weeks. The useful shape is that a one-way internal hop typically costs a fraction of the equivalent US domestic fare, and that the gap between the cheapest and most expensive carrier on the same route is often smaller than the baggage fee that separates them.

The routes that matter
Hanoi to Ho Chi Minh City is one of the busiest air corridors in the world and runs many times a day, taking a little over two hours against more than thirty by rail. Hanoi to Da Nang and Ho Chi Minh City to Da Nang are the next tier, both around an hour and a quarter, and both replace a very long ground journey.
Beyond the big three, useful services run to Phu Quoc, Nha Trang via Cam Ranh, Hue via Phu Bai, Da Lat via Lien Khuong, and Con Dao. Flights also reach Dien Bien and Con Dao on smaller aircraft, which are the only practical way to reach either.

The route that is not worth flying
Hue to Da Nang. The airports are close enough together that flying makes no sense once you count transfers, and the train between them is both cheaper and one of the best rides in the country.

Booking and baggage
Book directly with the airlines where you can. Fares rise closer to departure in the usual way, and the holiday periods — Tet above all, then the April–May pair and National Day — book out and spike well in advance.
Check the baggage allowance before you book a low-cost fare, because the headline price frequently excludes checked bags and adding one at the airport costs considerably more than adding it online. Weigh your bag: enforcement is real and consistent.

When the train or bus is the better call
Flying is not automatically right just because it is fast. On the shorter corridors the door-to-door maths closes up: a one-hour flight between Hue and Nha Trang becomes four hours once you add two airport transfers, check-in and security, against a train that leaves from the middle of town.
The rule of thumb that holds is that flying wins decisively above roughly five hundred miles, loses below about a hundred and fifty, and is a genuine toss-up in between — decided by what time of day each option runs and whether you would rather sleep through the journey or see it.

Airports and timing
Domestic terminals are separate from international ones at both Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, so a same-day international-to-domestic connection means clearing immigration, collecting bags and moving terminals. Allow three hours, more at holidays.
For a purely domestic flight, ninety minutes at the airport is comfortable. Vietnamese domestic airports are efficient and the security queues are short by US standards.

Delays
Delays are common on the low-cost carriers, particularly late in the day as the schedule accumulates slippage. If a flight has to connect with something else — an international departure, a cruise check-in, a prepaid transfer — book it early in the day and leave a buffer rather than relying on the schedule.

The island and remote routes
Some places are effectively only reachable by air. Con Dao, the former prison islands off the southern coast, has no practical alternative. Phu Quoc is served by ferry from the Mekong Delta, but the flight from Ho Chi Minh City takes an hour against most of a day by road and boat. Dien Bien Phu in the far northwest is a long mountain drive otherwise.
These routes often use smaller aircraft with tighter baggage limits and thinner schedules — sometimes one or two flights a day — so they are the ones where a cancellation genuinely strands you. Build a spare day around any of them rather than connecting straight onto an international departure.

How flights change the shape of a trip
The real effect of cheap internal flights is that they decouple your route from geography. Without them, a two-week trip is a forced march down a single line; with them, you can skip the middle, double back, or treat Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City as two separate bases. Which legs to fly and which to take slowly is one of the first decisions worth making deliberately rather than by default, and the complete Vietnam decision manual lays that sequence out.

TITLE: Sleeper Bus vs Sleeper Train in Vietnam
EXCERPT: Both save you a hotel night and a day of travel. They differ on comfort, safety and how much of your trip you are willing to spend horizontal.

Vietnam's long distances and cheap overnight transport make the sleeper a genuine planning tool: you cover a long leg and a night's accommodation at once. The choice between bus and train is not really about money, because both are inexpensive. It is about what each one costs you in comfort and risk.

What each one is
The sleeper bus is a coach fitted with reclining berths rather than seats, usually in two tiers down three aisles. You lie down, more or less flat, in a space built to a Vietnamese frame. Shoes come off at the door and go in a bag. Routes cover essentially the whole country, including places the railway does not reach.
The sleeper train is a four or six-berth compartment on the Reunification line, air-conditioned on the better services, with a door you can close. It runs only where the single north–south railway runs, plus the Hanoi to Lao Cai branch for Sapa.

The honest comfort comparison
The train wins, and it is not close if you are tall. Sleeper bus berths are short, and anyone around six feet will spend the night with their knees bent. The train also gives you a flat berth you can sit up in, a door, and the ability to walk about. The bus is cheaper and goes more places.

The safety question
This is where the two genuinely diverge, and it is worth being plain about. Road travel is the leading cause of serious injury to visitors in Vietnam, and an overnight coach on mountain roads sits inside that risk. Operator quality varies, and the cheapest tickets on the most competitive routes are the ones most likely to be run by the operators cutting the most corners.
That does not make sleeper buses unreasonable — millions of journeys happen uneventfully, and most travellers who take them are fine. It does mean the choice is not purely about comfort, and that paying more for a better-reviewed operator is buying something real. The train, running on rails with no overtaking, does not carry the same exposure.

Where each makes sense
Take the train for Hanoi to Hue or Da Nang, and for Hanoi to Lao Cai if you are going to Sapa by rail. These are the legs where the railway goes exactly where you want and the overnight timing works.
Take the bus where the train does not go — Da Lat, Mui Ne, the Mekong towns, Phong Nha, Cat Ba — or where the bus is dramatically more direct. Many of these are daytime journeys, which sidesteps the overnight question entirely.

And sometimes, take neither
For the longest hops, a cheap internal flight usually beats both. Hanoi to Ho Chi Minh City by sleeper bus is over thirty hours; the same trip is a little over two hours in the air for a fare that is often not much higher than the bus. Spending a day and a half horizontal to save a modest amount is a trade worth making consciously rather than by habit.

Practical notes for either
Book ahead in high season and well ahead around Tet. Bring a layer: both buses and trains run the air conditioning hard. Bring earplugs and an eye mask. Keep your passport, cards and phone on your body rather than in a bag in the luggage hold or on a shelf. On buses, pick a berth in the middle of the vehicle, away from the wheels and the door.

Choosing a bus operator
If you are taking the bus, the operator is the decision, not the price. Look for recent reviews rather than a brand name, because quality on a given route changes as companies rotate vehicles. Book through your accommodation or a reputable agency rather than the cheapest aggregator, and be wary of a fare that undercuts everything else on the route by a wide margin.
Two specifics worth asking about: whether the service is a cabin bus with individual pods rather than open berths, which is markedly more comfortable and increasingly common on the main routes, and whether the ticket includes a transfer to the central drop-off point. Many long-distance buses terminate on the edge of a city at an inconvenient hour, and a stranded 5am arrival on an unlit ring road is the most common bad experience people report.

What you are really choosing
Look at what this decision quietly settles. An overnight leg removes a hotel night and a travel day, which is the main reason it is tempting on a two-week trip — but it also fixes where you wake up, which fixes the next day's plan, and it trades a comfortable arrival for an early, tired one. Stack two overnight legs into a fortnight and you have bought yourself four extra days of sightseeing and spent them being too tired to use.
How many overnight legs a trip can absorb, and which ones to spend them on, is the sort of thing that only resolves when you look at the whole route at once — which is what the complete Vietnam decision manual is for.
