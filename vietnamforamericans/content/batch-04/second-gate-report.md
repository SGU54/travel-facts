Task A — In-context errors only

These findings concern contradictions, arithmetic, unsupported deductions and withheld answers in the supplied text. They do not establish whether current routes, schedules or terminal arrangements are correct.

1. flights-to-vietnam-from-us — The seasonal time differences are reversed.

“Vietnam has a single time zone and does not observe daylight saving, so the gap to the United States shifts by an hour twice a year rather than staying fixed — roughly eleven hours ahead of the East Coast in winter and twelve in summer.”

Vietnam is UTC+7; Eastern Standard Time is UTC−5 and Eastern Daylight Time is UTC−4. The differences are 12 hours in winter and 11 in summer. The supplied identity.timezone value contains the same error; its “verified” label does not resolve the arithmetic.

2. flights-to-vietnam-from-us — Calendar displacement becomes a manufactured leave requirement.

“The practical consequence is that a trip described as two weeks needs sixteen or seventeen days off work.”

This does not follow from crossing time zones. It depends on whether “two weeks” means total absence or time in Vietnam, the flights, weekends and the traveller’s work schedule. Calendar days and days off work are also different quantities. The sentence converts an unspecified itinerary into a compulsory leave requirement.

3. flights-to-vietnam-from-us — The arrival-day recommendation contradicts the handoff.

“Build the arrival day in as a write-off rather than scheduling anything into it.”

Later:

“One thing worth weighing when you choose a routing: a connection that lands you in Vietnam at dawn gives you a usable, if groggy, first day.”

The first instruction writes off the arrival day categorically; the handoff assigns usable time to that same day without acknowledging an exception.

The next sentence manufactures a further consequence:

“One that lands near midnight costs you a hotel night and the morning after.”

A hotel night does not establish the loss of the following morning. The claimed difference in usable trip time requires assumptions about sleep, access to accommodation and individual recovery that the article does not establish.

4. flights-to-vietnam-from-us — Explicit withholding of the fare answer.

“We are not going to print fare figures: they move constantly, and a number frozen in an article is misleading within weeks.”

“Check live.”

The article introduces a price-and-booking section and then refuses to answer its numerical component. Price variability does not make every dated example or qualified range misleading. This is exactly the withholding pathology identified in the gate: automatic TRUST 0.

5. domestic-flights-vietnam — The same fare-withholding failure.

Under “What the fares look like”:

“Low, and variable enough that quoting figures here would be misleading within weeks.”

The substitute answer is:

“The useful shape is that a one-way internal hop typically costs a fraction of the equivalent US domestic fare, and that the gap between the cheapest and most expensive carrier on the same route is often smaller than the baggage fee that separates them.”

Neither “low” nor an unspecified fraction of an unspecified US fare answers what the fares look like. The article explicitly declines the numerical answer while continuing to make price comparisons: automatic TRUST 0.

6. domestic-flights-vietnam — A helpful transport option becomes a prerequisite for a two-week trip.

“Internal flights are cheap and frequent, and they are what makes a two-week Vietnam itinerary possible.”

“Vietnam's domestic air network is dense, cheap by American standards, and the single thing that makes a short trip through a long country workable.”

Later:

“Without them, a two-week trip is a forced march down a single line; with them, you can skip the middle, double back, or treat Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City as two separate bases.”

These claims silently require the traveller to cover widely separated regions. A regional itinerary does not require that.

The supplied US-flights article explicitly recognises:

“For a trip focused on the central coast — Hoi An, Hue, the beaches — flying in and out of Da Nang removes two internal legs that would otherwise eat a day each.”

That is an internally supplied counterexample to the claim that domestic flying makes a short trip possible. The handoff inflates a genuine routing decision into a false necessity.

7. domestic-flights-vietnam — The train comparison omits the quantity needed to support its conclusion.

“On the shorter corridors the door-to-door maths closes up: a one-hour flight between Hue and Nha Trang becomes four hours once you add two airport transfers, check-in and security, against a train that leaves from the middle of town.”

A central departure point is not a journey duration. The sentence supplies an estimated flight total but no rail total, so it has not demonstrated that the “maths closes up.” This finding does not depend on whether the stated flight currently exists.

8. domestic-flights-vietnam — Thin schedules become certain stranding, despite acknowledged alternatives.

“These routes often use smaller aircraft with tighter baggage limits and thinner schedules — sometimes one or two flights a day — so they are the ones where a cancellation genuinely strands you.”

The same section states:

“Phu Quoc is served by ferry from the Mekong Delta, but the flight from Ho Chi Minh City takes an hour against most of a day by road and boat.”

“Dien Bien Phu in the far northwest is a long mountain drive otherwise.”

An inconvenient alternative can still be an alternative. A thin schedule supports a risk of substantial delay; it does not establish that a cancellation necessarily strands the traveller. The sentence escalates “often” and “sometimes” into a categorical consequence.

9. reunification-express-train-guide — Its general conclusion contradicts its Sapa explanation.

“The train survives as the choice for people who would rather sleep through the journey than lose a daylight half-day to it, and the private sleeper carriages on this route are the most comfortable rail travel in the country.”

Later:

“The honest summary is that the train is a choice about how you want to spend the hours, not a way to save them, except on the Hue–Da Nang leg where it is simply the best option available.”

The article has already identified saving usable daylight as a reason to take the Sapa sleeper. Its conclusion fails to distinguish elapsed journey time from usable daytime.

It also introduces Hue–Da Nang as the exception without supplying a road journey time. “Best option” does not establish “saves time.”

10. sleeper-bus-vs-sleeper-train-vietnam — Two overnight legs become four sightseeing days through double-counting.

“Stack two overnight legs into a fortnight and you have bought yourself four extra days of sightseeing and spent them being too tired to use.”

The preceding sentence says:

“An overnight leg removes a hotel night and a travel day, which is the main reason it is tempting on a two-week trip — but it also fixes where you wake up, which fixes the next day's plan, and it trades a comfortable arrival for an early, tired one.”

A saved hotel night is not an additional sightseeing day. Even accepting the article’s simplified assumption of one saved daytime journey per sleeper, two legs yield two potentially freed travel days, not four.

“Spent them being too tired to use” then turns a possible recovery cost into a guaranteed outcome. Both errors directly inflate the handoff’s stakes.

11. sleeper-bus-vs-sleeper-train-vietnam — Guaranteed day-saving conflicts with the supplied journey duration.

“Both save you a hotel night and a day of travel.”

And:

“Take the train for Hanoi to Hue or Da Nang, and for Hanoi to Lao Cai if you are going to Sapa by rail.”

“These are the legs where the railway goes exactly where you want and the overnight timing works.”

But reunification-express-train-guide states:

“For the long hops the train loses on time to flying by a wide margin — Hanoi to Da Nang is roughly fifteen to seventeen hours by rail against about an hour and a quarter in the air.”

A fifteen-to-seventeen-hour journey extends substantially beyond an ordinary night. Without departure and arrival times, the blanket claim that this removes a travel day is not established. It also cannot be assumed to save daylight compared with flying.

12. sleeper-bus-vs-sleeper-train-vietnam — Price and reviews are converted into an unsupported safety relationship.

“Operator quality varies, and the cheapest tickets on the most competitive routes are the ones most likely to be run by the operators cutting the most corners.”

“It does mean the choice is not purely about comfort, and that paying more for a better-reviewed operator is buying something real.”

Variable operator quality does not establish that the cheapest operators are the most unsafe, or that paying more buys a safety improvement. Reviews may describe comfort or punctuality rather than maintenance and driving practices. The problem is the asserted relationship, not the absence of a fact-span.

There is also an upstream evidence problem. The article says:

“Road travel is the leading cause of serious injury to visitors in Vietnam, and an overnight coach on mountain roads sits inside that risk.”

Although the supplied fact layer repeats that ranking, its support only says:

“The traffic framing is supported by the same page's extensive road-safety and driving-licence warnings.”

Extensive warnings do not establish a statistical ranking of injury causes. The article inherits an evidentiary overreach from the fact layer.

13. sleeper-bus-vs-sleeper-train-vietnam — A possible bad arrival becomes an unsupported frequency claim.

“Many long-distance buses terminate on the edge of a city at an inconvenient hour, and a stranded 5am arrival on an unlit ring road is the most common bad experience people report.”

The first clause does not establish the specific setting or the “most common” ranking. The sentence turns a plausible inconvenience into a purportedly dominant, vividly threatening outcome without supporting evidence.

14. vietnam-airport-guide — A variable exchange-rate comparison becomes a universal certainty.

“Airport exchange counters give the worst rates you will see on the trip, so change only enough to reach your accommodation and handle the rest in the city.”

“Worst rates you will see” asserts a ranking over every exchange option the reader will encounter. That does not follow from the advice to compare airport and city rates. This is an unsupported absolute, not a finding that airport counters necessarily offer good rates.

Cross-article reconciliation: The repeated three-hour international-to-domestic connection allowance agrees. The repeated Hanoi–Da Nang airborne time agrees. The articles consistently favour the Hue–Da Nang train. The genuine conflicts are in what those choices supposedly save or make possible, particularly the long sleeper’s daylight cost and the claimed necessity of domestic flights. A recommendation to choose rail over a bus is not, by itself, a contradiction of recommending flying over both.
