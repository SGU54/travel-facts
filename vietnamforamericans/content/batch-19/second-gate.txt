----- ARTICLE: Vietnam Itinerary: 3 Weeks  (slug vietnam-itinerary-3-weeks)
EXCERPT: Three weeks is where the things everyone else cuts come back in. What to add, and in what order.

Twenty-one days on the ground is the length at which Vietnam stops being a choice between regions and becomes a question of what to add. The fourteen-day shape already covers the country north to south; three weeks buys you the places that get cut from it, plus slack for weather.

The fourteen-day spine

Start from the standard two-week route, because three weeks is that plus additions rather than a different plan. Hanoi; a northern excursion; the centre around Hue and Hoi An; Ho Chi Minh City. Open-jaw into Hanoi and out of Ho Chi Minh City, with two internal flights and the Hue to Da Nang leg by train.

What three weeks changes is that you no longer have to choose between the northern options, and the south becomes a region rather than a two-day stop.

What to add, in order of value

Both northern excursions rather than one. On two weeks, Ninh Binh and Sapa are a fork. On three, take both: Ninh Binh as a two-night trip from Hanoi, Sapa as a three-night one including the travel, plus the bay cruise. That is the single biggest gain and it is why the north grows most.

The Mekong properly. An overnight in Can Tho for the dawn floating market, which is the version that works, rather than the day circuit out of Ho Chi Minh City. Two nights makes it comfortable.

Hue as its own stop. Two nights, the Imperial City and two tombs, instead of a rushed day trip.

And then one of these

Phu Quoc or Con Dao for a beach ending; Da Lat and the Central Highlands for coffee country and a cooler climate; Ha Giang for the far northern loop, which is the most demanding addition and the one that rewards experienced travellers most. Pick one, not two.

A shape that works

Days 1 to 4, Hanoi, with a day trip or a slow start. Days 5 to 6, Ninh Binh. Days 7 to 8, Ha Long or Lan Ha. Days 9 to 11, Sapa, including the travel each way. Day 12, fly to Hue. Days 12 to 13, Hue. Day 14, train to Da Nang, transfer to Hoi An. Days 14 to 17, Hoi An and the centre. Day 18, fly to Ho Chi Minh City. Days 18 to 19, the city. Days 20 to 21, Can Tho and the delta, returning to fly out.

That is three internal flights rather than two, because Sapa and the delta both sit at the ends of the country. It is also tight at the back end; swapping the delta for a beach stay removes a transfer.

The south at three weeks

On two weeks the south is Ho Chi Minh City and little else. On three it becomes a region: two days in the city for the museums and the markets, two nights in the delta at Can Tho, and either a beach ending or the long way out through Chau Doc if you are continuing to Cambodia.

That last option is worth knowing about. The boat up the Mekong from Chau Doc to Phnom Penh turns the end of a Vietnam trip into the start of another one, and at three weeks there is room for it.

The things three weeks does not fix

The weather, mostly. Vietnam's regions have different wet seasons and three weeks moving through all of them means at least one is likely to be in its difficult months. Three weeks gives you slack to reschedule a cruise or a trek, which two weeks does not, but it does not give you a dry country.

It also does not make the long legs shorter. Sapa is five to six hours from Hanoi each way whatever the length of the trip, and that is a real cost against two days in Ninh Binh.

Pace

The commonest mistake at this length is filling the extra week with more places rather than more time in the same ones. Three weeks at a two-week pace with an extra region is more tiring than two weeks, not less.

Build in at least two unplanned days. On a trip this long they will be used — by weather, by something you did not know existed, or by needing to do nothing for a day.

What three weeks is actually worth

The honest summary: the extra week buys you the north twice over, the delta properly, and the ability to lose two days to a typhoon without the trip falling apart. It does not buy you a fourth region or a different country.

If the additions you want are Ha Giang and the Central Highlands rather than Sapa and the delta, that is a different three weeks and a more demanding one, weighted away from the standard route.

Booking order

International tickets first; they set the direction. Then the three internal flights, which are inexpensive and fill at the useful hours. Then the accommodation in the fixed stops, then the cruise, then the Sapa trek. Everything else can be arranged a day or two ahead in the country.

How a trip this length should be sequenced against the seasons in three different regions is a question the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: Mekong Delta Homestays: Which Province, and Why It Matters  (slug best-mekong-delta-homestay-tours)
EXCERPT: A homestay is not a longer tour. It is a different product, and the province you pick decides what you get.

The Mekong Delta homestay is sold as an upgrade on the day trip, and it is not — it is a different thing entirely. The day trip shows you a circuit of stops. A homestay puts you in one place for a night and the value is in what that place is. Which means the province matters more than the operator.

What a homestay actually is here

Typically a family house or a small guesthouse on a canal or an orchard, with a few rooms, mosquito nets, a shared dinner cooked by the household, and a morning on the water. Some are genuinely a family home with guests; others are purpose-built guesthouses using the word. Both can be good; they are not the same experience.

What you are buying is the slow part of the delta — the hours when the coaches have gone, the canals at dusk and before dawn, and a household's normal evening.

The provinces, and what each gives you

Ben Tre is the closest to Ho Chi Minh City and the coconut province. Narrow palm canals, coconut-candy workshops, and the easiest access of the four. It is also the most visited of the near provinces, so the homestays sit alongside the day-trip circuit rather than away from it.

Vinh Long is further in, on the island between the river's two main branches, with orchards and brick kilns and the Cai Be floating market nearby. Quieter than Ben Tre, and the homestays feel more rural.

Can Tho is the delta's city and the base for Cai Rang, the big dawn wholesale market. Homestays here are on the surrounding canals rather than in the city, and the reason to be here is the market.

An Giang, out towards Chau Doc and the Cambodian border, is the furthest and the least visited, with the flooded Tra Su forest, Cham and Khmer communities, and Sam Mountain.

How to choose between them

By what you want the morning to be. If it is the floating market, you need Can Tho and you need to be on the water before dawn. If it is canals, orchards and a quiet night, Vinh Long gives you more of that for less travel. If it is the shortest trip from Ho Chi Minh City, Ben Tre. If you are crossing to Cambodia afterwards, An Giang puts you at the border.

The common mistake is booking a homestay for the idea of it without checking which province it is in, and then discovering that the dawn market you pictured is three hours away.

Questions to ask before booking

Which province and how far from the nearest town. Is it a family home or a guesthouse. How many rooms. Is dinner with the family or served separately. What the morning activity is and what time it starts. Whether transport from Ho Chi Minh City is included or arranged separately.

The answer about the morning start time is the one that matters most, because the delta's best hours are early and an operator who sets off at nine has sold you a different day.

What an evening and a morning look like

Arrive in the afternoon, usually by road from Ho Chi Minh City or from a neighbouring delta town. A boat or a bicycle before dark through the orchards and along the canals. Dinner with the household, which is where most of the value is and which tends to involve more food than anyone expects. An early night, because the morning starts early.

Then the water before or around dawn: the floating market if you are near Can Tho, otherwise the canals when the fishing boats are out and the light is low. Back for breakfast, and generally away by late morning.

Getting there independently

Buses run frequently from Ho Chi Minh City's western bus station to all the delta towns, and private cars are not expensive split between a few people. Most homestays will arrange a pickup from the nearest bus station if you ask.

Booking directly rather than through a packaged tour is usually cheaper and gives you the morning at your hour rather than the operator's.

Practical points

Bring mosquito repellent; the delta has more mosquitoes than anywhere else you will go in Vietnam, and nets are standard for a reason. Bathrooms are usually shared and simple. Cash, because card payment is rare outside the towns. And a torch, because the paths between buildings are not lit.

If you have a dietary requirement, say so when you book rather than at dinner — the meal is cooked for the household and the guests together.

The decision underneath this

Here is the part that is not really about homestays. The delta, Phu Quoc, Con Dao and a slower few days in Ho Chi Minh City are all competing for the same slot: the end of the trip, before the flight out. They are not additive — each of them costs two or three days and the days come from each other.

A delta homestay is the right end if you want the country's agricultural heart and an early morning on the water. A beach is the right end if you want to stop moving. Which one serves the trip you have actually built, given where you fly out of and what the month is doing, is the kind of ordering problem the complete Vietnam decision manual is built around.


----- ARTICLE: Con Dao Islands Guide  (slug con-dao-islands-guide)
EXCERPT: A former prison archipelago that is now the quietest beach destination in Vietnam. Harder to reach, and that is the point.

Con Dao is a group of islands about 230 kilometres off the southern coast, reached by air or a long ferry. It has empty beaches, a national park covering most of the land and the surrounding sea, and a prison complex that is one of the most significant historical sites in the country. Very few foreign visitors get there, which is most of its appeal.

The two reasons to go

The first is that it is quiet in a way no other Vietnamese beach destination is. The main island has one small town, a handful of hotels and resorts, and long stretches of coast with nobody on them. There is no development at Phu Quoc's scale and no sign of it arriving at that pace.

The second is the history, and it is heavy. The French built a prison on Con Son in the nineteenth century and it was used through the colonial period and the war that followed, including the cells known as the tiger cages. The site is preserved and open, and Hang Duong cemetery holds thousands of graves, including that of Vo Thi Sau, a young resistance fighter executed here and now venerated — Vietnamese visitors come overnight to her grave in large numbers.

How the two sit together

Awkwardly, and that is honest. Con Dao is simultaneously a beach holiday and a place of national mourning, and most Vietnamese visitors are there for the second. Visitors who treat it only as an island resort miss what the place is, and the prison is not an optional half hour.

The beaches and the water

Dam Trau, near the airport, is the best known and the one with a beach bar or two. An Hai and Lo Voi are walkable from the town. The quieter ones need a motorbike or a boat.

The sea around the islands is a marine protected area with better coral and visibility than most of the Vietnamese coast, and diving and snorkelling trips run from the town. Sea turtles nest on several of the outer islands between roughly June and September, and the national park runs supervised visits in season.

The national park

Most of Con Son and the smaller islands are protected. There are marked walking trails through the forest, including one to Dam Trau and another up to a viewpoint over the bay, and the park office in town arranges permits and guides for the longer ones and for the outer islands.

The forest holds the Con Dao black squirrel and the long-tailed macaque, and the birdlife is good. Dugong are present in the seagrass beds and are rarely seen.

The prison, in more detail

Several sites make up the complex and the usual circuit takes a morning. Phu Hai, the largest of the camps, with its communal cells. The tiger cages, small open-topped cells built under the French and used afterwards, which became internationally known in 1970. Phu Binh camp, built later. And the museum, which sets out the chronology.

Hang Duong cemetery is a short distance away and is a working place of commemoration rather than a monument. Vietnamese visitors come at night to Vo Thi Sau's grave; if you are there in the evening, stand back and watch rather than walking through it.

Getting there

Flights run from Ho Chi Minh City and from Can Tho to the island's small airport, and the schedule is limited — book early, and build in the possibility of weather cancelling a flight. A high-speed ferry runs from Vung Tau and from Soc Trang; it takes several hours and does not sail in rough conditions.

Neither route is a casual day trip. Con Dao is a three- or four-night destination at minimum, and the transport is the reason.

Getting around

The main island has one road around the inhabited part and very little traffic. Hotels rent bicycles and electric scooters. Renting a motorbike is common among visitors, and the usual position applies: a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam.

When to go

Roughly March to September is the calmer, drier period for the sea on the main side of the island, and the turtle season falls within it. October to February brings rougher water and more cancelled ferries, though the island itself is pleasant.

Vietnamese holiday periods, particularly the lunar new year, fill the island's limited accommodation, and Vo Thi Sau's commemoration draws large numbers.

Eating and staying

The town has a market, a short row of restaurants and a few cafes, and the seafood is excellent and cheap. Accommodation runs from guesthouses in town to one very expensive resort on the coast, with not much in between, and the supply is small enough that booking ahead matters in any busy period.

Evenings are quiet. That is either the attraction or the problem, depending on who you are.

Who it suits

People who want an empty beach and do not mind that there is very little to do in the evening. People with an interest in the war and the colonial period, for whom the prison alone justifies the trip. And anyone who has been to Phu Quoc and found it too developed.

It suits a first-time two-week Vietnam itinerary considerably less well, because the flights and the minimum stay cost four days that the rest of the country wants.


----- ARTICLE: National Parks and Treks in Vietnam  (slug best-national-parks-treks-vietnam)
EXCERPT: Six parks covering caves, karst, jungle and highland terraces, and what each one actually asks of you.

Vietnam's protected areas run from the limestone caves of the centre to the terraced valleys of the far north, and they vary enormously in how much effort they require. Here are the ones worth planning around and what each involves.

Phong Nha-Ke Bang

A UNESCO World Heritage site in Quang Binh province, and the country's great cave landscape. Son Doong, the largest cave passage in the world, is here and is accessible only on an expensive multi-day expedition booked far in advance through the single licensed operator.

The rest of the park is more reachable. Paradise Cave and Phong Nha Cave are developed, walkable and suitable for anyone. Hang En requires an overnight trek and a camp on a beach inside the cave, and is the one most people who want a serious cave experience end up doing. Tu Lan is a separate system of wet caves reached by swimming.

Phong Nha is a stop on the north-south route between Hanoi and Hue, which makes it easier to fit than its remoteness suggests.

Sapa and the northern valleys

Not a national park but the country's main trekking destination: terraced valleys between Hmong and Dao villages, walked over a day or several with a local guide and village homestays. The walking is more demanding than the distances suggest because of the elevation changes, and the trails are a different proposition wet and dry.

September and October are the best months, with the terraces gold before harvest. The wet summer brings mud and leeches.

Ha Giang

The far north, on the Chinese border, and the most spectacular landscape in the country. The Ha Giang loop is usually done over three or four days on a motorbike — and since a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, that means riding pillion with an easy-rider, which is how a large share of visitors do it.

It is a long way from Hanoi and it needs the time. On anything under three weeks it competes directly with Sapa.

Ba Be

A lake park in the far north-east, less visited than Sapa or Ha Giang and reachable in a long day from Hanoi. The lake is the largest natural one in the country, surrounded by limestone and forest, and the usual visit is a boat trip, a cave and a night in a Tay stilt-house homestay.

It suits people who want the northern mountains without the Sapa infrastructure, and it is gentler than either Sapa or Ha Giang in what it asks.

Cuc Phuong

Vietnam's oldest national park, inland from Ninh Binh and easy to add to a visit there. Primary rainforest, a thousand-year-old tree, limestone caves with prehistoric remains, and two serious conservation centres — the Endangered Primate Rescue Center and the Turtle Conservation Center — which are the main reason to go.

Walking here is gentle and the trails are marked. In April and May the park's butterfly emergence is a genuine spectacle.

Cat Ba

The island park in the Ha Long archipelago, with forest covering most of the interior and the critically endangered Cat Ba langur, found nowhere else. The short climb to the Ngu Lam viewpoint takes about an hour; the traverse to Viet Hai village is a half-day walk that can be combined with a boat back.

Bach Ma and Cat Tien

Bach Ma, between Hue and Da Nang, is a cool highland park with waterfalls, a summit road and the remains of a French hill station. It is an easy day from either city and is often overlooked.

Cat Tien, north of Ho Chi Minh City, is lowland rainforest with gibbons, crocodiles in Bau Sau lake, and night safaris. It is the most accessible serious wildlife park from the south and needs an overnight.

Practical points for all of them

Guides are required in some parks and strongly advisable in most; the park offices arrange them. Permits for the longer treks and the caves are arranged in advance, not on arrival.

Leeches are a feature of the wet season in forested parks and are harmless but unpleasant; long socks and repellent help. Carry more water than you expect to need, and start early — the heat is the limiting factor more often than the terrain.

Choosing between them

If you have one slot: Phong Nha if caves and landscape are the draw and you are travelling the country north to south anyway, because it is on the route. Sapa if the terraces and the village walking are the image you have in mind. Cuc Phuong if you are already in Ninh Binh and want a day of forest and primates.

If you have two slots and three weeks, Phong Nha and one of the northern options is the pairing that covers the most different kinds of landscape.

What to book ahead

Son Doong, months in advance. Hang En and the Tu Lan caves, weeks. Sapa homestays in the autumn peak, days. Everything else can generally be arranged locally the day before.


----- ARTICLE: SIM Card or eSIM in Vietnam?  (slug sim-card-vs-esim-vietnam)
EXCERPT: Buy a local SIM at the airport, unless your phone is eSIM-only or you want coverage the moment you land. Here is the full comparison.

Vietnam has cheap, fast and widely available mobile data, and getting connected is one of the easier parts of arriving. The choice is between a physical local SIM, a local eSIM, and an international roaming eSIM, and the right answer depends on your phone and on how much you care about the first hour.

The short answer

If your phone takes a physical SIM and is unlocked, buy a local tourist SIM at the airport on arrival. It is the cheapest option by a wide margin and the data allowances are generous.

If your phone is eSIM-only, or you want to be connected before you reach the arrivals hall, buy a local eSIM online before you fly. It costs more than the counter SIM and less than roaming.

If you are in Vietnam for two or three days and value doing nothing, your home carrier's roaming package is the expensive but frictionless option.

The networks

Three main operators: Viettel, Vinaphone and Mobifone. Viettel has the broadest coverage, including in the mountains and on the islands, which matters if you are going to Ha Giang, Sapa or Con Dao. In the cities all three are fine.

Coverage in Vietnam is good and the data is fast by international standards. Dead spots exist in the far north and on the smaller islands.

Buying a physical SIM

Counters in the arrivals halls at Hanoi, Da Nang and Ho Chi Minh City sell tourist SIMs with a data allowance over a fixed number of days, and they will install and activate it for you. Bring your passport — registration is required and the staff handle it.

Buying in town from an official operator store is cheaper than the airport and takes longer. Buying from a street kiosk or a convenience store is cheapest and the registration is sometimes handled less carefully, which is worth avoiding.

What to check at the counter

How many days the package runs and what happens at the end. Whether the data is genuinely unlimited or throttled after a threshold. Whether it includes calls, which you probably need for Grab drivers and hotels. And that the SIM is registered in your name with your passport, which is a legal requirement.

eSIM

All three Vietnamese operators sell eSIMs, and so do a number of international travel-eSIM companies. The practical difference is that a local eSIM uses a Vietnamese network directly, while many travel eSIMs roam onto one.

Buy before you fly, install on arrival at the first wifi you reach, or install before leaving home and activate on landing. The advantage is being connected the moment you clear immigration, which is worth something if you are getting a Grab immediately.

The advantage over a physical SIM is that your home number stays active for messages and two-factor codes. That is a good reason to choose it, and it is the one that catches people out when they do not.

Dual SIM and keeping your number

If your phone has a physical slot and eSIM support, the neat arrangement is your home number on one and a Vietnamese data plan on the other, with data set to the Vietnamese line and your home line on calls and texts only. Turn off data roaming on the home line explicitly, or your carrier will bill you for the background traffic.

Roaming from home

Most American carriers sell an international day pass or a travel bundle. It is the most expensive option per day by a long way, and the allowances are usually much smaller than a local SIM's.

For a short stop it is defensible. For two weeks it is not.

What you will actually use it for

Grab, constantly, which is the single biggest reason to be connected here. Maps. Translation. And messaging apps — Zalo is the Vietnamese standard and many hotels, drivers and tour operators will want to reach you on it rather than by email.

Wifi

Nearly universal in hotels, cafes and restaurants, and generally fast and free. That is not a substitute for mobile data when you are standing on a street trying to get a car, but it does mean you need less data than you think.

If something goes wrong

Operator stores are in every town and will sort out a SIM that is not working, usually quickly. Keep the receipt and the packaging, which carries the number. If an eSIM fails to activate, the seller's support is the only route and that is a reason to buy from an established one rather than the cheapest listing.

What to do, concretely

Unlocked physical-SIM phone, trip of a week or more: buy at the airport counter, keep your home SIM in the tray if you have dual slots. eSIM-only phone: buy a Vietnamese eSIM online before you fly. Either way, keep your home number reachable for two-factor codes, which is the thing people forget until a bank wants to text them.
