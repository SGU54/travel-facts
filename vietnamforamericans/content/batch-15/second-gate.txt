----- ARTICLE: Phu Quoc Travel Guide  (slug phu-quoc-travel-guide)
EXCERPT: Vietnam's largest island, developing fast, with the country's only visa exemption for Americans. What is good and what has been built over.

Phu Quoc sits in the Gulf of Thailand off Vietnam's south-western coast, closer to Cambodia than to Ho Chi Minh City. It is the country's largest island, and over the last fifteen years it has gone from a quiet fishing and pepper-farming place to a resort destination with an international airport.

The visa exception

This is the one genuinely unusual thing about Phu Quoc for an American traveller. Vietnam's visa requirement has a single exception for tourists, and it is a stay of 30 days or less on the island of Phu Quoc — arriving and leaving directly, without clearing immigration on the mainland.

The condition matters more than the exemption. If your flight routes through Ho Chi Minh City or Hanoi and you pass immigration there, you have entered Vietnam and need a visa like anyone else. The exemption is for arriving at Phu Quoc directly from abroad and leaving the same way.

The beaches

Long Beach, on the west coast running south from Duong Dong town, is the main strip and where most of the accommodation is. The sunsets face straight out to sea, which is the island's signature.

Sao Beach, in the south-east, is the postcard one — white sand, clear water, and crowded in the middle of the day. Ong Lang, north of Duong Dong, is quieter and more low-key. The far north has beaches that are still comparatively undeveloped, though that is changing.

What the island is now

Worth being direct about. The south of the island around An Thoi has been heavily developed, with a large resort and entertainment complex, a theme park, and a cable car running out to Hon Thom island. The north has a safari park and a second large resort development.

Some visitors want exactly that and are very happy. Others arrive expecting an undeveloped tropical island and find a construction site with beaches attached. Both descriptions are accurate about different parts of it, and which one you meet depends entirely on where you stay.

The cable car

The cable car from An Thoi out over the water to Hon Thom is extremely long and the ride over the fishing boats and islets is genuinely striking. It is also the gateway to a theme park, which is the thing at the other end.

Beyond the beaches

The island interior is largely national park, with forest, a few waterfalls that run in the wet season, and roads through it. Pepper farms and fish-sauce factories take visitors — Phu Quoc nuoc mam is a protected designation and the warehouses full of wooden vats are more interesting than they sound.

The An Thoi archipelago off the southern tip is where the snorkelling and diving trips go, on day boats out of An Thoi harbour. The night market in Duong Dong sells seafood by weight and is the main evening in town.

Eating

Seafood, sold by weight at the Duong Dong night market and at the restaurants along Long Beach. Squid, crab, prawns and the local herring salad, goi ca trich, which is raw herring dressed with coconut, onion and herbs and wrapped in rice paper.

The island's fish sauce is the thing to bring home, though check your airline's rules on carrying it — it is a liquid and it is pungent, and more than one bottle has ended a flight badly for its owner's luggage.

When to go

Phu Quoc's seasons run opposite to much of the rest of the country. The dry season is roughly November to April, which is the period the sea is calm and the sunsets are reliable. May to October is the wet season, with heavy afternoon rain and rougher water, and some boat trips do not run.

That makes Phu Quoc a good December-to-March destination at exactly the time the central coast has finished its storm season and the north is cold.

Getting there and around

Phu Quoc International Airport takes domestic flights from Ho Chi Minh City, Hanoi and Da Nang, and a growing number of direct international routes — the latter being what makes the visa exemption usable. Ferries run from Ha Tien and Rach Gia on the mainland, which is slower and only sensible if you are already in the delta.

On the island, Grab operates, taxis are plentiful, and most resorts run shuttles. Renting a motorbike is common among visitors but a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, so the legal position is the same here as everywhere else in the country.

How long to stay

Three or four nights is enough for the beaches, a boat trip and the night market. A week suits a holiday rather than a stop on a tour. One or two nights is not worth the flight.

What it costs relative to the mainland

Phu Quoc is more expensive than the Vietnamese average across accommodation, food and transport, and noticeably so at the resort end. Budget options exist around Duong Dong and the northern end of Long Beach. Taxi fares on the island are the other thing that adds up, because the distances are real.

Who it suits

Phu Quoc is a beach holiday rather than a cultural destination, and it works best treated as one — bolted onto the end of a Vietnam trip, or visited on its own using the visa exemption. If what you want from Vietnam is cities, food and history, this is not the part of it to spend a week on.


----- ARTICLE: Nha Trang Travel Guide  (slug nha-trang-travel-guide)
EXCERPT: A city beach, a Russian-language resort economy, Cham towers and some of the best diving in Vietnam. Not everybody's idea of a beach town.

Nha Trang is a coastal city in south-central Vietnam with a long curved bay, a wall of hotels along the seafront, and a reputation that divides visitors more sharply than almost anywhere else in the country.

What it actually is

A city of several hundred thousand people with a beach running down one side of it, not a beach resort with a town attached. The seafront is a boulevard of high-rise hotels; behind it is a working Vietnamese city with markets, traffic and ordinary streets.

It has also been a major destination for Russian and, more recently, Korean and Chinese package tourism, and the signage, menus and businesses along the front reflect that. Visitors who expected a Vietnamese fishing town are often surprised; visitors who wanted a city with a beach are not.

The beach

Six kilometres of sand along the bay, publicly accessible along its length, with a landscaped promenade behind it. The municipal beach is busy, clean, and used heavily by locals early in the morning and in the evening.

The water is clear through the dry months and the bay is sheltered by islands. In the wet season it can be rough and murky, and that difference is the single biggest factor in whether visitors enjoy the place.

Diving and the islands

Nha Trang is one of Vietnam's main diving centres, with operators running out to the islands in the bay and to Hon Mun marine protected area. Visibility and marine life are better than most of the Vietnamese coast, though divers who have been to Indonesia or the Philippines tend to find it modest.

Boat trips to the bay islands are the standard day out: Hon Mun for snorkelling, Hon Tam and Hon Mot for beaches, and the party-boat versions that are a different proposition entirely. Ask which kind you are booking.

Po Nagar and the Cham towers

On a rise above the Cai river at the northern edge of the city stand the Po Nagar Cham towers, built between roughly the eighth and eleventh centuries and still an active place of worship. They are the most significant historical site in the city and take an hour.

The Long Son Pagoda, with its large white seated Buddha on the hill behind, is the other main sight, and the view from the top covers the city and the bay.

The mud baths

Nha Trang's mineral mud baths are a local institution and a genuinely odd afternoon — communal or private tubs of warm mud, then mineral pools and showers. Several centres operate on the outskirts of the city. It is touristy and it is also fun.

Where to stay

The seafront strip has the hotels with the view and the highest prices. A block or two back the same standard costs considerably less and you are still two minutes from the sand. The northern end towards Po Nagar is quieter; the southern end towards the port has the newer high-rise developments.

Bai Dai, a long undeveloped beach between the city and the airport, has a separate cluster of resorts and is a different proposition — quiet, with nothing around it.

Eating

Seafood, and a lot of it, sold by weight at restaurants along the front and in better value at the places a few streets back. Nem nuong Ninh Hoa — grilled pork skewers rolled at the table with rice paper, herbs and a thick dipping sauce — is the regional dish to look for.

The Dam market and the streets around it are where to eat away from the seafront, at a fraction of the price.

When to go

Nha Trang's dry season runs roughly January to August, which puts it out of step with the central coast immediately north of it. September to December is its wet period, with the heaviest rain and the roughest sea in October and November.

That makes it a useful summer beach when Hoi An and Da Nang are hot and humid, and a poor choice in late autumn.

Getting there

Cam Ranh International Airport is around forty minutes south of the city and takes domestic and regional flights. The north-south railway stops in the city centre, and the overnight train from Ho Chi Minh City is a reasonable way to arrive. Buses run everywhere.

Day trips

Inland from the city, the Ba Ho waterfalls and the Hon Ba nature reserve make half-day trips into the hills. The Thap Ba hot springs sit on the way to the Cham towers. Doc Let, a long white beach about an hour north, is where people go when the city beach is not what they wanted.

Is it worth a stop

If you want a beach day in the middle of a long overland route, and you are passing through anyway, yes. If you are choosing a Vietnamese beach destination deliberately and the surroundings matter, there are quieter options. And if the Cham towers or the diving are the draw, those are reasons to come in their own right.


----- ARTICLE: Phu Quoc or Nha Trang: Which Beach?  (slug phu-quoc-vs-nha-trang-which-beach)
EXCERPT: They are opposite kinds of place with opposite weather. The answer usually comes down to which month you are travelling.

These are the two beach destinations most Vietnam itineraries end up choosing between, and they are not similar places. One is an island resort destination; the other is a city with a beach. They also have opposite wet seasons, which settles the question more often than any preference does.

Start with the month

This is the part people work out last and should work out first.

Phu Quoc's dry season is roughly November to April. Nha Trang's is roughly January to August. So if you are travelling in December, Phu Quoc is the reliable one. In June or July, Nha Trang is. January to April is the overlap when both work, and that is the only period the question is genuinely about preference.

In October and November Nha Trang is at its wettest and roughest. In June to September Phu Quoc is in its wet season. Picking the wrong one for the month is how people end up on a grey beach wondering what the fuss was.

The character difference

Phu Quoc is an island with resorts strung along its coast and a small town in the middle. You stay at a property, you use its beach, and you take taxis to anything else. It is a holiday destination in the conventional sense, with the developed south and the quieter north offering genuinely different experiences.

Nha Trang is a city. The beach is public and runs along the front of it, there is street life behind, and you can walk to dinner. It is cheaper, busier, less polished and more Vietnamese in daily texture, with a large package-tourism layer on top.

Getting there

Phu Quoc is a flight. There is no sensible overland route — the ferries from the delta are slow and only make sense if you are already there. That means a dedicated leg of the itinerary, and an airport transfer at both ends.

Nha Trang is on the north-south railway and the main highway, so it drops into an overland route without a detour. If you are working your way down the country, it is on the way and Phu Quoc is not.

The visa point

Phu Quoc has one feature nowhere else in Vietnam does: the visa requirement has one exception for tourists, and it is a stay of 30 days or less on the island of Phu Quoc, arriving and leaving directly without clearing immigration on the mainland. That is only useful if you fly in and out internationally without touching the mainland, which rules it out for most people on a Vietnam tour — but it makes Phu Quoc viable as a standalone trip in a way Nha Trang is not.

The beaches themselves

Phu Quoc's are better. The sand is whiter, the water is clearer in season, and Sao Beach in particular is the kind of beach people photograph. The trade is that the good ones are spread around the island and you travel to them.

Nha Trang's single long municipal beach is clean and perfectly pleasant, with a city behind it rather than palms. It is a good beach; it is not a beautiful one.

What else there is to do

Nha Trang has more: the Po Nagar Cham towers, the pagoda, the mud baths, the diving, the islands, and a real city to eat in. Phu Quoc has the beaches, boat trips to the An Thoi islands, a national park interior, pepper farms and fish-sauce warehouses, and a large theme park complex.

If you need the beach day to be one of several kinds of day, Nha Trang gives you more variety. If the beach is the point, Phu Quoc is the better beach.

Who each one disappoints

Phu Quoc disappoints people who booked an unspoilt tropical island and landed in the developed south, and people who wanted things to do in the evening beyond their own resort. Nha Trang disappoints people who expected a beach town and found a high-rise city, and anyone who arrives in October.

Both of those are expectation problems rather than problems with the places, and both are avoidable by reading what the place actually is before booking.

Cost

Nha Trang is the cheaper of the two across accommodation, food and transport, in part because it is a city with a full range of options and in part because Phu Quoc's development has been weighted towards resorts. Both have budget and expensive ends; the midpoint sits lower in Nha Trang.

The third option

If neither fits the month or the route, Con Dao, Quy Nhon and Mui Ne each solve a different version of the problem — Con Dao for remoteness, Quy Nhon for an undeveloped coast on the north-south route, Mui Ne for wind and dunes rather than swimming.

So, which

Travelling November to December, or April: Phu Quoc. Travelling May to August: Nha Trang. Travelling January to March, when both work: Phu Quoc if you want a beach holiday, Nha Trang if you want a beach day inside a wider trip. Travelling September to November: consider neither, and look at the question of whether a beach belongs in that particular itinerary at all.


----- ARTICLE: Where to Stay on Phu Quoc: The Resort Areas  (slug best-beach-resorts-in-phu-quoc)
EXCERPT: Five parts of the island with five different holidays. Choose the coast before you choose the hotel.

Phu Quoc's accommodation is spread around an island roughly fifty kilometres long, and the areas are far enough apart that the one you pick decides the shape of the week. This is about the areas rather than individual properties, because the area is the decision that is hard to undo.

Long Beach

The main strip, running south from Duong Dong town down the west coast. Everything from hostels to large international resorts, restaurants and bars along the road behind, and the island's best sunsets straight off the beach.

It is the practical choice for a first visit: you can walk to food and a drink, you are near the town and the night market, and the range of prices is the widest on the island. The northern end near town is busiest; further south it thins out.

Duong Dong town

The island's main town, at the northern end of Long Beach. Cheaper accommodation, the night market, the fishing harbour and actual Vietnamese daily life. It is not a beach location, but it is a short ride from one and it is where the island's character is.

Ong Lang

North of Duong Dong, a stretch of coast with mid-size resorts and bungalow properties spaced out along it. Quieter than Long Beach, with coves rather than a continuous strip, and more trees.

It suits couples and anyone who wants the resort to be the evening. The trade is that there is very little within walking distance, so dinner means the property's restaurant or a taxi.

The far north

The northern tip has a large integrated resort development with its own beaches, a safari park and a casino, and separately some genuinely remote properties on undeveloped coast. These are two very different things in the same part of the island.

Either way you are a long way from Duong Dong — most of an hour — and the week is spent where you are staying. That suits some holidays exactly and strands others.

An Thoi and the south

The southern end of the island, around the An Thoi harbour and the entertainment complex. This is the most heavily developed part: a large themed resort town, the cable car to Hon Thom, a theme park, and the departure point for the island-hopping boats.

It suits families and anyone who wants things to do beyond the beach. It is also the part that least resembles the island people imagine when they book Phu Quoc, which is worth knowing in advance rather than discovering.

Sao Beach and the south-east

The island's most photographed beach is on the south-east coast, and there is comparatively little accommodation on it. Most people visit Sao for the day from elsewhere, which is why it is busy at midday and empty by late afternoon.

What the price buys

At the top end, Phu Quoc's resorts are genuinely international in standard and priced accordingly. In the middle, the island's rapid building means a lot of properties are new, which shows in the rooms and sometimes in the surroundings — a finished hotel next to an unfinished one is a common sight.

At the budget end, Duong Dong and the northern end of Long Beach have guesthouses and hostels at mainland prices. The gap between the ends here is wider than almost anywhere else in Vietnam.

How to choose

First visit, no car, want options: Long Beach. Couple, want quiet, happy to eat at the hotel: Ong Lang. Family with children who need entertaining: the south. Want the beach and nothing else and do not mind isolation: the far north. Travelling cheaply and want the island rather than the resort: Duong Dong.

The common mistake is booking on the property photographs without checking where on the island it is. A beautiful resort an hour from everything is a different holiday from the same resort ten minutes from a town.

Practical points before booking

Check the distance to Duong Dong and to the airport, in minutes by road rather than kilometres. Ask whether the property runs a shuttle and how often. Check whether the beach in front of it is swimmable in the month you are going — the west coast is calm in the dry season and considerably less so from May to October.

Check what is within walking distance, specifically. On much of this island the answer is nothing, and that is fine if you have decided it rather than discovered it.

Checking the construction

Development is continuous on parts of the island, and a beachfront property with building work next door is a real risk. Recent reviews are the only reliable guide to this, because it changes faster than any guide can track — look specifically for reviews from the last few months mentioning noise or site access.

When the island is full

Phu Quoc fills during the lunar new year period and the Vietnamese summer holidays, and prices move accordingly. Outside those, the dry season from November to April is high season for international visitors and the wet months are considerably cheaper.


----- ARTICLE: Mui Ne Guide  (slug mui-ne-guide)
EXCERPT: Sand dunes, kitesurfing and a fishing village four hours from Ho Chi Minh City. Not a swimming beach, and that is the point.

Mui Ne is a coastal strip in Binh Thuan province, about four to five hours east of Ho Chi Minh City. It is known for two things that have nothing to do with each other: sand dunes that look like a desert, and some of the most reliable wind in Southeast Asia.

The wind

This is what Mui Ne is actually for. From roughly November to April the wind blows consistently along this coast, and the bay has been a kitesurfing and windsurfing destination for two decades. Schools, rental shops and instructors line the main road, and the standard of teaching is high because the market is competitive.

The same wind that makes the sport work makes the sea choppy and the beach blustery. If you are coming to lie on sand and swim, you have picked the windy season at the windy beach.

The dunes

Two sets, and they are different. The white dunes, further out, are the large ones — a genuine expanse of pale sand with a lake in the middle, reached by jeep and usually visited at sunrise. The red dunes, closer to town, are smaller, orange, and busier, and are the sunset option.

The standard way to see them is the jeep tour: a pre-dawn pickup, the white dunes for sunrise, the fairy stream, the fishing village, and back for breakfast. It is a well-worn circuit and it works.

The fairy stream

A shallow warm stream running through a small canyon of red and white sandstone formations, walked barefoot in ankle-deep water. It takes half an hour, it is genuinely pretty, and it is on every jeep itinerary.

The fishing village

Mui Ne is still a working fishing port, and the harbour full of round coracle boats and the morning market on the sand are the most photographed thing here after the dunes. Go early; the boats come in at dawn and the activity is over by mid-morning.

The beach, honestly

The main strip has lost a lot of sand to erosion in places, and several resorts have sea walls where there used to be beach. The eastern end towards the harbour and the western end towards Phan Thiet are better. In the windy season the water is choppy and swimming is unappealing along much of it.

In the calm months, roughly May to October, the sea is flat and the beach works — but that is also the wet season, with heavy afternoon rain. Mui Ne's two good things happen in opposite halves of the year.

Where to stay

The strip divides roughly in three. The western end towards Phan Thiet has the larger resorts and the better remaining beach. The middle is where most of the kite schools, guesthouses and restaurants are, and it is the practical base if the wind is why you came. The eastern end towards the harbour is quieter and closer to the fishing village and the dunes.

Because everything is on one road, the question is not which area has more but which end of the road you want to be walking along.

Getting there

Four to five hours from Ho Chi Minh City by bus or private car, and a similar time from Nha Trang in the other direction. Sleeper buses run the route frequently and cheaply. A railway station at Phan Thiet, about half an hour away, takes trains from Ho Chi Minh City.

Mui Ne is strung out along one long road, so getting around means a taxi, a Grab, or walking a long way. Check where on that road your accommodation sits.

How long to stay

Two nights covers the dunes, the fishing village and a day on the beach. If you are learning to kitesurf, a week is the minimum that makes sense. If you are simply breaking the journey between Ho Chi Minh City and Nha Trang, one night is enough.

Eating

Seafood at the harbour end is the cheapest and freshest. The main road has a long run of restaurants aimed at visitors, with Russian, Vietnamese and international menus side by side. The local speciality is banh canh cha ca, a thick noodle soup with fish cake, eaten at breakfast.

Who it suits

Kitesurfers, straightforwardly — this is one of the best places in the region to learn, in season. Photographers, for the dunes and the harbour. And anyone who wants a stop between the south and the central coast that is not another city.

It suits a conventional beach holiday considerably less well, and visitors who booked it as one are the ones who leave disappointed. The dunes and the wind are what you are coming for; the beach is incidental.

The Russian signage

Like Nha Trang, Mui Ne developed heavily around Russian package tourism, and the signage and menus along the main road reflect it. It is part of the character of the place now, and worth knowing if you were expecting something else.
