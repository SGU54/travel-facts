--- hanoi-travel-guide-first-timers ---
TITLE: Hanoi Travel Guide for First-Timers
EXCERPT: Hanoi is a thousand-year-old street plan still working as one. Here is how to read it on a first visit, and what to expect.

Hanoi is the harder of Vietnam's two big cities and the one most visitors end up preferring. It is older, denser, cooler in winter, and organised around a lake and a tangle of trading streets that have been doing the same jobs for centuries. Three days is enough to stop feeling lost.

How the city is laid out
Hoan Kiem Lake is the centre of gravity and the thing to orient yourself by. North of it is the Old Quarter, a dense grid of narrow streets historically organised by trade — one street for tin, one for silk, one for votive paper — and still partly organised that way. South and east is the French Quarter, with wider boulevards, the Opera House and the grander hotels.
West of that sits Ba Dinh, the political district, where the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum complex and the embassies are. Further northwest, around West Lake, is Tay Ho, a leafier expat-and-cafe neighbourhood that is genuinely pleasant and genuinely far from everything else.

The first thing to understand
Distances look walkable on a map and are, but the pavements are frequently parked solid with motorbikes, so pedestrians walk in the road. That is normal rather than dangerous, and the traffic flows around people who move predictably. Give yourself a day to acquire the rhythm.

Three days, loosely
Day one: walk. Hoan Kiem at six in the morning, when the lakeside fills with exercise classes and badminton, is a different city from Hoan Kiem at noon. Then the Old Quarter on foot with no particular destination. Egg coffee somewhere with a balcony.
Day two: the Ho Chi Minh complex in the morning, because it closes early and shuts entirely for annual maintenance. Then the Temple of Literature, Vietnam's first university, founded in 1070 and the calmest half-hour in the city.
Day three: one museum and one escape. The Vietnamese Women's Museum is the best-curated in the city. Hoa Lo Prison, the so-called Hanoi Hilton, presents its American POW period from the Vietnamese side, and going in knowing that is part of why it is worth seeing.

Eating
Hanoi's food is its real argument. Pho here is the plainer northern original — clear broth, no basil plate, no hoisin — and it is a breakfast dish eaten at shops that sell nothing else. Bun cha, grilled pork in a bowl of dressed broth with cold noodles, is the lunchtime dish and the one Hanoi is proudest of.
Beyond those: cha ca, turmeric-and-dill fish cooked at your table; banh cuon, steamed rice rolls; and egg coffee, a Hanoi invention that is far better than it sounds. Eat where there is a queue of locals and high turnover.

Practicalities
Grab is the default for any distance, and a GrabBike through traffic is both the fastest way across the city and the most memorable. The metro exists but serves commuter corridors rather than the sights. Carry cash: street food, markets and small cafes are cash-only.

The weather nobody warns Americans about
Hanoi has four real seasons, including a genuinely cold winter. December to February runs in the 50s and 60s Fahrenheit with a persistent grey drizzle, and visitors who packed for Southeast Asia in the abstract arrive badly dressed. Summer is the opposite — 90s with high humidity and the heaviest rain of the year.
One more consideration: the State Department notes that air pollution is a significant problem in Vietnam’s major cities, especially Hanoi, and the worst stretch overlaps the cool dry months. If you are asthmatic, check a live reading rather than a figure in any article.

What Hanoi is like compared with Ho Chi Minh City
Worth knowing if you are doing both. Hanoi is older, denser and more reserved; Ho Chi Minh City is bigger, hotter, flatter and more openly commercial. Hanoi's food culture is narrower and deeper — a handful of dishes done exactly — while the south is sweeter, more varied and more influenced by the Mekong.
Most visitors expect to prefer the south and end up preferring the north. The usual reason given is that Hanoi is a city you walk and Ho Chi Minh City is a city you ride through.

Safety, in proportion
Hanoi is a safe city by any ordinary measure. Petty theft is the common risk, with phone snatching from passing motorbikes its signature form, so do not walk and scroll. Emergency numbers are 113 for traffic police, 115 for an ambulance, though be aware they are frequently answered only in Vietnamese and an international hospital is often the faster route for anything serious.
The scams that exist are price-ambiguity scams rather than theft: an unsolicited shoe repair, a cyclo quoted per person rather than per trip. Agreeing the number before anything begins defuses nearly all of them.

--- best-things-to-do-in-hanoi ---
TITLE: Best Things to Do in Hanoi
EXCERPT: The short list that earns its place in two or three days, with the honest note on which ones are worth the queue.

Hanoi rewards wandering more than ticking off, but there is a real short list underneath the wandering. These are the things worth building a day around, with a plain note on each about what you are actually getting.

Hoan Kiem Lake and the Old Quarter
Not an attraction so much as the city's organising principle, and the single best use of a first morning. The lake at six is full of exercise classes, badminton and tai chi; by evening it is couples and ice cream. Ngoc Son Temple sits on an island reached by a red bridge and takes twenty minutes.
At weekends the streets around the lake close to traffic and the whole district changes character — street games, music, families. If your dates include a weekend, keep an evening for it.

Temple of Literature
Vietnam's first university, founded in 1070, and the best place in the city to see pre-colonial Vietnamese architecture in a sequence of courtyards. It is calm in a way almost nothing else in central Hanoi is. An hour is enough.

The Ho Chi Minh complex
The mausoleum, the stilt house where he actually lived, the Presidential Palace grounds and the One Pillar Pagoda sit together and are done as one walk. The mausoleum has limited morning hours, closes one or two days a week, and shuts entirely for a period each year for maintenance — check before going.
The dress code is the strictest you will meet in Vietnam: no shorts, no bare shoulders, hats off, and phones and cameras are not permitted inside the viewing chamber. The queue moves quickly and the room itself takes about ninety seconds.

The two museums that matter
The Vietnamese Women's Museum is consistently the best-curated museum in the city and the one that most reframes the country for a visitor — it covers marriage customs, street vendors and the role of women in the war with real depth. Two hours.
Hoa Lo Prison, the Hanoi Hilton, is the other. Its account of the French colonial period is harrowing and its account of the American POW period is partisan. Both halves are worth your time, and knowing which is which going in is the point.

Train Street, with a caveat
The narrow residential stretch where the railway runs within arm's reach of cafe tables is genuinely striking. Access has been restricted and reopened repeatedly on safety grounds, and the current rules change — check locally rather than planning around it.

Water puppets, and whether to bother
The Thang Long Water Puppet Theatre runs a short show of an art form unique to the northern delta, performed by puppeteers standing waist-deep behind a screen. It is touristy and it is also eight hundred years old. An hour, and worth it once.

Getting out onto the water and the hills
Two half-days inside the city limits are easy to miss. West Lake is large enough to cycle around in a leisurely couple of hours, passing Tran Quoc Pagoda — the oldest in Hanoi, on a causeway over the water — and a string of lakeside cafes that make a good late-afternoon stop.
The Long Bien Bridge, designed by Gustave Eiffel's firm and bombed repeatedly during the war, is still in use and can be walked. Underneath it, on the sandbanks in the middle of the Red River, there are banana plantations and market gardens that feel a very long way from the Old Quarter ten minutes behind you.

A cooking class, if you have four days
Most start at a market, which is the useful half — someone explaining what the herbs are and why northern cooking uses fewer of them than southern does changes how you eat for the rest of the trip. The cooking itself is secondary.

Eat as an activity

Eat as an activity
The most memorable thing most people do in Hanoi is not on a list of sights. Bun cha at lunch, pho at breakfast, bia hoi — the fresh draught beer poured on street corners in the late afternoon — and egg coffee in a room above the street. A guided street-food walk on a first evening is one of the few organised activities that reliably pays for itself.

The quieter alternatives
If the main list feels crowded, three things reliably are not. The Temple of the Jade Mountain's quieter neighbour, Quan Thanh Temple near West Lake, is a Taoist temple with a huge bronze statue and almost no visitors. The Fine Arts Museum has an excellent collection of lacquer and silk painting in a colonial building, and is usually near-empty. And the Long Bien Bridge, Eiffel-designed and bombed repeatedly during the war, can be walked across for a view of the river and the banana fields below it.

Day trips
Ninh Binh, two hours away, is the strongest: karst landscape on rivers rather than sea, reached by rowed boat. Ha Long Bay as a day trip is roughly eight hours of transport for three on the water, which is why the overnight cruise is the version that works. The Perfume Pagoda is a boat ride and a climb, mobbed during its January-to-March festival and quiet otherwise.

--- where-to-stay-in-hanoi ---
TITLE: Where to Stay in Hanoi
EXCERPT: Four neighbourhoods, four different trips. The Old Quarter is the obvious answer and not always the right one.

Where you sleep in Hanoi changes the trip more than it does in most cities, because the Old Quarter is both the best-located neighbourhood and the loudest. Here are the four areas worth considering and who each one actually suits.

The Old Quarter
The default, and the right default for a first visit of two or three nights. Everything is on foot: Hoan Kiem is a few minutes away, the street food is at the door, and you are inside the thing you came to see. Accommodation runs from hostel dorms to small boutique hotels, and the value is remarkable by American standards.
The cost is noise. Scooters start before six, the streets are commercial all day, and the bar strip around Ta Hien runs loud until late. Ask for a room at the back and above the second floor, and look at where the hotel sits relative to Ta Hien before booking — two streets of separation is the difference between atmospheric and sleepless.

Who it suits
First-timers, short stays, anyone who wants to walk everywhere, and anyone who would rather be woken at six than take a taxi to dinner.

The French Quarter
South and east of the lake: wider streets, colonial architecture, the Opera House, and the city's grander hotels including the Metropole. Ten to fifteen minutes on foot from Hoan Kiem, which is close enough to walk in and far enough to sleep.
This is the right answer for a second visit, for anyone noise-sensitive, and for travellers who want a more comfortable base without losing walkability. The trade is that the immediate streets are quieter in the evening — you walk to the life rather than opening a window onto it.

Tay Ho and West Lake
Northwest of the centre, around a large lake: leafy, low-rise, full of cafes and expatriates, and the most pleasant place in Hanoi to spend an unhurried day. The air feels better and the pace is slower.
It is also genuinely far. Every outing to the Old Quarter is a fifteen to twenty-five minute Grab ride depending on traffic, which adds up across a short trip. Tay Ho makes sense for stays of a week or more, for families, and for anyone working while travelling. It is the wrong choice for three nights of sightseeing.

Ba Dinh
The embassy and government district, west of the Old Quarter around the Ho Chi Minh complex. Calm, green, wide pavements that are actually walkable, and noticeably fewer tourists. Hotels here are often better value for the standard than their equivalents near the lake.
It is a reasonable compromise if the Old Quarter is full or if quiet matters more than immediacy, and it puts the mausoleum complex on your doorstep for its early opening hours.

Two areas worth ruling out
Anywhere out by the airport. Noi Bai is forty-five minutes to an hour from the centre and there is nothing around it; an early flight is better served by a late Grab than by sleeping out there.
And the far side of the Red River, in Long Bien district. Listings there look central on a map and are cheap, but you are across a bridge from everything and the crossings are congested at exactly the hours you will want to use them.

How many nights
Three is the number most itineraries land on and it works: one to arrive and acclimatise, two to see the city. Two nights is workable if you have been to Vietnam before. Four or more makes Tay Ho a genuine option and gives room for a Ninh Binh day trip without cutting anything.

What to check before booking

What to check before booking
Ask which floor the room is on and whether there is a lift, because many Old Quarter buildings are tall, narrow and stair-only. Ask whether the room has a window — a surprising number of cheap rooms in the Old Quarter do not, and the price often does not signal it.
Check the walking distance to Hoan Kiem on a map rather than trusting a listing that says central, and look at where the nearest bar street is. In winter, check that the room has heating as well as air conditioning; Hanoi's damp cold catches both travellers and hotels out.

The registration point
Under Vietnamese rules every visitor must have their overnight address registered with the local police. Hotels do this automatically from your passport at check-in, which is why they ask for it and sometimes keep it briefly. In an Airbnb or a private homestay the host is responsible, and it is worth confirming when you book that they handle it.

Prices, in rough terms
As an indicative snapshot from October 2026 rather than a quote: a dorm bed runs a few dollars, a clean private room in a small Old Quarter hotel commonly falls somewhere in the twenty-to-forty-dollar range, and a well-reviewed four-star in the French Quarter often sits under a hundred. Rates rise around Tet and the April–May holiday pair. Check live before planning around any of that.

--- hanoi-old-quarter-guide ---
TITLE: Hanoi Old Quarter Guide
EXCERPT: Thirty-six streets, each once dedicated to a trade, still largely doing the same jobs. Here is how to read the district rather than get lost in it.

The Old Quarter is the reason most people remember Hanoi. It is a thousand-year-old commercial district that never stopped being commercial, and understanding its underlying logic turns an overwhelming tangle into something you can navigate.

The thirty-six streets
The district grew as a cluster of craft guilds, each occupying its own street and each named for what it sold. The prefix Hang means merchandise, and what follows is the product: Hang Bac is silver, Hang Gai silk, Hang Ma votive paper and festival decorations, Hang Thiec tin.
Much of that survives. Walk Hang Thiec and you will pass a row of metalworkers hammering; Hang Ma is still a street of red and gold paper goods and becomes spectacular before Tet and Mid-Autumn. Others have turned over to tourism, but the pattern holds often enough to be useful: if you see one shop selling something, the next six will sell it too.

Navigating
Street names change every block, which makes the district feel larger and more confusing than its actual footprint. Use the lake as your southern anchor and St Joseph's Cathedral as a western one. Beyond that, being briefly lost here is the point rather than a problem.

What to actually see
Dong Xuan Market at the northern edge is the district's wholesale heart, three floors of fabric, household goods and dried everything. It is a working market rather than a souvenir one, which is what makes it worth an hour.
Bach Ma Temple, the oldest in the quarter, is a small dark hall on Hang Buom that most people walk past. The Heritage House at 87 Ma May is a restored merchant's home that shows how these narrow tube houses actually worked — deep, thin plots with internal courtyards, built that way because frontage was taxed.
St Joseph's Cathedral, neo-Gothic and weathered almost black, anchors a square that has become one of the better places in the city to sit with a drink in the late afternoon.

The tube houses
Look at the shape of the buildings and the district's history becomes legible. Frontage onto the street was taxed, so merchants built narrow and deep — plots two or three metres wide running twenty or more metres back, with internal light wells and courtyards to keep the far end habitable.
You can still walk through several. The restored house at 87 Ma May is the obvious one, but many shops are tube houses with a family living behind and above the business, which is why a cafe entrance sometimes looks like somebody's corridor. It usually is.

The rhythm of the day
The quarter runs on a schedule worth knowing. Before seven it belongs to pho shops and exercise; mid-morning the shops open and the deliveries clog the lanes; the early afternoon is the quietest it gets; from five the bia hoi corners fill and the night market sets up at weekends; after ten the shutters come down street by street.
Seeing it twice — once at six in the morning and once at nine at night — tells you more about Hanoi than any museum.

Bia hoi corner
The junction of Ta Hien and Luong Ngoc Quyen is the famous one: fresh draught beer brewed that morning, served on plastic stools on the pavement for less than a dollar. It is crowded, loud and genuinely good fun early in the evening, and it is why you do not want a bedroom window directly above it.

Eating your way through
The Old Quarter is the densest concentration of good cheap food in Vietnam. Bun cha at lunch, banh cuon in the morning, cha ca on the street named after it, and egg coffee in an upstairs room reached through what looks like somebody's hallway — several of the best cafes are hidden behind shops.
The selection rule is turnover: a queue of locals and food cooked in front of you. Tidiness is a weaker signal than throughput.

Where the quarter ends
The Old Quarter is smaller than it feels — roughly a kilometre across — and knowing its edges helps. Dong Xuan Market marks the north, the lake the south, Hang Da and the cathedral the west, and the river embankment the east. Step over any of those and the streets widen, the density drops and the prices fall.
That matters practically: walk five minutes east towards the river and you are in an ordinary residential Hanoi with ordinary Hanoi prices, which is often where the better pho is.

Staying here, briefly
Sleeping in the quarter puts everything at the door and the noise in the room. Scooters start before six and the bar corner runs late. Ask for a room at the back, above the second floor, and check how many streets separate you from Ta Hien before you book.

Shopping, and the price question

Shopping, and the price question
Hang Gai sells silk and tailoring, Hang Bac silver, and the streets around Dong Xuan sell everything else. Bargaining is expected in markets and with street vendors, not in shops with marked prices.
The common complaint is not dishonesty but ambiguity — a service performed before a price is agreed, then an aggressive number. Settle the price first, in dong, every time, and the problem disappears.

TITLE: Getting Around Hanoi
EXCERPT: Walk the centre, Grab the rest, and understand why the metro will not help you yet.

Hanoi is more walkable than it first looks and more congested than any map suggests. The short version: walk the central district, use Grab for everything beyond it, and do not plan around the metro.

Walking
The Old Quarter, Hoan Kiem and the French Quarter form a compact area you can cross on foot in twenty minutes, and walking is the only way to see any of it properly. The complication is that pavements are routinely parked solid with motorbikes, so pedestrians walk in the road as a matter of course.
That sounds alarming and is normal. Traffic flows around people who move at a steady, predictable pace. Walk with the traffic beside you where you can, and expect scooters on the pavement too.

Crossing
Step out at a slow constant pace, keep going, keep watching. Riders who have seen you steer around a path they can predict; stopping halfway is what causes problems. Cars and buses are a different matter — wait those out. Crossing alongside a local for the first few goes is the fastest way to learn it.

Grab, and the apps
Grab is the default for any distance beyond walking, and it is the single most useful thing to install before you arrive. It works on a foreign phone number and a foreign card, and it fixes the fare before the ride, which removes meter disputes entirely.
Two products matter: GrabCar, an air-conditioned car, and GrabBike, riding pillion behind a driver who hands you a helmet. GrabBike is markedly faster through congestion and costs a fraction of the car. It feels alarming for about four minutes. Be and Xanh SM are the main competitors, Xanh SM running electric cars and bikes, and having a second app installed is useful at peak times.

Taxis
Metered taxis are legitimate and widely used, and the established companies are fine. The reason to prefer an app is not safety but price certainty: the fare is agreed before you get in, and there is nothing to argue about at the end. If you do take a street taxi, make sure the meter is running.

The metro, honestly
Hanoi has an operating metro — Line 2A from Cat Linh to Ha Dong, and a section of Line 3 — and it is clean, cheap and pleasant. It is also built for commuters rather than visitors: the lines run out to the southwest and west rather than between the places tourists go.
Ride it once if you are curious. Do not plan an itinerary around it. The network that would make it useful to visitors is still under construction.

Buses, cyclos and bicycles
Public buses are extremely cheap and comprehensive, and the 86 express from Noi Bai airport to the Old Quarter is genuinely useful. Beyond that route, the system is hard to use without Vietnamese and the time cost rarely beats a Grab.
Cyclos — the three-wheeled pedal rickshaws — are a tourist ride rather than transport, priced accordingly, and the classic place for a price misunderstanding. Agree the number and the duration before you sit down. Cycling is pleasant around West Lake and not advisable in the Old Quarter.

Renting a motorbike
Short version: do not. A US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, riding without a Vietnamese licence is illegal, and your travel insurance may decline a claim after an accident. GrabBike gives you the same experience with a driver who reads the traffic fluently, for a couple of dollars.

Traffic, and when to avoid it
Hanoi's congestion is predictable enough to plan around. The worst periods are roughly seven to nine in the morning and five to seven in the evening, when a journey that takes fifteen minutes at midday can take forty. The Old Quarter's narrow streets clog with delivery vehicles in the late morning.
If you have a fixed appointment — a train, a tour pickup, an airport run — add half an hour to whatever the app estimates during those windows. A GrabBike slips through congestion that stops cars entirely, which is the argument for it beyond price.

Rain changes everything
A heavy downpour empties the pavements, fills the apps and floods some streets within minutes. Grab wait times climb sharply and surge pricing follows. The local solution is a one-dollar plastic poncho bought from any street corner and simply carrying on; the visitor solution is usually to sit it out in a cafe, which is also pleasant.

Getting to and from the airport

Getting to and from the airport
Noi Bai is about sixteen miles north and takes forty-five minutes to an hour, longer in peak. Grab is the simplest option and the pickup point is signposted, though it is a short walk from arrivals. The 86 express bus runs to the Old Quarter for a fraction of the price and takes about an hour. Allow more time than the distance suggests, particularly for a morning departure.
