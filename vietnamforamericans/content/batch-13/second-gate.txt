----- ARTICLE: Da Nang Travel Guide  (slug da-nang-travel-guide)
EXCERPT: A working coastal city with a long beach, an airport and a bridge that breathes fire. What it is for, and what it is not.

Da Nang is Vietnam's third city and the gateway to the central coast. It has an international airport, a station on the north-south railway, a long city beach and a river running through the middle. Most visitors arrive here for somewhere else — Hoi An to the south, Hue to the north — and the question is how much of the city itself to use.

The shape of the city

The Han River divides Da Nang. The west bank is the older city: the market, the cathedral, the museum, the business district. The east bank is the beach side: My Khe and the hotel strip, the An Thuong grid of cafes and small hotels behind it, and the Son Tra peninsula rising at the northern end.

Several bridges connect the two, the best known being the Dragon Bridge. Distances across the city are short, and Grab is cheap and everywhere.

The beach

My Khe runs for several kilometres along the eastern side of the city — wide, sandy, and lined with hotels from budget to international resort. It is a genuine city beach rather than a resort strip in isolation, with local swimmers early in the morning and in the evening.

Swimming conditions change with the season. From roughly September to November the central coast is in its storm season, the sea gets rough and flags go up; the drier months are when the beach works.

What to see

The Museum of Cham Sculpture holds the largest collection of Cham art in the world, much of it removed from My Son and the other temple sites, and it is the single most rewarding indoor hour in the city — particularly before or after a trip to My Son.

The Son Tra peninsula, north-east of the centre, is a forested headland with viewpoints, a long coast road and the Linh Ung Pagoda with its tall Lady Buddha statue facing the sea. The Marble Mountains, south towards Hoi An, are a cluster of limestone hills with caves and shrines inside them.

The Dragon Bridge

A steel bridge in the form of a dragon, lit at night, which breathes fire and sprays water at set times on weekend evenings. It is unapologetically a spectacle, it is free, and the crowds on the embankments are part of it. Check the current schedule locally, because the timing changes.

Eating

Da Nang is a seafood city. The restaurants along the beach road and on the river sell fish, crab and shellfish chosen from tanks and priced by weight — agree the weight and the price before it is cooked.

The local dish to look for is mi quang, a turmeric-yellow noodle dish from this province with pork, shrimp, herbs and only a small amount of broth. Banh xeo, the crisp folded rice-flour pancake, is a Da Nang staple too, eaten wrapped in rice paper and herbs.

Where to stay

Three broad choices. The My Khe beachfront, where the international resorts and the sea view are. An Thuong, the grid of streets a block or two back, with smaller hotels, hostels and cafes at lower prices. Or the west bank near the river and the market, which is cheaper again and closer to where the city eats.

The bridges add time at busy hours, so which side of the river you are on matters more than the map suggests.

Day trips

Hoi An is around forty-five minutes south by car and is the most common day trip, though most people stay there rather than visit. The Marble Mountains sit on the same road and take an hour or two. Ba Na Hills, with its cable car and the Golden Bridge, is west of the city and takes a full day. The Hai Van Pass heads north towards Hue.

My Son Sanctuary is roughly ninety minutes south-west, and is usually combined with Hoi An rather than done from Da Nang.

How long to stay

Da Nang rewards a stay in proportion to what else you want. Two nights covers the Cham museum, the beach, Son Tra and an evening on the river. A week works if you want a beach base with a city attached and day trips in several directions. One night is an airport stopover, which is a legitimate use of it.

What Da Nang is not is a substitute for Hoi An. The two are different propositions forty-five minutes apart, and treating one as a day trip from the other costs real time each way.

An evening on the river

The riverfront between the Dragon Bridge and the Han River Bridge is where the city walks in the evening. Bridges and buildings are lit, there are cafes along the embankment, and the Han River Bridge swings open late at night to let boats through.

Getting there and around

The airport sits close to the city centre and takes domestic flights from Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City as well as regional international routes. The railway runs north to Hue along the coast and south towards Nha Trang and Ho Chi Minh City. Within the city, Grab covers everything; renting a motorbike yourself is not an option for most visitors, because a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam and driving without a Vietnamese licence can mean a fine, arrest, or jail.

Where this sits in the trip as a whole — whether Da Nang is the base, the stopover or the airport — is part of a wider question that the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: Ba Na Hills and the Golden Bridge  (slug ba-na-hills-golden-bridge-guide)
EXCERPT: A French hill station turned mountain-top theme park, reached by a very long cable car. What is actually up there.

Ba Na Hills sits on a mountain west of Da Nang, about an hour from the city. It was a French hill station in the colonial period, abandoned, and redeveloped from the 2000s into a resort and entertainment complex at the top of a very long cable car. The Golden Bridge, held up by two enormous stone hands, is the image that made it famous.

The cable car

The cable car is the main piece of infrastructure and, for a lot of visitors, the best part of the visit. It climbs more than a kilometre of vertical height over forested mountainside, and the ride takes a substantial part of the journey time. Several lines run at different points; the one you take depends on which station the operator uses.

The cabins are enclosed and the ride is long. Anyone uncomfortable with heights should know that in advance.

The Golden Bridge

A curved pedestrian walkway held up by two giant weathered stone hands, standing out from the mountainside with the valley below. It is short, it is busy, and it was designed to be photographed.

The experience depends almost entirely on two things: cloud and crowds. On a clear morning the bridge appears to float above the valley. In cloud you see the hands and the walkway and very little else. Arriving at opening is the single most useful thing you can do.

What else is up there

A full French village reconstruction — streets, a square, a church facade, hotels and restaurants — built in European style at the summit. A large indoor games and amusement area. Gardens, a wine cellar, a pagoda complex and a large seated Buddha. Shows and parades at set times.

It is a theme park, and it does not pretend otherwise. Visitors who arrive expecting a mountain beauty spot are the ones who find it strange; visitors who arrive expecting a theme park with an extraordinary cable car generally do not.

Tickets and what they cover

Entry is a single ticket that includes the cable car and access to most of the attractions at the top, with some activities and the food charged separately. Buy it online in advance or at the base station. Children are priced by height rather than age, which is worth knowing if you are travelling with a tall child.

Transport from Da Nang is separate: a hired car, a Grab, or a bus included in a packaged tour. A car with a driver waiting is the straightforward version, since the base station is not somewhere you want to be looking for a ride at the end of the day.

Timing the day

Go early. The first cable cars run before the coach groups arrive, the bridge is at its emptiest in the first hour, and the cloud tends to build through the day. Going up in the afternoon on a clear day is the second-best option; going up in the middle of the day in cloud is the worst.

Allow a full day from Da Nang: an hour each way on the road, a long cable car at each end, and three to five hours at the top depending on how much of the park you use.

Weather

The summit is much cooler than the coast, often by ten degrees or more, and it has its own weather. A warm layer is worth carrying even in the hot months, and a rain layer in the wet ones. Check a mountain forecast rather than Da Nang's before committing to the day.

Getting there from Da Nang

The base station is about an hour west of the city. The options are a car with a driver for the day, a Grab each way, or a seat on a packaged tour bus that includes the ticket. A car waiting at the base is the version that does not strand you at the end of the day.

Shuttle buses also run from Da Nang on a fixed schedule; check the last return time before you commit to it, because the site empties quickly once the afternoon shows finish.

Is it worth it

It divides people sharply, and the division is predictable. If you want authenticity, a quiet mountain or a historical site, this is the wrong day out. If you are travelling with children, or you want the cable car and the photograph and are relaxed about the rest, it delivers exactly what it advertises.

The cable car and the view from the bridge on a clear morning are genuinely remarkable. The French village is a film set. Both of those things are true at once, and knowing that before you buy the ticket is most of the battle.

With children

This is the most child-friendly day out in the region by a distance — the cable car, the indoor games area, the shows and the parades are all built for it. If you are travelling with children and the rest of the itinerary is temples and old towns, this is the day that buys you goodwill.

Practical notes

Wear comfortable shoes; there is more walking and more staircase than the photographs suggest. Food at the top is plentiful and priced accordingly. The site is busy at weekends and during Vietnamese holiday periods, when the queues for the cable car are the constraint on the day.


----- ARTICLE: Marble Mountains Guide  (slug marble-mountains-guide)
EXCERPT: Five limestone hills with caves, shrines and a lift, on the road between Da Nang and Hoi An. An hour well spent if you time it.

The Marble Mountains are a cluster of five limestone and marble outcrops rising out of flat coastal ground between Da Nang and Hoi An. Inside them are caves, Buddhist shrines, tunnels and viewpoints, and the whole thing sits directly on the road you will be travelling anyway.

What is there

The five hills are named for the classical elements. Only one, Thuy Son — the water mountain — is developed for visitors, and it is the one all the photographs come from.

Thuy Son holds a network of caves and grottoes with Buddhist and Hindu shrines inside them, pagodas on the surface, stone staircases cut into the rock, and viewpoints looking over the coast and back towards Da Nang. The best-known chamber is Huyen Khong cave, a tall grotto with daylight falling through openings in the roof onto an altar.

The lift

There is a lift up one side of Thuy Son, sold separately from the entry ticket, which saves a long staircase. It only goes up to the first level; there is still climbing inside. Taking the lift up and the steps down is a reasonable compromise.

How long it takes

An hour is enough for the main caves and one viewpoint. Two hours covers the site properly. It fits naturally as a stop on a Da Nang to Hoi An transfer, which is how a lot of visitors do it, and it works equally well as a half-morning out of either place.

There is a second, much less visited cave complex at Am Phu — the "hell cave" — with sculpted depictions of the Buddhist underworld, entered separately near the main entrance.

Timing it

Go early or late. The site is largely stone, it is exposed, and the middle of the day in the hot months is genuinely uncomfortable. The caves themselves are cool, which makes the contrast worse when you come back out.

Coach groups work the site through the middle of the day. Early morning gives you the caves close to empty and the best light coming through the roof openings.

Practical notes

Wear shoes with grip. The stone steps are worn smooth in places and are slippery when wet, and there is more scrambling inside the caves than the entrance suggests. Bring water.

The shrines are in use. Covered shoulders and knees are appropriate, and the usual temple courtesies apply inside the caves as much as in the pagodas above them.

The stone carving village

The settlement at the foot of the mountains, Non Nuoc, is a marble carving village, and the road in is lined with workshops producing everything from small figures to pieces several metres high. The carving is genuine and worth looking at.

The marble used is now largely brought in from elsewhere, since quarrying the mountains themselves was stopped to protect them. If you are buying, be clear about shipping before you commit to anything you cannot carry.

What to see inside Thuy Son

From the top of the staircase or the lift, the route runs past Tam Thai Pagoda, the main temple on the summit plateau, then through a series of openings into the caves below. Linh Ung Pagoda and its tower sit on the eastern side, with the viewpoint over the coast behind it.

Huyen Khong is the chamber to find: a high grotto with shrines around the walls, statues set into the rock, and shafts of daylight coming through holes in the roof. The light is best in the middle of the morning, which is the one argument for not going at opening.

The war

The caves were used by Viet Cong fighters during the war, within sight of an American airbase on the coast below, and there are markers at several points in the complex noting what happened there. It is a quiet layer under what is otherwise a pleasant climb, and it is easy to walk past the plaques without reading them.

Getting there

About twenty minutes from central Da Nang and twenty-five from Hoi An, directly on the main road between them. A Grab in either direction is straightforward, and most drivers doing the transfer will stop here if you ask when you book.

Parking, ticket booths and the lift entrance are all at the base on the main road side. Entry is inexpensive, with the lift and the Am Phu cave ticketed separately.

The second viewpoint

Most visitors climb to one viewpoint and leave. There are two, on opposite sides of the summit, and the second one — reached by a short unmarked scramble up bare rock — looks inland over the other four mountains rather than out to sea. It takes ten minutes and is usually empty.

Combining it

The natural pairing is with the transfer between Da Nang and Hoi An, or with a Son Tra and Linh Ung morning out of Da Nang. It does not combine well with Ba Na Hills, which is a full day in the opposite direction.


----- ARTICLE: Cat Ba Island Guide  (slug cat-ba-island-guide)
EXCERPT: The largest island in the Ha Long archipelago, with a national park, a bay of its own and a way of seeing the karsts that is not a cruise.

Cat Ba is the largest island in the Ha Long Bay archipelago and the only one with a substantial permanent population. It has a town, a national park, beaches, and direct access to Lan Ha Bay — the southern section of the same karst seascape, with far fewer boats on it.

Why it exists as an option

Most visitors see Ha Long Bay from an overnight cruise booked out of Hanoi. Cat Ba is the alternative: you base yourself on the island, sleep on land, and go out on the water by day boat or kayak from there.

What you gain is flexibility, lower cost, a national park, and Lan Ha Bay, which is the same limestone landscape with a fraction of the cruise traffic. What you give up is the thing the cruises sell — waking up anchored among the karsts with nothing else around.

Getting there

From Hanoi, the usual routes are a direct bus-and-ferry combination ticket, or a bus to Hai Phong and a ferry from there. Allow most of a morning either way. A number of operators sell the whole journey as one ticket, which removes the connection risk.

Once on the island, the town is small enough to walk and the rest is reached by taxi, rented bicycle or the local bus towards the national park.

Lan Ha Bay

The reason to come. Day boats run from Cat Ba town and from Beo pier into Lan Ha, usually combining a cruise through the karsts with kayaking, a swim, a floating fishing village and sometimes a cave. A full day on the water costs a fraction of an overnight cruise out of Hanoi.

Kayaking is the part people remember, because the scale of the limestone only registers at water level. Boats will drop kayaks in the quieter inlets.

Choosing a boat

Ask how many people are on the boat, what the route is, whether kayaking is included or extra, and whether the price covers the national park and bay fees. Smaller boats get into places the large ones cannot.

Overnight on the water

Operators out of Cat Ba also run one-night and two-night cruises into Lan Ha, which is the middle option between a day boat and a Hanoi cruise. The boats are generally smaller and the routes less crowded, and the price sits between the two.

Cat Ba National Park

Most of the island's interior is protected forest, with hiking trails of varying difficulty. The short climb to the Ngu Lam viewpoint takes an hour or so and gives the view out over the forested karst interior; the longer traverse to Viet Hai village is a half-day walk and can be combined with a boat back to town.

The park is home to the Cat Ba langur, a critically endangered primate found nowhere else. Sightings are a matter of luck and are not something to plan a day around.

The town and the beaches

Cat Ba town is a working harbour with a strip of hotels along the waterfront, restaurants, and a floating fishing village offshore. It is not a pretty town, and the high-rise hotel development along the front has changed it considerably in recent years.

Cat Co 1, 2 and 3 are small beaches cut into the cliffs a short walk or ride from town, connected by a cliff path. They are pleasant rather than remarkable; the water is the point, not the sand.

Getting around the island

The island is bigger than it looks and the road from the ferry to the town crosses the park. Taxis and the local bus cover the main route. Bicycles are available in town and are fine for the beaches and the coast road, less so for the park road, which climbs.

Renting a motorbike yourself is not a practical option for most visitors, because a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam.

When to go

The northern coast has a cool, often grey and drizzly winter and a hot summer. The clearest water and best boat weather is generally in the shoulder months. Summer is also when the domestic holiday market fills the island, and the town is at its busiest.

Typhoon season affects the north through the late summer and autumn, and boats do not sail when the bay is closed. If the water is the whole reason for the trip, build in a spare day.

Booking

Accommodation on the island ranges from hostels in town to resorts on the quieter side, and outside the summer peak it is usually available at short notice. Day boats are booked through hotels and agencies in town the day before, and that is also where to confirm what the price includes.

How Cat Ba compares with an overnight cruise, and which suits a particular trip, is a decision the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: The Best Time to Trek in Sapa  (slug best-time-to-trek-in-sapa)
EXCERPT: The answer is September and October, with March to May a close second. Here is what each part of the year is actually like.

Sapa sits at around 1,500 metres in the far north of Vietnam, which gives it a climate unlike anywhere else in the country. The trekking is through terraced rice valleys between villages, and the year divides very clearly into periods that suit that and periods that do not.

The short answer

September and October are the best trekking months. The rice terraces are gold and ready for harvest, the heavy rain has eased, the air is clearer, and the temperature is comfortable for walking all day.

March to May is the second window: cooler, greener, with the terraces being prepared and planted, and far fewer visitors than the autumn peak.

September to November

The harvest season and the reason most people come. The terraces turn from green to gold through September, and the cut happens through late September and October. The paths are drying out from the summer rain, and visibility over the valleys is at its best.

The trade is that this is the busiest period and accommodation books up. November turns colder and the terraces are cut and bare, but the walking is still good and the crowds thin.

December to February

Genuinely cold. Sapa can drop close to freezing, mist sits in the valleys for days, and snow falls on the highest ground in some years. The views are frequently gone entirely.

It is not a write-off — the mist has its own quality and the villages are quiet — but it is the wrong time to come if walking in the terraces with a view is the point. Pack for actual winter if you do go.

March to May

The other good window. The weather warms through spring, the valleys green up, and the terraces are flooded and planted — which gives the mirrored-water effect that photographs well in a different way from the gold.

Rain increases through May as the summer pattern sets in. Early in this window is drier and cooler; later is warmer and wetter.

June to August

The wet season. This is the hottest and by far the wettest part of the year, with heavy afternoon rain, leeches on the trails, and paths that turn to deep mud. The terraces are at their most intensely green.

Landslides close roads in this period, and the drive or train up from Hanoi can be disrupted. If this is when you can travel, go with flexible plans and lower expectations about the longer treks.

Month by month, briefly

January and February: cold, misty, often no view. March: cool and clearing, terraces bare or being prepared. April and May: green, warming, flooded terraces, rain building. June to August: hot, very wet, mud and leeches, the deepest green. September: the terraces turn gold, the rain eases. October: harvest, the clearest air, the busiest month. November: cut terraces, cold air, quiet trails. December: winter sets in.

The festivals and markets

The weekly markets in the surrounding valleys are a reason to time a visit to a particular day rather than a particular season. Bac Ha, a few hours away, runs on Sundays and is the largest. Several smaller valley markets run on fixed weekdays.

The lunar new year period in late January or February shuts a good deal down and fills the transport, and it is the one date worth actively planning around.

What the weather means for the walking

The trails between villages are earth, rock and terrace edge, and they are a different proposition wet and dry. In the dry months they are a walk; in the wet months they are a scramble, and a guide earns their fee.

Sturdy shoes matter more here than almost anywhere else in Vietnam. Several guesthouses and agencies lend rubber boots in the wet season, which is a better solution than trainers.

Hiring a guide

Local guides, many of them women from the surrounding Hmong and Dao villages, run treks of a half day to several days with homestays. This is the standard way to do it and the money goes to the valley.

Agree the route, the duration, what is included and the price before setting off, and be realistic about your own fitness — the elevation changes between villages are larger than the distances suggest.

How many days to give it

A single day of walking out of Sapa town covers one valley and gets you back for dinner. Two days with a village homestay is the version most people do and is where the trek stops feeling like a day trip. Three or more gets you into valleys the day walkers do not reach.

Add a night either side for the travel from Hanoi. A two-day trek realistically costs four days of the trip, which is the thing most itineraries underestimate.

Getting there

Sapa is five to six hours from Hanoi by road on the expressway, by sleeper bus or private car. The overnight train runs to Lao Cai, an hour short of Sapa, and is the slower and more comfortable option.

Whichever month you choose, book the accommodation before the transport in the autumn peak; the buses run constantly and the good guesthouses do not.
