TITLE: Vietnam Money and Costs for Americans
EXCERPT: Vietnam is cheap, but the dong has three extra zeros and cards stop working sooner than you expect. Here is how the money actually behaves.

Vietnam is one of the cheapest countries an American can fly to, and the gap between travelling carefully and travelling comfortably is unusually small. What catches people out is not the price level. It is the shape of the money: a currency with three extra zeros, two banknotes that look alike, and a card network that stops working earlier than you expect.

The currency, and the mistake everyone makes
Vietnam uses the dong, and it trades in the tens of thousands to the dollar, so every price carries three more zeros than your instincts expect. A bowl of pho priced at 50,000 looks alarming for about two days and then stops registering. Check the current rate before you go rather than memorising one, because it moves.
The expensive mistake is not arithmetic, it is colour. The 500,000 dong note and the 20,000 dong note are both blue-ish polymer, and they are separated by a factor of twenty-five. Learn those two notes specifically before you land. Handing over a 500,000 believing it is a 20,000 is the single most common money error visitors make, and it usually happens in dim light at the end of a long day.

What things actually cost
Street food runs a couple of dollars a bowl, a bottle of local beer less than that, and a mid-range hotel room a fraction of a US equivalent. Intercity transport is cheap enough that the choice between bus and plane is about time and comfort rather than money. We deliberately do not print a daily budget figure here: prices move, and a number frozen into an article in October is misleading by spring.

Where cards work, and where they stop
Cards work in hotels, chain supermarkets, malls and mid-to-upper restaurants in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang and Hoi An. Beyond that, Vietnam runs on cash: street food, markets, most independent cafes, motorbike-taxi fares paid in person, locally bought train and bus tickets, and very nearly everything outside the big cities.
The part Americans are least prepared for is QR payment. You will watch everyone around you pay by phone, because VietQR is near-universal among Vietnamese — and it is tied to a domestic bank account, so it is effectively unavailable to a visitor. Apple Pay is not the equivalent. Plan to carry more physical cash than you would anywhere in Europe.

Getting cash without paying twice for it
Vietnamese ATMs charge a flat fee per withdrawal rather than a percentage, and they cap how much you can take out at once. Those two facts together decide what cash actually costs you, and the rule that follows is simple: withdraw the maximum your bank and the machine allow, as rarely as you can stand.
The arithmetic is worth seeing once. A fixed fee on a small withdrawal can run several percent of the amount; the same fee on a large withdrawal is a fraction of one percent. Five small withdrawals cost five fees. Your US bank's own foreign-transaction charge stacks on top of whatever the Vietnamese machine takes, so check what your own card charges abroad before you travel — that half is within your control and is often the larger one.

Practical habits that save real money
Decline the machine's offer to bill you in dollars. Dynamic currency conversion looks like a courtesy and is a worse exchange rate every time; always choose to be charged in dong. Beyond that: tell your bank you are travelling, carry a second card in a different pocket, and keep a small reserve of clean US dollars for emergencies rather than for spending.

One habit that is not about money at all: tipping. It is not customary in Vietnam and leaving nothing is normal, which surprises most Americans — our practical guide to tipping in Vietnam covers the few situations where it genuinely is expected.

Changing money, and what not to do
Gold and jewellery shops are the traditional place Vietnamese people change money and often give better rates than banks, which is legal and ordinary. Airport exchange counters are the worst rate you will see all trip, so change only what you need to reach your hotel. Never change money with someone who approaches you on the street.
Count what you are handed before you walk away, every time. This is not about dishonesty so much as the zeros: in a stack of similar-looking notes, a miscount is easy in both directions.

What your money decisions are really deciding
Look at what runs underneath all of this. How much cash you carry sets which parts of the country you can comfortably reach, because the cash-only zone and the rural zone are the same map. How you pay shapes which transport you can book on the spot versus in advance. Where you can withdraw sets how far off the main corridor you can go before you have to plan around a machine.
In other words, your money setup quietly draws the boundary of your itinerary, and most people decide it last — after the flights, after the route, after the bookings that assumed they could pay. Which decisions depend on which, and the order that keeps them from colliding, is worked out in the complete Vietnam decision manual.

TITLE: Tipping in Vietnam: A Practical Guide
EXCERPT: Tipping is not customary in Vietnam and leaving nothing is normal. Here are the few situations where it genuinely is expected.

Tipping is not customary in Vietnam, and walking out of a restaurant having left nothing is a completely normal thing to do. For American travellers that takes some unlearning, because the reflex runs deep and the anxiety about getting it wrong is usually larger than the stakes.

The default: no tip, no offence
Vietnam has no tipping culture in the American sense. Service staff are not paid a sub-minimum wage topped up by gratuities, the price on the menu is the price, and nobody is calculating a percentage as you leave. At a street stall, a local pho shop, a bahn mi cart or a neighbourhood cafe, you pay what you owe and you go.
That is the baseline to start from, because the common American failure mode here is not stinginess. It is over-tipping from nervousness, which is a small problem rather than a large one but adds up across three weeks and quietly distorts prices in heavily touristed places.

Why the advice online disagrees with itself
Tourism has imported the habit into tourist-facing businesses, so you will find confident articles saying tipping is expected and equally confident ones saying it is not. Both describe something real. The honest version is that tipping is absent from ordinary Vietnamese life and present at the tourist interface, and knowing which one you are standing in is the whole skill.

Where it genuinely is expected
A short list covers nearly everything. Private guides and drivers hired for a day or longer, spa and massage therapists, hotel porters who carry bags, and cruise crews on Ha Long Bay overnight boats, where the convention is a pooled envelope collected at the end rather than individual tips. In those situations a tip is anticipated and its absence is noticed.
Amounts are modest and we are not going to print a table of them, because currency values shift and a figure frozen in an article ages badly. The useful rule is proportionality: a tip in Vietnam is a gesture, not a percentage, and it should feel small relative to what the service cost.

Restaurants and the service charge
Mid-range and upper restaurants, especially in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, increasingly add a service charge to the bill, often alongside VAT. When that line is there, the tipping question is already settled and anything further is optional. Read the bottom of the bill before reaching for extra.
In Western-facing restaurants and bars without a service charge, rounding up or leaving small change is common and welcome. Nobody will chase you down the street if you do not.

Hotels
At a budget guesthouse nobody expects anything. At a mid-range or upper hotel, porters who carry bags to your room are tipped a small note, and housekeeping is sometimes left something at the end of a multi-night stay rather than daily. Neither is obligatory, and a Vietnamese guest would often leave nothing.

Taxis, Grab and the rounding habit
Taxi drivers do not expect tips, and rounding up to avoid fiddling with small notes is the normal gesture. Grab settles the fare in the app before the ride, which removes the question entirely — though the app offers a tip option and drivers appreciate it on long or awkward trips.
One thing worth knowing: with a metered taxi, drivers frequently do not carry much change. Having small notes is more useful to everyone than a tip, and it prevents the familiar standoff where a 500,000 note cannot be broken.

Barbers, nail salons and small services
These sit on the boundary and the honest answer is that it depends who is around you. In a neighbourhood shop where the clientele is local, pay the posted price. In a salon on a tourist street with English signage and prices to match, a small tip is common and expected. Judge the room rather than the service type.

How to tip without making it awkward
Hand money directly to the person rather than leaving it on a table, use both hands or your right hand supported at the left elbow, and do it quietly. A tip presented with ceremony can read as a performance of generosity rather than thanks, which lands badly.
Tip in dong rather than dollars. Foreign notes have to be changed, small denominations often cannot be changed at all, and a few dollar bills are worth less to the recipient than the same value in usable local currency. The exception is a pooled cruise envelope, where dollars are commonly accepted.

The one that is not a tip
If someone performs an unrequested service and then names a price — a shoe repair begun while you are standing still, a photograph taken with a conical hat and a shoulder pole — that is a transaction you did not agree to, not a tipping situation. Decline before it starts rather than paying afterwards. Our wider look at how prices get set in these moments is in our honest assessment of safety in Vietnam, and the same habit of fixing the number first is one strand of the cash-and-cards planning that the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.

--- vietnam-visa-extension-guide | handoff=seed | swamp=visa-choice ---
TITLE: Vietnam Visa Extension Guide
EXCERPT: There is no such thing as extending a Vietnamese e-visa from inside the country. That single fact reshapes how you should fill in the application.

Most articles on this subject explain how to extend a Vietnamese visa. This one has to begin by correcting the premise, because for the visa almost every American actually holds, extension is not a procedure with a difficult process. It does not exist.

An e-visa cannot be extended from inside Vietnam
The State Department states it plainly: Vietnam does not allow you to renew or extend an e-visa while you are in the country. There is no office to visit, no fee to pay and no form to file. The e-visa you were granted is the stay you have.
One corollary matters just as much. Vietnam usually grants exactly the period you requested rather than defaulting to the maximum, so asking for three weeks gets you three weeks even though 90 days was available. That request is final.

What that means for anyone selling you an extension
Agencies do advertise e-visa extensions, and the service they are selling is one the government says is not available. In practice what they can offer is a different thing wearing the same name — usually an exit and re-entry, or a new visa of another category arranged through a sponsor. That may be legitimate work, but it is not an extension, and the distinction is worth insisting on before money changes hands.

If you have not applied yet, the duration question is covered alongside the rest of the requirements in our guide to Vietnam visa requirements for Americans.

Who can extend, and where
Extension exists as a real procedure for other visa categories — sponsored business visas, work-linked visas, and holders of temporary residence cards. For those, the Immigration Department handles it in person: you must appear at the office with jurisdiction over where you are actually staying, not whichever office is nearest or most convenient.
The three offices are in Hanoi at 69 Mac Thai To in Yen Hoa Ward, in Da Nang at 78 Le Loi in Hai Chau for central Vietnam, and in Ho Chi Minh City at 196 Nguyen Thi Minh Khai in Xuan Hoa Ward. The same offices handle overstays.

If you want longer than your visa allows
The answer is to decide before you fly. Request the duration you actually need, with a buffer at both ends, and remember the fee is $25 for single entry or $50 for multiple entry — a small premium for a lot of flexibility if your plans are loose.
The other route people use is a border run: leave to Cambodia, Laos or Thailand and come back on a fresh e-visa. This works, but it is not free of friction. You need the new visa approved before you return, your overland crossing must be one of the gates where e-visas are accepted, and the round trip costs a day or two plus the fee. Treat it as a plan rather than an improvisation.

Making a border run work
If you do plan to leave and re-enter, two details decide whether it goes smoothly. The new e-visa has to be approved before you travel back, which means allowing the 3 to 5 working days the Immigration Department usually takes — a border run improvised over a weekend is how people end up stranded on the wrong side. And the crossing you use must be one of the gates where e-visas are accepted, which covers every international airport but only a subset of land crossings into Cambodia and Laos.
The cheapest version is a short flight to Bangkok or Phnom Penh rather than an overland crossing, because the air gates are all eligible and the timing is predictable. Budget two days and the visa fee, and do not book anything in Vietnam that assumes you will be back by a particular hour.

Overstaying is the expensive version
Overstaying is not a quiet administrative matter in Vietnam. The State Department warns that staying beyond your visa, even by accident, means fines and delays getting permission to leave — and that a medical emergency is not treated as an exception. Vietnam requires valid status to exit as well as to enter, so an expired visa blocks your departure rather than merely annoying someone at the gate.
Check your entry stamp against your visa when you land. The stamp occasionally grants less time than the visa does, and that discrepancy is the commonest way a careful traveller overstays without realising. Photograph it.

What Phu Quoc does and does not change
The island's visa exemption covers a stay of 30 days or less, arriving and leaving Phu Quoc directly. It is not an extension mechanism and it does not reset anything: fly from Phu Quoc to the mainland and you have entered Vietnam proper, which needs a visa like any other arrival. Treat it as a separate short trip rather than a way to add time to an existing one.

The decision this really belongs to
Because the duration cannot be fixed later, the visa form stops being paperwork and becomes one of the first real commitments of the trip — made before you have settled the route, often before the flights. How long you ask for, single or multiple entry, and which port you name all constrain what the itinerary can later become, which is one of the sequencing problems the complete Vietnam decision manual works through in order.

TITLE: Hiring a Driver vs Riding Yourself in Vietnam
EXCERPT: Riding yourself is illegal for most visitors and voids insurance. That makes this less of a preference question than it looks.

This looks like a question about preference and budget — do you want the freedom of your own bike, or the comfort of someone else driving? The legal position narrows it more than most articles admit, so it is worth putting that on the table before the comparison.

The constraint that frames the choice
A US licence and an International Driving Permit are each invalid in Vietnam, and driving without a Vietnamese licence can mean a fine, arrest, or jail. Converting a foreign licence requires residency paperwork a tourist does not hold, so for a two-week visitor there is no lawful route to ride their own motorbike.
That does not stop anyone — rental shops ask for a passport photocopy and nothing else — but it does change what the decision is. You are not choosing between two equivalent options. You are choosing between a legal one and one where your travel insurance may not pay your medical bills after an accident.

Why insurance is the deciding number
Road traffic is the leading cause of serious injury to foreign visitors in Vietnam, well ahead of crime. A serious motorbike accident can mean evacuation costs in the six figures. Riding unlicensed means finding out at the worst possible moment whether your policy pays — a risk you are accepting rather than a cost you have budgeted.

What hiring a driver actually gets you
A car with a driver is the standard product in Vietnam, and it costs roughly what bare self-drive rental costs in the United States. For the long legs — Hanoi to Ninh Binh, Hue to Hoi An over the Hai Van Pass, anything in the Mekong Delta — it is comfortable, air-conditioned, and removes both the licence question and the navigation.
The trade is the obvious one. You lose spontaneity, you are on someone else's schedule, and a car cannot take the narrow lanes and paddy tracks that make the countryside worth seeing. Most drivers will wait while you wander, which recovers some of that.

The easy-rider middle ground
Riding pillion behind an experienced local rider is how a large share of the Ha Giang loop is actually done, and it is the option most first-timers underrate. You get the motorbike experience, the mountain roads and the stops a car cannot reach, with none of the licence exposure. You also get a guide, and you get to look at the scenery instead of at the road surface.
For short city hops, Grab's motorbike taxis do the same job for a couple of dollars. Over a two-week trip that covers most of what people imagine they need a rented scooter for.

When self-drive still makes sense
Being straight about it: plenty of travellers rent anyway, and in quiet places — the lanes around Hoi An, a loop of Cat Ba, the flat roads of Ninh Binh — the practical risk is lower than in Ho Chi Minh City traffic. If that is your choice, make it knowingly rather than by default.
Reduce what is in your control. Photograph the whole bike with the renter watching before you ride off, or expect existing scratches on your bill. Never hand over your actual passport as a deposit; offer a photocopy and cash. Wear a proper helmet rather than the token shell supplied. And note that Vietnamese law requires zero alcohol while driving — not a 0.08 threshold, zero.

The option this comparison usually forgets
For the genuinely long legs, neither riding nor hiring a driver is the obvious answer — flying or taking the train often is. Hanoi to Da Nang is roughly an hour and a quarter in the air against fourteen hours or more on the ground, and domestic fares are low enough that the arithmetic rarely favours the road.
The train earns its place on one stretch in particular. The Hue to Da Nang leg over the Hai Van Pass is a sight in its own right, costs very little, and gives you the coastal views that are the whole reason people consider riding that road. Taking the train there and a car elsewhere is a common and sensible split.

How to decide
A useful split: hire a car with a driver for the long intercity legs, use Grab in the cities, and take an easy-rider for the one mountain route you actually came for. That covers the whole country legally and costs less than most people expect.
What makes this worth deciding early is that it is not really a transport choice. It determines which places are reachable, how long each leg takes, and therefore what the itinerary can contain — which is why it belongs in the same conversation as your route rather than being sorted out on arrival. the complete Vietnam decision manual works that sequence through in order. The licence and insurance detail sits in our guide to renting a motorbike in Vietnam.

TITLE: Vietnam Customs and Duty-Free Rules
EXCERPT: What you can bring into Vietnam duty-free, the cash you must declare, and the one rule that works in your favour at the red channel.

Vietnamese customs is straightforward for an ordinary tourist, and most people walk through the green channel without speaking to anyone. The allowances are worth knowing anyway, because two of them — cash and alcohol — are easy to exceed without noticing, and the rules are more generous and more precise than the summaries online suggest.

Alcohol: three limits, pick one
Vietnam's duty-free allowance on arrival gives each traveller, per entry, 1.5 litres of spirits at 20% ABV or above, or 2 litres under 20%, or 3 litres of beer. These are alternatives rather than a combined total — you get one of the three, not all of them.
There is a sensible overflow provision most sources omit. A single bottle that goes over the limit by no more than one litre passes duty-free in its entirety; past one litre over, only the excess is dutiable. In practice that means a standard bottle bought in an airport duty-free shop on the way in is almost never a problem.

Tobacco
The tobacco allowance is 200 cigarettes, or 250 grams of shredded tobacco, or 20 cigars per entry, and again these are alternatives. The limits do not apply to anyone under eighteen, who has no allowance at all rather than a reduced one.

Everything else: a value cap, not an item list
Beyond alcohol, tobacco and a reasonable quantity of personal belongings, other goods come in duty-free up to a total customs value of 10,000,000 dong per entry. The figure is set in dong by decree, so its dollar equivalent drifts with the exchange rate — check the current conversion rather than trusting a number you read somewhere.
For a normal holiday this is not a constraint. It becomes one if you are bringing gifts in quantity, carrying new electronics still in their boxes, or travelling with professional equipment. Anything above the allowance has to clear customs as imported goods rather than as luggage.

The rule that works in your favour
One provision is worth knowing before you reach the counter: if you are over the allowance, you choose which items count inside it and which are declared. The decree gives that choice to the traveller, not to the officer. If you are carrying one expensive item and several cheap ones, you can place the expensive one inside the allowance and declare the remainder.

None of this replaces the entry requirements themselves, which are a separate matter — passport validity, the pre-arrival declaration and the visa are covered in our guide to Vietnam visa requirements for Americans.

Cash you must declare
There is no limit on how much money you may bring into or out of Vietnam, but above either threshold you must declare it in writing: more than 15,000,000 dong, or more than 5,000 US dollars in foreign currency. The same figures apply on the way out.
Note what the obligation actually is. You are not asking permission and you are not being taxed — you are filling in a form. A competing figure of 7,000 dollars circulates online and traces back to a superseded notice from 2005; the current thresholds are the ones above.

What you cannot bring
The prohibited-import list is set by decree and changed on 5 September 2026, when Decree 292/2026 replaced Decree 69/2018. The categories that matter to a tourist are the predictable ones: weapons and ammunition, narcotics, and politically sensitive publications or material deemed contrary to Vietnamese cultural norms.
Two practical notes. Drones frequently attract attention and may require permission, so research before packing one rather than discovering the rule at the airport. And prescription medication should travel in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the prescription, because some ingredients in common prescriptions, including certain ADHD treatments, are controlled in Vietnam.

The allowance resets each entry
Every figure here is per person, per entry, granted against your passport. If your trip includes a hop to Cambodia or Laos and back, you arrive with a fresh allowance on the return — which matters more for someone doing a border run than for a single-entry visitor.
The personal-belongings category is deliberately written as a reasonable quantity rather than a list, which gives officers discretion and gives you latitude. Clothes, a laptop, a camera and a phone in ordinary numbers are luggage. Six identical new phones in retail packaging are not, whatever their total value, because the question shifts from what they are worth to what they are for.

Taking things home
The allowances above govern what comes in. On the way out your constraint is mostly your own country's: US Customs sets what you may bring back duty-free and that figure has nothing to do with Vietnam's. The one Vietnamese restriction worth checking before you buy is on genuine antiques and certain cultural objects, which require documentation to export — a reproduction lacquer piece is fine, a genuinely old one may not be, and a reputable dealer will tell you which you are holding.

Green channel, red channel
If you are inside every allowance and carrying nothing restricted, you take the green channel and keep walking. If you are over on any of them, or carrying cash above the thresholds, take the red channel and declare. The paperwork is short and declaring costs you nothing; being found over the line after choosing green is a different conversation entirely.
