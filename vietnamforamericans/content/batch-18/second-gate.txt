----- ARTICLE: Vietnam Itinerary: 10 Days  (slug vietnam-itinerary-10-days)
EXCERPT: Ten days is the length where three regions become possible without becoming a transit log. Here is the shape that works.

Ten days on the ground is the first length at which all three of Vietnam's regions fit without the trip turning into a sequence of airports. A week forces you to pick two. Fourteen days gives you slack. Ten sits between them, and the plan has to be tighter than the one for two weeks because there is no room to absorb a mistake.

The frame

Fly into Hanoi, out of Ho Chi Minh City, open-jaw. Two internal flights: Hanoi to Da Nang, and Da Nang to Ho Chi Minh City. Count the travel days separately from the ten — the crossing takes a calendar day each way, and the arrival day should be treated as a write-off.

There is no nonstop from the United States to Hanoi, so a north-first trip means a connection. If you are flying the nonstop from San Francisco or Los Angeles to Ho Chi Minh City, run everything below in reverse.

Days 1 to 3 — Hanoi

Arrive, lose the afternoon, and give the city two full days. Hoan Kiem and the Old Quarter on foot, the Temple of Literature, the Ho Chi Minh complex in a morning, and one museum. Bun cha for lunch, pho for breakfast.

Two days is enough for Hanoi on a ten-day trip, and the temptation to make it three is what costs you the centre later.

Days 4 to 5 — the northern excursion

One, not two. The realistic options are an overnight cruise on Ha Long or Lan Ha bay, or two days in Ninh Binh, which is about two hours from Hanoi and gives you the same karst landscape on rivers.

Sapa is the one to leave out at this length. It is five to six hours each way, which is most of two days of the ten spent on the road, and the trek itself wants more time than what remains.

If the bay is the image you came for, take the cruise. If you would rather walk and cycle than sit on a boat, take Ninh Binh — it costs less travel and you can do it as a long day trip or an overnight.

Days 6 to 8 — the centre

Fly Hanoi to Da Nang early on day six and transfer to Hoi An, about forty-five minutes. That gives you an afternoon, two full days and a morning in the centre.

Spend them on the Ancient Town at its quiet hours, a day for My Son or the Marble Mountains, and a beach afternoon at An Bang. If you want clothes made, order on the first morning — two fittings need the time.

Hue can be added as a day trip from Hoi An by train to Da Nang and car, but it is a long day and the Imperial City deserves better. On ten days, either give Hue a night of its own and take a day from somewhere, or leave it.

Days 9 to 10 — the south

Fly Da Nang to Ho Chi Minh City on the morning of day nine. Two days there covers the War Remnants Museum and Reunification Palace, Ben Thanh and the markets, a street food evening, and the Cu Chi tunnels if the war sites are a priority.

Leave the Mekong Delta out. The packaged day trip from the city is widely reported as the weakest day on a Vietnam itinerary, and the version worth doing needs an overnight in Can Tho for the dawn floating market. Neither fits in ten days without taking the centre apart.

The alternative: centre and south, slower

If three regions in ten days sounds like too much movement, the other shape is two regions done properly. Four nights in Hoi An with Hue as an overnight, then four in Ho Chi Minh City with an overnight in Can Tho for the floating market, and one internal flight instead of two.

That version loses Hanoi and the bay, which for most first visits is a large thing to lose. It is the better plan if you are returning to Vietnam, or if the pace matters more than the coverage.

What the plan is actually trading

Three things get cut to make ten days work: Sapa, Hue as a proper stop, and the Mekong. Each of those is a real destination and each would be in a two-week version. If one of them is the reason you are going to Vietnam, the plan changes shape around it rather than adding to it.

The other honest limitation is the weather. Vietnam's regions have different wet seasons, and ten days moving through all three means at least one region is likely to be in its difficult months whatever you do.

Booking order

International tickets first, because they set the direction and everything else follows. Then the two internal flights, which are cheap and fill at the useful hours. Then accommodation. Then the cruise or Ninh Binh. Tours and classes can wait until you are in the country.

Which region to build the trip around when the month you can travel does not suit it is a question the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: A First Vietnam Itinerary: How to Decide the Shape  (slug vietnam-itinerary-first-timers)
EXCERPT: Before the day-by-day, four decisions set everything else. Make them in this order.

Most Vietnam itineraries go wrong before any days are assigned, because the shape of the trip was decided by accident. Four questions settle it, and they have to be answered in order, because each one closes off options for the next.

One: how many days on the ground

Not how many days of leave. Vietnam is a long flight from the United States and the crossing eats a calendar day each way, with the arrival day largely written off. Ten days of leave is seven or eight days of Vietnam.

Count the real number first, because every subsequent decision scales off it. Seven days on the ground is two regions. Ten is three, tightly. Fourteen is three with slack. Three weeks is three regions plus the things everybody cuts.

Two: which regions

Vietnam divides into north, centre and south, each about an hour's flight from the next. The north is Hanoi, Ha Long, Ninh Binh and Sapa. The centre is Hue, Da Nang and Hoi An. The south is Ho Chi Minh City, the Mekong Delta and the islands.

On a short trip, pick adjacent regions. North plus centre, or centre plus south. The north-plus-south combination skips the middle, which is where Hoi An and Hue are, and is the one people most often regret.

The thing that actually decides it

Whichever region holds the thing you most want to see. If that is Ha Long Bay, you are starting north. If it is Hoi An, you are building around the centre. Pick that anchor first and let the adjacent region be the second one.

Three: which month

Vietnam does not have one dry season. The north is coldest and greyest from roughly December to February and hottest and wettest in summer. The central coast has a storm season from September to November. The south is wet from May to October, in the afternoon-downpour way rather than the all-day way.

There is no month that is perfect everywhere, which means the month you can travel partly decides which regions are worth the days. Travelling in October with the centre as your anchor is a plan with a problem in it.

Four: which direction

North to south is the more common shape and suits most itineraries: the harder city first, the weather-sensitive north early, and a departure from the city with the widest choice of onward flights.

South to north is the right answer if the nonstop from San Francisco or Los Angeles to Ho Chi Minh City is what makes the trip work, or if the season favours finishing in the north. Open-jaw tickets into one city and out of the other are widely available, so there is rarely a reason to backtrack.

This decision is effectively locked when you buy the international tickets, which is usually before anything else is planned. That is why it belongs in the first four questions rather than the day-by-day.

What the month does to the answer

Worked examples. December: the north is cold and often grey, the centre is coming out of its storm season, the south is dry. A southern-weighted trip makes sense. June: the north is hot and wet in the afternoons, the centre is dry, the south is in its wet season. A centre-weighted trip makes sense. March and April are the closest the country comes to working everywhere at once, which is also why they are busy.

Then, and only then, the days

With those four answered, the day plan writes itself from a small number of rules. Two nights minimum in any city worth stopping in. One overnight excursion per region at most. A transfer day is a half day gone, not a free one. And the internal flights are short and cheap, so use them rather than overland for the long legs.

The things people underestimate

How long Hanoi to Hoi An takes door to door, which is a flight plus two transfers rather than an hour. How much of a cruise day is the coach. How much time the Mekong needs to be worth doing. And how tired the first forty-eight hours are.

What to book and when

International flights first — they lock the direction. Internal flights next: they are short and inexpensive and the useful departure times go first. Then accommodation in the places you have fixed. Then one or two anchor experiences, such as a bay cruise, that need a date.

Everything else — day tours, cooking classes, drivers, food tours — can be arranged in the country a day or two ahead, usually more cheaply and with better information about the weather.

A sanity check before booking

Write the plan out and count the half-days lost to movement. If more than a quarter of the trip is transfers, remove a region rather than compressing the stays. A shorter list done properly is the thing people remember; a longer list done at speed is the thing they describe as exhausting.

And leave one unplanned day in anything longer than a week. It will be used, either by weather or by a place that turned out to be better than expected.


----- ARTICLE: Vietnam with Children: Planning the Trip  (slug vietnam-itinerary-for-families)
EXCERPT: A good country for family travel, with three genuine constraints. How to build around them.

Vietnam works well with children. The food is mild by default and endlessly snackable, Vietnamese attitudes to children travelling are warm to the point of enthusiastic, and the costs are low enough that a family can afford space and comfort that would be out of reach elsewhere. There are three real constraints, and the plan is built around them.

Constraint one: the traffic

Road traffic is the hazard the State Department emphasises most for Vietnam, and city streets are the part of the trip that demands most attention with small children. Crossing a busy road in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City with a child is a different exercise from doing it alone.

What this means practically: hold hands and cross as a unit at a steady pace; use Grab rather than walking long distances in the cities; and choose accommodation on a quieter street rather than the busiest one.

Car seats are not standard in Vietnamese taxis or ride-hailing cars. Families travelling with small children either bring a portable seat or accept the arrangement as it is, and that is a decision worth making before you arrive rather than at the airport.

Constraint two: the distances

Vietnam is long, and the trip shape that works for adults — three regions in ten days — is punishing with children. Fewer places and longer stays is the single most useful adjustment.

A family week is best as one region. A family fortnight is two, with a beach stay as one of them. Use the internal flights rather than overnight buses or long drives.

Constraint three: the heat

Most of Vietnam is hot and humid for most of the year, and children tolerate it less well than adults. Build the day around it: out early, back for the middle of the day, out again in the late afternoon. Accommodation with a pool stops being a luxury and becomes the thing that makes the afternoon work.

What children actually enjoy here

Water, mostly. The bay cruises, the kayaking, the beaches at An Bang, Da Nang and Phu Quoc, and the delta boats. Ba Na Hills, with the cable car and the games area, is the most deliberately child-oriented day out in the country. The water puppet theatre in Hanoi is short and genuinely engaging.

Also: lantern-making and cooking classes in Hoi An, cycling through the rice paddies at Tra Que, the Marble Mountains caves, and the sheer novelty of the street life.

What to go lightly on

The war sites. The War Remnants Museum in Ho Chi Minh City presents the conflict unsparingly, and parents of younger children often choose Reunification Palace instead. Cu Chi involves confined tunnels and a firing range whose noise carries across the site. These are decisions to make deliberately rather than on arrival.

Ages and what changes

With a baby or toddler, the heat and the pavements are the constraints: Vietnamese city pavements are frequently occupied by parked motorbikes and stalls, and a pushchair is often more trouble than a carrier. With primary-age children, the water activities and the sheer sensory volume of the place do most of the work. Teenagers do well here, particularly with the food, the markets and the motorbike-passenger side of it.

Ages also change the war sites, the length of the days and how much heritage anyone will tolerate before lunch.

A shape that works for two weeks

Three or four nights in Hanoi and a bay cruise; a flight to Da Nang and five or six nights split between Hoi An and the beach; a flight south and three nights in Ho Chi Minh City, or straight to Phu Quoc if the trip should end on sand.

Two internal flights, two bases that are more than three nights each, and a pool in at least one of them.

Practical points

Nappies, formula and familiar medicines are available in the cities and harder to find in small towns; buy in Hanoi, Da Nang or Ho Chi Minh City. Pharmacies are everywhere and well stocked.

Apartments and family rooms are cheap by American standards and far more useful than two hotel rooms. Many hotels will add a bed for very little.

Food is easy: rice, noodles, grilled meat, fresh spring rolls and fruit cover most children, and chilli is almost always served on the side rather than cooked in.

Getting around with children

Grab is the default and the fares are low enough that taking cars everywhere in the cities is affordable. For longer legs use the internal flights, which are short. The overnight train is a genuine option with older children and an adventure in its own right; the sleeper bus is not, with any age.

Health before you go

Talk to a travel clinic about the routine and recommended vaccinations well in advance, since some need more than one dose. Carry oral rehydration salts, which are the single most useful thing in the bag with children in a hot country.


----- ARTICLE: Temples and Pagodas in Vietnam: What You Are Looking At  (slug vietnam-temples-pagodas-guide)
EXCERPT: Buddhist, Taoist, Confucian, Cham and Cao Dai, often in the same town. A short guide to telling them apart.

Vietnam's religious buildings come from several different traditions, and a visitor who treats them all as "temples" misses most of what is interesting. Here is what the main kinds are, how to recognise them, and how to behave inside.

Chua — the Buddhist pagoda

The most common. A chua is a Buddhist place of worship, usually a complex of halls around courtyards rather than a single building, with a main hall holding Buddha statues, incense burners, and often a bell and a drum.

Vietnamese Buddhism is predominantly Mahayana in the north and centre, which is why you will see Quan Am — the bodhisattva of compassion, the Vietnamese form of Guanyin — very frequently, often as a white standing figure outside. In parts of the south and the Mekong, Theravada Buddhism of the Khmer tradition appears instead, and those temples look quite different: taller, more ornate, closer to Cambodian architecture.

Den — the temple

A den honours a person or a spirit rather than the Buddha: a national hero, a historical general, a mother goddess. Den Ngoc Son on Hoan Kiem lake in Hanoi is one; the Tran Hung Dao temples across the north are others. They often look similar to a chua from outside and the difference is who is on the altar.

Van Mieu — the Confucian temple of literature

Dedicated to Confucius and to scholarship rather than to a deity. The one in Hanoi was Vietnam's first university and has stone stelae on turtles' backs recording the names of doctoral graduates from the fifteenth century on. Several provincial cities have smaller versions.

Dinh — the communal house

The village hall and the shrine of the village's tutelary spirit, usually the oldest and most elaborately carved wooden building in an old village. These are not tourist sites and are often closed, but the woodwork inside the ones that open is some of the best in the country.

Cham towers

Brick towers from the Cham civilisation, Hindu rather than Buddhist, found along the central and southern coast. My Son near Hoi An is the largest group; Po Nagar in Nha Trang and the towers around Phan Rang are others. The architecture reads closer to Indian and Khmer temple building than to anything else in Vietnam.

Cao Dai temples

Cao Dai is a Vietnamese religion founded in the 1920s, syncretic and visually unmistakable: bright colours, a divine eye above the altar, and a congregation in white. The Great Temple at Tay Ninh, north-west of Ho Chi Minh City, is the centre and is the usual place visitors see one. Services run at set times and visitors are welcome at the back.

Churches

Vietnam has a substantial Catholic population and the French-era churches are a visible part of the urban landscape — St Joseph's in Hanoi, the Notre-Dame basilica in Ho Chi Minh City, the pink church in Da Lat, and the stone cathedral at Phat Diem, which was built in Vietnamese temple style.

What is usually inside a chua

The layout repeats enough to be worth learning. A triple gate or an entrance arch; a courtyard, often with a bodhisattva figure and a pond; the main hall with tiered altars, the largest Buddha figures at the back and offerings of fruit and flowers in front; a bell and a drum, either in the hall or in their own small towers; and behind or beside it, an ancestral hall with photographs of the dead and the monks of the pagoda.

Incense burners stand outside the hall doors. The smoke and the stacked coils hanging from the ceiling in busier pagodas are the first thing you will notice.

How to behave

Cover shoulders and knees. Remove shoes where you see others doing so, which is usually at the threshold of an inner hall rather than the courtyard. Step over the raised threshold rather than on it.

Do not point your feet at an altar or a statue, which means watching how you sit. Walk around a statue rather than in front of someone praying. Ask before photographing people at worship, and look for signs prohibiting photography inside halls.

If you want to offer incense, buy it at the gate, light it, hold it at forehead height with both hands, bow, and place it in the burner. Nobody minds a visitor doing this and nobody expects it.

The ones worth going out of your way for

The Perfume Pagoda south of Hanoi, which is a boat trip and a climb. Bai Dinh and the Trang An complex at Ninh Binh, vast and modern. Thien Mu above the Perfume River at Hue. The Marble Mountains caves near Da Nang, where the shrines are inside the rock. Linh Ung on the Son Tra peninsula with its tall Lady Buddha. And the Cao Dai Great Temple at Tay Ninh, which is unlike anything else in the country.

Most of these are covered in their own right elsewhere on this site; what they have in common is that the setting is as much the point as the building.

Donations

Boxes are provided and contributions are voluntary. At some popular sites you will be offered incense or a blessing by someone who then expects payment; that is a transaction rather than a temple custom, and declining is fine.


----- ARTICLE: War History Sites in Vietnam: A Guide for American Visitors  (slug vietnam-war-history-sites-guide)
EXCERPT: Where the war is on the ground rather than in a museum, how the sites are framed, and what to expect as an American.

For a lot of American visitors the war is part of why they are going, and Vietnam has preserved a great deal of it. The sites are spread the length of the country, they vary enormously in what they offer, and they are presented from a Vietnamese perspective — which is the point of visiting them rather than a caveat.

The south: Ho Chi Minh City and Cu Chi

The War Remnants Museum is the main one. It presents the conflict unsparingly, with heavy emphasis on civilian casualties, chemical defoliants and their consequences. Aircraft and armour in the courtyard, photojournalism upstairs. It is a difficult couple of hours and it is the single most visited war site in the country.

Reunification Palace, ten minutes away, is preserved roughly as it was in April 1975 — the cabinet room, the president's quarters, the basement command bunker. It completes the picture the museum starts: the museum shows the war as it was experienced, the palace shows the government conducting it and the rooms where that ended.

Cu Chi is an hour and a half to two hours out of the city, a tunnel system with sections widened for visitors, a firing range whose noise carries, and displays of the tunnels' construction. Two sites operate; Ben Duoc is the quieter of them and is worth asking for.

The centre: the DMZ out of Hue

The old demilitarised zone along the seventeenth parallel is about two hours north of Hue and is a long day out. The circuit takes in the Ben Hai river and the old border, the Khe Sanh combat base, and the Vinh Moc tunnels.

Vinh Moc is the one most people remember. A village lived underground there through years of bombardment, and the tunnels were dug for civilians to live in rather than for fighters to move through. They are reported to be close to their original dimensions rather than widened for visitors.

Khe Sanh divides opinion — a museum, a stretch of airstrip, equipment in the open, and the hills the 1968 siege was fought over. If it is specifically why you are going, say so when booking, because some itineraries allow very little time there.

The north: Hanoi

Hoa Lo Prison, the "Hanoi Hilton", is now a museum. Most of it covers the French colonial period and Vietnamese political prisoners; a smaller section covers the American pilots held there and is presented in terms American visitors generally find striking for its framing rather than its content.

The B-52 wreckage in Huu Tiep lake, in a residential street in the Ngoc Ha district, is a fragment of a bomber left where it came down in December 1972. It takes ten minutes and it is the most unexpected of these sites.

The Vietnam Military History Museum has moved to a large new building on the western edge of the city with a substantial outdoor collection of aircraft and armour.

What is not a war site, and still is

Some of the most affecting things are not presented as sites at all. The Long Bien bridge in Hanoi, bombed repeatedly and still carrying trains and bicycles. The war cemeteries along Route 9 and at Truong Son, which are enormous and almost entirely visited by Vietnamese families. The ordinary presence of people with disabilities attributed to defoliant exposure, which the War Remnants Museum addresses directly and which is visible outside it.

None of these requires a ticket and all of them change the register of the trip.

What to expect from the framing

These are Vietnamese national sites, and the war is called the American War. The presentation is unambiguous and in places polemical, particularly at the War Remnants Museum. Going in expecting a balanced international account is the wrong expectation; going in to see how the war is remembered here is the right one.

That is a different thing from the reception you get. Visitors commonly report being treated with ordinary warmth at all of these places, by staff and by other visitors.

A note on the ground itself

Unexploded ordnance remains a real hazard in parts of central Vietnam, concentrated at construction sites and away from populated areas; the landmine risk is low in urban and populated areas, including tourist destinations. Stay on marked paths at the DMZ sites and do not pick anything up.

Going with a guide

At the DMZ sites a guide is close to essential, because most of what you are looking at is terrain and the interest is in what happened on it. At the museums it matters less — the exhibits carry their own captions, in English.

Veterans and families of veterans travelling to specific locations are generally better served by a private guide and driver than by a group tour, and there are operators who specialise in it. Be clear about the places you want when you book; a standard circuit will not go to them.

How to sequence them

If you are doing the whole country, you will meet these in order: Hanoi's sites early, the DMZ from Hue in the middle, the museum and Cu Chi at the end. That is a reasonable progression, and it means the heaviest material comes last rather than on your second day.

Doing the War Remnants Museum and Cu Chi on the same day is possible and compresses a great deal into one day with a long drive in the middle. Splitting them gives each room.
