----- ARTICLE: Where to Stay in Hoi An and Da Nang  (slug where-to-stay-hoian-danang)
EXCERPT: Six distinct areas across two places twenty-five miles apart, and what each one is actually like to wake up in.

Hoi An and Da Nang are usually booked as one leg of a trip, which makes the accommodation question more complicated than it looks: you are choosing not just a hotel but which of six quite different areas you wake up in. This page describes each one and who it suits.

Hoi An: inside the Ancient Town

Small hotels and converted houses on and immediately around the old streets. You walk to the restaurants, the tailors, the riverside and the lantern-lit streets at night, and you can be out in the town at the hours when it is quietest.

The trade is noise and traffic. The old town is busy through the middle of the day, and some streets carry bar noise late. Ask for a room at the back, and look at where the hotel sits relative to the main nightlife street before booking.

Hoi An: the edge of town and the paddies

A ring of mid-size resorts and guesthouses sits in the rice fields a few minutes out, between the Ancient Town and the beach. Prices tend to be lower than in the centre for more space, and many have pools.

You will use a bicycle or a Grab for everything. That is a mild inconvenience in daylight and a decision to make each evening.

An Bang and the Hoi An beaches

Low-rise guesthouses, villas and small resorts behind a wide beach, roughly ten to fifteen minutes from the Ancient Town by bicycle. Beach restaurants, a slower pace, and sand at the door.

It suits longer stays and families. The limitation is the same one that makes it pleasant: you are not in the old town, so evenings there take planning. During the September-to-November storm season the beach is the part of the proposition that fails first.

Da Nang: My Khe and the beachfront

A long strip of beachfront hotels, from budget rooms a block back to the large international resorts on the sand. This is where most visitors to Da Nang stay.

You get a proper beach, a lot of choice at every price, and the city behind you. You also get a strip that is genuinely built up, with the character that implies. The Ancient Town is around forty-five minutes away by car, so this is a base for Da Nang rather than a base for Hoi An.

Da Nang: the city centre and the Han River

Across the river from the beach: business hotels, apartment blocks, the Han Market, the riverfront and the bridges. Cheaper than the beachfront, and closer to where the city actually eats.

It suits short stops, early flights, and travellers who would rather be in a working city than on a resort strip. The beach is a short Grab ride when you want it.

Da Nang: An Thuong

The grid of streets a block or two back from My Khe beach, with small hotels, hostels, cafes and western-facing restaurants. Cheaper than the beachfront, walkable to the sand, and where a good deal of the longer-stay and remote-working crowd ends up.

It suits anyone who wants the beach without resort prices, and anyone staying more than a few nights. It is less suitable if you want a quiet residential street or a view.

What the choice turns on

Three questions settle most of it. How many nights do you have in the region? Is the Ancient Town the point of the stay, or one thing among several? And are you travelling in the dry stretch or the storm season?

Two nights with the old town as the reason: stay in it. Four or more with beach time: An Bang, or split. A week, a family, or a flight at either end: Da Nang, and treat Hoi An as a day and an evening out.

The split

Two nights in each is a common shape and costs one transfer. It is the version that gets you the Ancient Town early and late, and a city with an airport at the end of the leg.

Practical points before you book

Ask whether the property runs a shuttle to the Ancient Town and at what times — it changes how easily you get to dinner from anywhere outside the centre. Check the walking distance from the hotel to the old town rather than the stated distance to "Hoi An", which can mean the district.

In Hoi An, check how close the property is to the river if you are travelling between September and November; the streets nearest the water flood in some years. In Da Nang, check which side of the river you are on, because the bridges add time at busy hours.

What you are paying for

The pattern across both places is that the premium sits on position rather than on the room. A central Ancient Town room and a larger room with a pool ten minutes out often cost about the same; the difference is whether you walk home from dinner. On the Da Nang beachfront the premium is the sand, and a block back it disappears quickly.

That makes the useful question not "how much" but "what does this position save me each day", which is answerable before you book.

Booking

Both places are heavily supplied with accommodation at every level, and outside the main holiday periods there is usually availability. The exception is the lunar new year period and the local holiday weeks, when the domestic travel market fills the coast.

Where this gets harder is when the accommodation question meets the shape of the trip — how many nights the region gets at all, and what is already booked on either side of it. That wider ordering is what the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: Hue to Hoi An: Train, Car, or the Hai Van Pass  (slug hue-to-hoian-how-to-travel)
EXCERPT: A short leg with four quite different versions, and the one most people should take is not the one most people book.

Hue to Hoi An is one of the shorter journeys on a Vietnam itinerary and one of the more interesting. The two places are separated by a mountain spur that drops to the sea, and how you cross it changes the day considerably.

The geography, briefly

Hue sits north of the Hai Van Pass, Da Nang immediately south of it, and Hoi An about forty-five minutes further south again. There is no airport or railway station at Hoi An, so every version of this journey goes through or past Da Nang.

That means the real choice is how you get from Hue to Da Nang. The last stretch to Hoi An is a car either way.

By train to Da Nang, then car

The railway runs along the coast below the road, and the Hue to Da Nang run is widely regarded as one of the best rail stretches in Southeast Asia. Allow around three hours. Tickets cost very little and are sold at the stations and through the national railway's booking site.

At Da Nang you take a Grab or a pre-booked car for the last leg to Hoi An. The whole thing is cheap, the scenery is the point, and it replaces a transfer you were making anyway. Ask for a seat on the sea side when you book.

By private car over the pass

A car with a driver, the whole way, over the Hai Van Pass rather than through the tunnel beneath it. With stops at the summit and perhaps a beach, it is a half day rather than a transfer.

This is the flexible version: your luggage goes door to door, you stop where you want, and you can add the Marble Mountains or Lang Co on the way. Say explicitly when booking that you want the pass and not the tunnel, because the tunnel is the default for much traffic.

By car through the tunnel

Faster and cheaper than the pass, and it sees none of the view. This is what a standard hotel transfer will usually do unless you ask otherwise. Reasonable if you are short of time or the weather has closed the view anyway.

By motorbike

The pass is well known as a ride, and the easy-rider arrangement — riding pillion behind an experienced local while your bag travels by van — is a common way to do this leg. It turns the transfer into the day's activity.

Riding it yourself is a different matter. A US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, driving without a Vietnamese licence can mean a fine, arrest, or jail, and your insurer may decline a claim after an accident. The road is also demanding: switchbacks, trucks, sudden cloud and wet surfaces, and road traffic is the main hazard for visitors to Vietnam.

By bus

Tourist buses and sleeper buses run the route and are the cheapest option. They generally take the tunnel. They are a reasonable choice if the budget is the constraint and the view is not the point.

What is worth stopping for

If you are going by road, the stops are what justify the version. The summit of the pass, with the old gate and the view back along the coast when the cloud allows. Lang Co, a long beach and lagoon at the northern foot of the pass. The Marble Mountains, a cluster of limestone hills with caves and pagodas just south of Da Nang, which sit almost exactly on the route.

A car doing all three turns the transfer into a full day out. A car doing none of them is just a transfer, and the train does that better.

How long each takes

Hue to Da Nang is about 100 kilometres. The train runs to a timetable; allow around three hours plus the last leg to Hoi An. A car through the tunnel is a straightforward morning. A car over the pass with stops is a half day. An easy-rider trip takes most of a day, because the stopping is what you are paying for.

None of these is a quick hop, and the mistake people make is treating this leg as dead time to be minimised rather than as something worth building the day around.

Weather and the view

The pass makes its own weather and can be sitting in cloud when both Hue and Da Nang are clear. September to November is the central coast's storm season and the worst stretch for a road crossing — cloud on the top, wet surfaces, and a view that may not appear.

The railway runs lower and closer to the sea, which is the practical reason it is the more reliable version of this journey in poor weather. Check the forecast the evening before if you are deciding between the two.

Which to pick

Train if you want the best of it for the least money and effort, and it is also the version least affected by cloud sitting on the pass. Private car over the pass if you have luggage, company, or want to stop. Easy-rider if the road itself is the reason. Tunnel or bus if you are simply moving.

Booking it

Train tickets are sold at Hue station and online through the railway's own site; book a day or two ahead for the better carriages. Cars and easy-rider trips are arranged through hotels and local operators in Hue, usually the day before. Agree in advance what is included — the stops, the luggage, whether the price covers the run from Da Nang to Hoi An.

Vietnam's other long legs raise the same question in harder form, and that is something the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: Hue Travel Guide  (slug hue-travel-guide)
EXCERPT: The imperial capital, a river, royal tombs spread across the countryside, and the food Vietnamese people travel for.

Hue was the capital of Vietnam under the Nguyen dynasty, the country's last ruling house, and the seat of its emperors until the middle of the twentieth century. What remains is a walled citadel on the north bank of the Perfume River, a scatter of royal tombs in the hills outside, and a distinct court cuisine.

The shape of the city

The Perfume River divides Hue. On the north bank is the Citadel — a walled and moated enclosure holding the Imperial City, which held the Forbidden Purple City at its centre. On the south bank is the modern town: most of the hotels, the restaurants, the backpacker streets and the riverside promenade.

The two are connected by bridges and the distances are walkable in good weather, though most people use a Grab for the crossing.

What to see

The Imperial City is the main event and needs a half day on its own. Beyond it, the royal tombs are what distinguish Hue from anywhere else in Vietnam — each emperor built his own, and they are spread through the countryside south and west of the city, set in gardens, lakes and pine.

The three most visited are Minh Mang, Tu Duc and Khai Dinh, and they are deliberately unalike: Minh Mang formal and symmetrical, Tu Duc rambling and overgrown, Khai Dinh a dark concrete hybrid of Vietnamese and European styles. Seeing two or three is better than trying to see them all.

Thien Mu Pagoda

A seven-tiered tower on a rise above the river, a few kilometres upstream from the Citadel, and one of the images most associated with the city. It can be reached by road or by dragon boat along the Perfume River, and the boat is the pleasanter approach.

Getting around the tombs

The tombs are not walkable from the centre and not close to each other. The usual options are a hired car with a driver for a half or full day, a motorbike taxi arrangement, a bicycle for the nearer ones if you are fit and the weather allows, or a boat trip that combines a pagoda with one or two tombs.

A car with a driver is the straightforward version. Agree the list of stops and the price before setting off, and expect each tomb to need thirty to sixty minutes.

The Perfume River

Dragon boats run upstream from the city towards Thien Mu and, on the longer trips, on to one or two of the tombs. The boats are slow and the commentary is limited, but the river is the thread the whole city was laid out along, and seeing it from the water reorders the map in your head.

Shorter evening cruises also run from the south bank. Agree the route and price before boarding, and be clear whether tomb entry fees are included.

The food

Hue has a culinary reputation within Vietnam that is out of proportion to its size, a legacy of the court. Bun bo Hue, a beef and lemongrass noodle soup, is the dish that travelled; it is spicier and more robust than northern pho and is eaten here at breakfast.

Beyond it the city is known for a family of small steamed and savoury cakes — banh beo, banh nam, banh loc — usually served as a set of little dishes. Com hen, rice with baby clams, is the local speciality that visitors most often miss.

Where to eat it

The south bank has the restaurants aimed at visitors; the better bun bo Hue is generally found at small specialist places that sell one dish and open early. The Dong Ba market on the north bank near the Citadel is the other place to eat, and is the city's main market besides.

When to go

Hue sits on the central coast and shares its weather pattern. September to November is the storm season, and Hue has a reputation for rain that extends beyond it — the city is wetter than Da Nang and Hoi An through much of the cool season. The drier, hotter stretch runs roughly from February to August.

Plan indoor-tolerant days if you are there in the wet months. The Imperial City is largely open ground and is a much better visit in dry weather.

How long to stay

One full day covers the Imperial City and one or two tombs at a brisk pace. Two days lets you do that without rushing and add the pagoda, the market and an evening on the river. More than two is for people with a specific interest in the dynasty or the food.

Many itineraries give Hue a single night on the way between Hanoi and Hoi An. That works, but it means choosing between the Citadel and the tombs rather than seeing both properly.

Getting there and away

Hue has its own airport with domestic flights, and a station on the north-south railway. The coastal train south to Da Nang is one of the best stretches in the country. North, the overnight train and the flights both run to Hanoi.

The DMZ and the Vinh Moc tunnels are a day trip north of the city, and they are the reason some visitors give Hue an extra night.


----- ARTICLE: Hue Imperial City Guide  (slug hue-imperial-city-guide)
EXCERPT: A walled city inside a walled city, heavily damaged in 1968 and under restoration since. What survives, and how to walk it.

The Imperial City at Hue is the walled palace complex of the Nguyen emperors, built from the early nineteenth century inside the larger Citadel. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and much of it is ruin, open ground and ongoing restoration rather than intact building.

Three walls, not one

The structure is easier to follow once you know it is concentric. The outermost is the Citadel, a large fortified enclosure with a moat, containing a working district of the city as well as the palace. Inside it is the Imperial City, the ceremonial and administrative enclosure. Inside that was the Forbidden Purple City, the emperor's private residence.

What you buy a ticket for is the Imperial City. The Forbidden Purple City is largely gone.

What happened to it

The complex was badly damaged during the Battle of Hue in 1968, which was among the longest and most destructive urban battles of the war. A large part of the Forbidden Purple City was destroyed. Restoration has been continuous since the site's UNESCO listing, and you will see scaffolding and rebuilt sections alongside original fabric and empty foundations.

The walk through

Enter through the Ngo Mon gate, the main southern gate with the pavilion on top from which the last emperor abdicated in 1945. Beyond it a bridge and courtyard lead to the Thai Hoa Palace, the throne hall, which is the best-preserved major building in the complex and the one that shows what the rest looked like.

Behind that the ground opens out. The halls of the mandarins face each other across a courtyard; further back are the Forbidden Purple City's foundations, the Royal Theatre, the reading pavilion, and the temple complexes on the western side.

The Mieu temples

The To Mieu temple compound on the west side holds the dynastic altars and the Nine Dynastic Urns, large bronze vessels cast in the 1830s, each dedicated to an emperor and decorated with scenes and objects from around the country. This corner is among the most intact and is often the quietest part of the site.

The Citadel outside the ticket

The enclosure around the Imperial City is a living district, not a monument: houses, shops, schools and streets inside the fortified walls and moat. Walking or cycling a stretch of it before or after the palace costs nothing and gives the scale of the thing, which is hard to read from inside the ceremonial enclosure.

The flag tower on the southern wall facing the river is the landmark most often photographed from the far bank, and it is part of this outer ring rather than of the ticketed site.

Practical notes

Allow two to three hours. The site is large, largely unshaded, and the walking is on stone and gravel; in the hot months go early. There is a combined ticket covering the Imperial City together with several of the royal tombs, which is worth asking about at the gate if you plan to see them.

Dress is not formally restricted across the site, but the temple buildings are active places of commemoration and covered shoulders are appropriate inside them.

Getting the most out of it

This is a site that rewards knowing what you are looking at, because a great deal of it is absence — foundations, gateways to nothing, lawns where buildings stood. An audio guide or a hired guide fills that in, and the difference is larger here than at a site whose buildings are standing.

If you go without one, read the dynastic sequence first. The Nguyen emperors are the thread tying the Imperial City to the tombs outside the city, and the two make much more sense seen as one story than as two attractions.

What the restoration means for a visit

Expect a mixture. Some halls have been rebuilt to a high standard with lacquer, gilding and reconstructed roofs; others are stabilised shells; others are marked foundations in grass. Work is ongoing, so a building that was closed on one visit may be open on the next, and the reverse.

That unevenness is worth knowing in advance because it shapes expectations. This is not an intact palace complex, and visitors who arrive expecting one tend to be the ones who leave disappointed.

Combining it with the tombs

The Imperial City in the morning and one or two tombs in the afternoon is the standard full day in Hue, and it works. The tombs are in the countryside and need a car or a boat; the Imperial City is in town and is walked.

If you only have half a day, take the Imperial City. It is the centre of the story and it is the thing you cannot see anywhere else in Vietnam.

Photography and timing

The complex opens early and the light in the first hour is both better and cooler than later. Tour groups tend to arrive mid-morning and concentrate around Ngo Mon and the throne hall; the western temple compounds stay comparatively quiet.


----- ARTICLE: DMZ and Vinh Moc Tunnels Tour from Hue  (slug dmz-vinh-moc-tunnels-tour-from-hue)
EXCERPT: A long day north of Hue through the old demilitarised zone. What the stops are, and what the day actually asks of you.

The Demilitarised Zone was the strip either side of the seventeenth parallel that divided Vietnam from 1954 until 1975. It is about two hours north of Hue, and the day trip out to it is the main reason visitors give Hue an extra night.

What the day covers

Most tours run a circuit of several sites spread over a wide area. The usual stops are the Ben Hai river and the bridge at the old border, the Vinh Moc tunnels on the coast, the Khe Sanh combat base inland, and sometimes the Rockpile, the Dakrong bridge or a roadside memorial along Route 9.

The sites are far apart. A full-day tour out of Hue is a long one, often ten to twelve hours door to door, and a good part of that is in the vehicle.

Vinh Moc

The part of the day most visitors remember. Vinh Moc is a tunnel system on the coast where a village lived underground through years of bombardment — living chambers off the main passages, a maternity room, a well, and exits onto the beach.

What distinguishes it from the better-known tunnels near Ho Chi Minh City is that it was dug for civilians to live in rather than for fighters to move through, and it has not been widened for visitors. The passages are the size they were. There is a small museum at the entrance.

Going underground

The tunnels are confined, humid and dark, with lighting and a guided route. Anyone uneasy in enclosed spaces should know that before arriving; there is no obligation to go in, and the site above ground and the museum are worth the stop on their own.

Khe Sanh

Inland, towards the Laos border, the former US Marine combat base. There is a museum, a stretch of airstrip, and some restored and replica equipment in the open. The surrounding hills are the landscape the siege of 1968 was fought over.

Khe Sanh is the stop that divides opinion most. Some visitors find the setting and the emptiness powerful; others find a small museum and a field. If the base is specifically why you are going, say so when booking, because some itineraries give it half an hour.

The Ben Hai river

The old dividing line, with a reconstructed bridge, flag tower and museum on the northern bank. It is a short stop and mostly a marker — the significance is in the line itself rather than in what stands there now.

The Rockpile and Route 9

Route 9 runs west from the coast towards the Laos border and was the axis of much of the fighting in this area. The Rockpile, a steep outcrop used as an observation post, is a roadside stop rather than a site you enter. The Dakrong bridge marks a crossing point on one of the routes south.

These are short stops and their interest is almost entirely in what the ground was used for, which is why the guide matters.

Tours and guides

These sites are spread out, poorly served by public transport and thin on explanation. A guided tour is the practical way to do it, and the guide matters more here than at most places, because much of what you are looking at is terrain.

Options range from large group buses to private cars with a guide. A private car costs considerably more and lets you set the stops and the pace, which on a day with this much driving is worth weighing. Ask in advance which sites are included and how long is allowed at each.

What to bring and expect

A long day, a lot of road, and sites that are mostly outdoors. Bring water, sun protection and shoes for uneven ground, and a light layer for the tunnels. Lunch is usually included or stopped for; check which.

The material is difficult, and the presentation at these sites is Vietnamese and reflects that perspective. Visitors consistently report being received with ordinary courtesy at all of them.

Timing in the year

Most of the day is outdoors and much of the ground is unsurfaced. The storm season from September to November makes the tunnels wetter and the inland stops muddier, and a heavy day can cut the itinerary short. The hot dry months are easier underfoot but the Khe Sanh site in particular has very little shade.

Is it worth the day

It depends on what you want from it. If the war is a specific interest, this is the part of the country where it is on the ground rather than in a museum, and Vinh Moc in particular is unlike anything else you will see. If it is a general interest, the War Remnants Museum in Ho Chi Minh City covers the subject in a morning without the twelve hours of driving.

The common compromise is a half-day trip to Vinh Moc and the Ben Hai river only, skipping Khe Sanh and the inland stops. It cuts the driving substantially and keeps the stop most people come for.
