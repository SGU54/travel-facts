----- ARTICLE: Hoi An, An Bang or Da Nang: Where to Base Yourself  (slug hoian-vs-anbang-vs-danang-where-to-base)
EXCERPT: Three bases within twenty minutes of each other that produce three different trips. The choice is less about the places than about what your days look like.

Central Vietnam's best-known destinations sit unusually close together. Hoi An's Ancient Town, An Bang beach and the city of Da Nang are all within about forty-five minutes of each other, and most itineraries treat them as one stop. Which one you sleep in shapes the trip more than the short distances suggest.

Hoi An Ancient Town

Sleeping inside or on the edge of the old town means walking to everything: the lanterns at night, the tailors, breakfast, the riverside. The trade is that the Ancient Town is heavily visited and the central streets are busy from mid-morning until late.

It suits short stays and first visits, and anyone whose main reason for coming is the town itself. The best hours there are before eight and after nine, which is considerably easier if you are already in it.

An Bang and the beach

Ten to fifteen minutes by bicycle or Grab from the Ancient Town, and a different pace entirely — low-rise guesthouses and small resorts, a wide beach, beach restaurants. You get the old town as a destination you ride to rather than a place you live in.

It suits stays of three nights or more and anyone travelling with children. The cost is that an evening in the Ancient Town means arranging transport back, which tends to shorten the evenings.

Da Nang

A proper city: a long beachfront, high-rise hotels, the Han River, better nightlife, and an airport a few minutes from the centre. Hoi An is around forty-five minutes south by car.

It suits travellers who want a city rather than a town, anyone arriving late or leaving early by air, and longer stays where the variety matters. It is the wrong base for a two-night trip built around the Ancient Town, because you spend ninety minutes a day commuting to it.

What each base costs you in travel

The distances are the whole argument, so it is worth being concrete about them. Hoi An to An Bang is three to four kilometres, which is ten minutes by bicycle on flat road or a few minutes in a Grab. Hoi An to central Da Nang is roughly thirty kilometres and forty-five minutes by car. Da Nang's airport is about ten minutes from its own city centre and around an hour from Hoi An.

So a Da Nang base costs about ninety minutes of round trip every time you want the Ancient Town, and an An Bang base costs about twenty. A Hoi An base costs nothing to reach the town and twenty minutes to reach the beach. Which of those you would rather pay depends entirely on what you plan to do twice a day.

How to choose, practically

Two nights and the old town is the point: stay in Hoi An. Three or four nights with beach time: An Bang, and ride in. A week, a family, or an early flight: Da Nang, or split between Da Nang and Hoi An.

Season matters too. Between roughly September and November the central coast is in its storm season, and a beach base loses most of its advantage when the sea is rough and the sky is grey. In those months the Ancient Town, which works in any weather, is the safer choice.

The split option

Two nights in Hoi An and two in Da Nang is a common and sensible shape for four nights, and it costs you one transfer. It gives you the town at its quiet hours and a city with an airport at the end.

What is nearby from each

From Hoi An: the Ancient Town, Tra Que vegetable village, the Thu Bon river, and My Son Sanctuary about an hour west. From An Bang: the same list, plus the beach itself and the quieter stretch of coast north of it. From Da Nang: the Marble Mountains, the Son Tra peninsula and Linh Ung Pagoda, My Khe beach, the Ba Na Hills complex with the Golden Bridge, and the Hai Van Pass heading north.

That last list is longer, which is the honest case for Da Nang on a longer stay. The sights around Da Nang are more varied; the sights around Hoi An are more concentrated.

Getting between them

Grab operates in all three and the fares are low. Bicycles cover Hoi An to An Bang easily on flat roads. Hotels in Hoi An and An Bang generally run shuttles to the Ancient Town, which is worth asking about before booking, since it changes how easily you get to dinner.

What this choice is actually setting

Look at what follows from it. Your base decides what time of day you see the Ancient Town, which is the difference between the lantern-lit streets people photograph and the midday crowds they complain about. It decides whether the beach is somewhere you stay or somewhere you visit. And it decides how a storm day plays out — in Da Nang you have a city, on An Bang you have a closed beach.

None of those are visible when you are looking at three dots on a map twenty minutes apart. Working out which base serves the days you actually want is the kind of thing the complete Vietnam decision manual sets out.


----- ARTICLE: Hoi An Travel Guide  (slug hoi-an-travel-guide)
EXCERPT: An intact trading port, best seen at the hours nobody is there. What to do, when to do it, and what the ticket actually buys.

Hoi An was a major international port from the fifteenth to the nineteenth centuries, trading with Japanese, Chinese, Dutch and Portuguese merchants, and then silted up and stopped mattering — which is why the old town survived largely intact. It is now a UNESCO site and the most visited small town in Vietnam.

The ticket system

Entry to the Ancient Town area is managed by a ticket that admits you to a set number of heritage sites from a longer list — old merchant houses, assembly halls, the Japanese Covered Bridge, a museum or two. It is sold at booths around the perimeter.

Enforcement varies by hour and by entrance, which generates a great deal of confusion online. The straightforward position is that the ticket funds conservation of the town you came to see, it is inexpensive, and the heritage houses are worth visiting.

What to see with it

Tan Ky House and Phung Hung House are the two merchant homes that best show how these buildings worked — deep, narrow, built for flooding, with Japanese, Chinese and Vietnamese elements in the same roof. The Fujian Assembly Hall is the most elaborate of the Chinese community halls. The Japanese Covered Bridge is the town's emblem and appears on the 20,000 dong note.

The hours that matter

Before about eight in the morning the Ancient Town belongs to people sweeping, setting up and buying breakfast. From mid-morning to early evening it is busy. After around nine at night the day visitors have gone and the lanterns are lit.

If you remember one thing from this page, make it that. The town people describe as magical and the town people describe as a tourist trap are the same streets at different hours.

The lantern nights

On the fourteenth day of each lunar month the town turns off its electric lights and lights the old quarter with lanterns. It happens monthly, it is genuinely beautiful, and it is busy. Check which date it falls on for your trip.

Eating

Hoi An has dishes that exist here and essentially nowhere else. Cao lau is the signature — thick noodles, pork, greens and very little broth, traditionally made with water from specific local wells. White rose dumplings are the other. Banh mi in Hoi An has a serious claim to being the best in the country.

Com ga Hoi An, turmeric chicken rice, completes the set. All four are available within a few streets of each other and cost very little.

Shopping, and the pressure

Hoi An sells lanterns, leather, tailoring and art, and the central streets are a near-continuous retail frontage. Prices in shops with tickets on the goods are generally fixed; prices in the market and from street vendors are not, and bargaining is expected there.

The practical advice is the same as anywhere in Vietnam: agree the number before anything begins, and in dong rather than dollars. That removes most of the disputes people report, though it is not a guarantee against one.

Beyond the old town

An Bang beach is ten to fifteen minutes by bicycle. The rice paddies and vegetable village at Tra Que sit between the two and make a pleasant cycle. My Son Sanctuary, the Cham temple complex, is about an hour away and is the main half-day trip.

Renting a bicycle is the right way to do all of this — the land is flat, the distances are short, and most accommodation lends them free.

Tailoring

Hoi An's tailors are a genuine industry rather than a gimmick, and a lot of visitors leave with more clothing than they arrived with. It needs time: allow two fittings, which in practice means arriving with at least two full days left.

Getting there

Hoi An has no airport and no railway station. Everything arrives via Da Nang, which has both — the airport is around forty-five minutes away by car, and the train station a little further. Grab operates between the two, as do fixed-price hotel transfers and the local bus.

From Hue, the usual approach is the train to Da Nang along the coast and a car from there, or a private car over the Hai Van Pass. From Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City it is a flight to Da Nang; the overnight train also runs but takes most of a day.

When to go, and the flooding

February to August is the dry run and the period the beaches work. September to November is the central coast's storm season, and the Ancient Town floods in many years — the streets nearest the river can be navigable by boat.

That is worth planning around rather than fearing. Flooding is usually short-lived and the town carries on, but a trip built on beach days in October is a gamble. Where to base yourself given all of that is covered in the complete Vietnam decision manual.


----- ARTICLE: Hoi An Tailors: How to Order Clothes That Actually Fit  (slug hoi-an-tailors-how-to-order)
EXCERPT: The industry is real and so is the variance. What to bring, how long it takes, and the questions that separate a good result from a bad one.

Hoi An has hundreds of tailoring shops and a long history of making clothes quickly. The results range from genuinely excellent to disappointing, and the difference is mostly down to time, fabric and how clearly you communicate — all of which are within your control.

Allow enough time

The single biggest determinant of a good outcome. A shop that promises a suit overnight can usually produce one, and the result is generally not as good as the same shop's work over three days with two fittings.

Plan on a minimum of two full days in town, ordering on the first morning. Three is better. If your itinerary gives you one night in Hoi An, this is not the trip to have clothes made on.

What two fittings actually do

The first garment off the machine is close but rarely right — a shoulder sits oddly, a sleeve is long, a waist needs taking in. The fitting is where that gets corrected, and a second fitting catches what the first one introduced. Shops that skip this stage are the ones whose output disappoints.

Bring a reference garment

The most useful thing you can do. A jacket, a pair of trousers or a dress that already fits you well gives the tailor a physical specification rather than a verbal one, and removes most of the ambiguity about cut and fit.

Photographs help for style, less for fit. Bring both if you can: the garment for how it should sit, the picture for how it should look.

Fabric is where the price sits

The quoted price of a suit varies enormously, and most of that variation is cloth rather than labour. Ask what the fabric is — wool, wool blend, linen, synthetic — and ask to see and feel the bolt rather than a swatch card.

A cheap suit in a synthetic blend will look like one, and will be uncomfortable in Vietnamese heat. Paying more for a natural fibre is the upgrade that shows.

Questions worth asking

What is the fabric and where is it from? Is the jacket fully canvassed, half canvassed or fused? How many fittings are included? Is the work done on site or sent out? What happens if it still does not fit at the final fitting?

A shop that answers those plainly is telling you something useful. A shop that deflects to how quickly it can be done is telling you something too.

Agreeing the price and the terms

Settle the full price before any measuring happens, in dong, and establish what it covers: the fabric, the lining, the fittings, any alterations after the first fitting. Most shops take a deposit and the balance on collection, which is reasonable; paying the whole amount up front removes your only leverage if the result is wrong.

Get the collection date in writing alongside the price. A shop that is vague about when the garment will be ready is a shop you may be chasing on your last morning.

Choosing a shop

Recent reviews matter more than longevity, because quality in this industry tracks the current staff. Walk in and look at the work in progress rather than the finished display pieces. Ask who will actually be sewing your garment.

Many accommodations have a tailor they recommend, which is usually a commission arrangement. That does not make it a bad shop — it does mean the recommendation is not disinterested.

What to do at the fitting

Stand normally rather than holding yourself straight, and move — sit down in the trousers, raise your arms in the jacket, reach forward. Fit problems show up in motion and hide in a mirror.

Say precisely what is wrong rather than that something feels off: the sleeve covers my knuckles, the waistband sits above my hip, the shoulder pulls when I reach. Tailors can act on that. They cannot act on "it doesn't look right". And if it is still wrong at the second fitting, say so then — not after you have paid and left.

What travels well

Suits, shirts, dresses and coats are the usual orders. Lightweight linen and cotton suits are a Hoi An speciality and genuinely useful if you live somewhere warm. Heavy winter coats are made here too and are harder to assess in thirty-degree heat.

Shoes are also made to order in Hoi An. The same rules apply, with the added caution that leather needs breaking in and you will not discover a fit problem in a single fitting.

Getting it home

Most shops will ship, and most will quote a price for it. Factor in that your own country's customs allowance applies on arrival, and that a large order may be dutiable at your end regardless of what you paid in Vietnam.

Managing expectations

What you are buying is competent made-to-measure at a low price, produced fast. It is not Savile Row and it is not sold as such. Visitors who go in expecting the former are the ones who are disappointed; visitors who expect a well-fitting linen suit for a fraction of the price at home generally are not.


----- ARTICLE: My Son Sanctuary: What to Know Before You Go  (slug my-son-sanctuary-guide)
EXCERPT: Cham temple ruins in a jungle valley, bombed during the war and never fully restored. Go at opening or not at all.

My Son is a complex of Hindu temple ruins in a valley about an hour west of Hoi An, built by the Cham civilisation between roughly the fourth and thirteenth centuries. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site and the most significant Cham site in Vietnam.

What you are looking at

The Cham kingdom controlled central Vietnam for centuries and was Hindu rather than Buddhist, which is why the towers here resemble temples in India and Cambodia more than anything else in the country. They were built of fired brick, set with a binding method that is still not fully understood, and dedicated largely to Shiva.

Groups of towers are labelled by letter. Group B, C and D are the best preserved and the usual focus of a visit; Group A was largely destroyed.

The war

The valley was used as a base during the war and was bombed heavily, and a number of the towers were destroyed outright. Bomb craters remain visible on the site. Restoration has been partial and deliberately conservative, so much of what you see is ruin rather than reconstruction.

Go early

This is the practical heart of it. The site opens early, the coaches from Hoi An and Da Nang arrive mid-morning, and the valley gets hot and crowded by eleven. A dawn or near-dawn arrival gives you cooler air, better light and considerably fewer people.

Sunrise tours exist for exactly this reason and are worth the early alarm. The alternative is late afternoon, when the coaches have gone.

Getting there

About an hour from Hoi An by road. Options are a group tour, a private car with a driver, or riding — though a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, which makes self-riding illegal and a problem for your insurance.

Some tours return by boat along the Thu Bon river, which adds time and is more pleasant than the minibus. Entry is ticketed separately from most tour prices; check which applies.

What a visit involves

An electric buggy runs from the ticket area to the temple groups, then it is walking on uneven ground between them. Allow two to three hours including the travel on site. There is a small museum near the entrance that is worth ten minutes before you walk in.

Reading the site without a guide

Unguided, My Son is a set of brick towers with limited signage, and much of what makes it interesting is not visible. A guide, whether hired at the entrance or included in a tour, is unusually worthwhile here — more so than at sites that explain themselves.

If you go without one, the things to look for are the brickwork itself, laid with almost no visible mortar; the sandstone lintels and pedestals, many of them carved; the kalan or main sanctuary tower of each group, square with a tapering roof; and the linga and yoni stones that were the focus of worship. The Cham sculpture museum in Da Nang holds much of the statuary removed from here, and visiting it either before or after changes what you see.

Practicalities

There is very little shade in the valley and it gets genuinely hot. Bring water, a hat and sunscreen, and wear shoes you can walk on broken ground in. Insect repellent is sensible.

A traditional Cham dance performance runs at set times near the entrance. It is short and clearly staged for visitors; some enjoy it, others walk past.

Is it worth it

If you have been to Angkor, My Son will feel small — it is a fraction of the scale and much of it is rubble. If you have not, it is an atmospheric and genuinely ancient site in a beautiful valley, and the damage is part of what it has to say.

Either way the early start is what makes the difference. My Son at seven in the morning and My Son at eleven are different experiences, and most of the complaints about the site come from people who saw the second one.

Tour or private car

Group tours from Hoi An are inexpensive and run on a fixed schedule, which usually means arriving with everyone else unless you specifically book the sunrise departure. A private car costs more and lets you set the hour, which on this particular site is the thing most worth paying for.

If you book a group tour, ask two questions: what time it leaves Hoi An, and whether the entry ticket is included. A tour departing at eight arrives in the heat and the crowd; a tour departing at five does not.

Combining it

It pairs naturally with a slow afternoon back in Hoi An rather than with anything else demanding. Tours that bundle My Son with the Marble Mountains and a Da Nang city tour are moving you constantly and give each site about forty minutes.


----- ARTICLE: Hai Van Pass: How to Cross It  (slug hai-van-pass-guide)
EXCERPT: The coastal mountain road between Hue and Da Nang, and the four ways to travel it. One of them is illegal for most visitors.

The Hai Van Pass climbs over a spur of the Annamite range between Hue and Da Nang, with the sea on one side and mountains on the other. It is the most celebrated stretch of road in Vietnam and you will cross between those two cities anyway, so the only real question is how.

What is actually up there

The pass tops out at around 500 metres at the Hai Van Gate, a stone fortification that has guarded the route for centuries and carries French and American-era bunkers from more recent use. The view back along the coast on a clear day is the reason people stop.

Weather matters more here than the altitude suggests. The pass is a genuine climatic boundary between the cooler north and warmer south, and it is frequently in cloud when both cities are clear.

The tunnel

Since 2005 a tunnel has run beneath the pass, and most traffic uses it. It is faster, it is closed to motorbikes, and it sees none of the view. Buses and many private cars take it by default — so if you want the pass, say so when you book.

By train

The railway hugs the coast below the road and takes about two and a half to three hours between Hue and Da Nang. It costs very little, it goes where cars cannot, and it is widely considered the best rail hour in the country. Sit on the right-hand side heading south.

For most travellers this is the answer: it replaces a transfer you were making anyway, and the views from the track are arguably better than from the road.

By car with a driver

A private car over the pass, with stops, takes three to four hours and lets you get out at the top and at the beaches on either side. Many drivers will take your luggage directly between hotels, which makes it a transfer and a day out at once.

This is the most flexible option and the one that suits families, groups and anyone who wants photographs without riding.

By motorbike, and the legal problem

The pass is famous as a ride, and the easy-rider model — riding pillion behind an experienced local while your bag goes by van — is how a lot of people do it. That version is straightforward and safe enough.

Riding it yourself is the problem. A US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, driving without a Vietnamese licence can mean a fine, arrest, or jail, and your travel insurance may decline a claim after an accident. The road is also genuinely demanding — switchbacks, trucks, sudden cloud and wet surfaces.

If you ride pillion

Easy-rider operators run the route daily in both directions and will usually stop wherever you ask. Agree in advance what is included — the bag transfer, lunch, the stops — and expect the journey to take most of a day rather than the three hours the distance suggests.

How long it takes

Da Nang to Hue is about 100 kilometres. Through the tunnel it is roughly two hours by car. Over the pass without stopping it is closer to two and a half. With stops at the summit and a beach, allow three to four hours, and a full easy-rider day will take five or six because the stopping is the point.

The train is two and a half to three hours and does not depend on traffic. Plan the day around whichever you pick rather than treating any of them as a quick hop — this is the leg where itineraries most often run short.

What to stop for

The summit is the main stop: the old gate, the bunkers, the stalls selling drinks, and the view in both directions when the cloud allows. Heading south, Lang Co beach and lagoon sit at the northern foot of the pass and are worth a stop in their own right. Heading north out of Da Nang, the Son Tra peninsula and Linh Ung Pagoda are a short detour before the climb begins.

Expect to be approached at the summit by vendors. Expect also that the view may simply not be there — the pass makes its own weather, and a grey crossing is a common outcome rather than bad luck.

Timing and weather

Mornings are generally clearer than afternoons, and the dry months from roughly February to August are clearer than the storm season from September to November. In the wet months the road can be in cloud, wet and slick, which is both less scenic and more demanding to ride.

If the pass is the reason you are making this journey and the forecast is poor, the train is the better fallback — it runs below the cloud line, along the coast, and delivers a view in weather that closes the road view entirely.

Which to choose

Train if you want the best views for the least money and effort. Car with driver if you want to stop, or have luggage and company. Easy-rider if the road itself is why you came. The tunnel if you are in a hurry and do not mind missing it.

Whichever you pick, the pass sits inside the wider question of how you cover Vietnam's long legs, which the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.
