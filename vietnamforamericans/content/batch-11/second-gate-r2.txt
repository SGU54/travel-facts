----- ARTICLE: Hoi An, An Bang or Da Nang: Where to Base Yourself  (slug hoian-vs-anbang-vs-danang-where-to-base)
EXCERPT: Three bases inside an hour of each other that produce three different trips. The choice is less about the places than about what your days look like.

Central Vietnam's best-known destinations sit close together. Hoi An's Ancient Town, An Bang beach and the city of Da Nang are all within about an hour of each other by road, which makes it easy to treat them as one stop. Which one you sleep in shapes the trip more than those distances suggest.

Hoi An Ancient Town

Sleeping inside or on the edge of the old town means walking to everything: the lanterns at night, the tailors, breakfast, the riverside. The trade is that the Ancient Town is heavily visited, and the central streets are busy from mid-morning until the evening.

It suits short stays and first visits, and anyone whose main reason for coming is the town itself. The quietest hours are early and late, which is considerably easier to use if you are already inside the town.

An Bang and the beach

A short ride from the Ancient Town — ten to fifteen minutes by bicycle, less in a Grab — and a different pace entirely: low-rise guesthouses and small resorts, a wide beach, beach restaurants. You get the old town as a destination you ride to rather than a place you live in.

It suits stays of three nights or more and anyone travelling with children. The cost is that an evening in the Ancient Town means arranging transport back.

Da Nang

A city rather than a town: a long beachfront, high-rise hotels, the Han River, a city's range of bars and restaurants, and an airport close to the centre. Hoi An is around forty-five minutes south by car.

It suits travellers who want a city, anyone arriving late or leaving early by air, and longer stays where the variety matters. It is the wrong base for a two-night trip built around the Ancient Town, because a large part of each day goes on the road.

What each base costs you in travel

The distances are the whole argument, so it is worth being concrete. Hoi An to An Bang is a few kilometres of flat road — ten to fifteen minutes by bicycle, less in a Grab. Hoi An to central Da Nang is roughly thirty kilometres and about forty-five minutes by car. Da Nang's airport sits close to its own city centre and around an hour from Hoi An.

So a Da Nang base puts most of an hour and a half on the road each time you want the Ancient Town and come back. An Bang puts about half an hour. A Hoi An base costs you nothing to reach the town and the same half hour to reach the beach. Which of those you would rather pay is the choice.

How to choose, practically

Two nights and the old town is the point: stay in Hoi An. Three or four nights with beach time: An Bang, and ride in. A week, a family, or an early flight: Da Nang, or split between Da Nang and Hoi An.

Season matters too. Between roughly September and November the central coast is in its storm season, and a beach base loses much of its advantage when the sea is rough and the sky is grey. The Ancient Town is less dependent on the weather, though it is not immune to it — the streets nearest the river flood in some years.

The split option

Two nights in Hoi An and two in Da Nang is a workable shape for four nights, and it costs you one transfer. It gives you the town at its quiet hours and a city with an airport at the end.

What is nearby from each

From Hoi An: the Ancient Town, Tra Que vegetable village, the Thu Bon river, and My Son Sanctuary roughly an hour west. From An Bang: the same list, plus the beach itself and the quieter coast north of it. From Da Nang: the Marble Mountains, the Son Tra peninsula and Linh Ung Pagoda, My Khe beach, the Ba Na Hills complex with the Golden Bridge, and the Hai Van Pass heading north.

That last list is the longer one, which is the honest case for Da Nang on a longer stay. The list around Hoi An is shorter and tighter together.

Getting between them

Grab operates in all three. Bicycles cover Hoi An to An Bang easily on flat roads. Some hotels in Hoi An and An Bang run shuttles to the Ancient Town — worth asking about before booking, since it changes how easily you get to dinner.

What this choice is actually setting

Look at what follows from it. Your base shapes how easily you can be in the Ancient Town early or late, which is the difference between the lantern-lit streets in the photographs and the middle of a busy day. It sets whether the beach is somewhere you stay or somewhere you visit. And it sets what a storm day looks like — in Da Nang there is a city to spend it in, on An Bang there is not much but the wait.

None of that is visible when you are looking at three dots sitting close together on a map. Working out which base serves the days you actually want is the kind of thing the complete Vietnam decision manual sets out.


----- ARTICLE: Hoi An Travel Guide  (slug hoi-an-travel-guide)
EXCERPT: An intact trading port, best seen at its quietest hours. What to do, when to do it, and what to ask about the ticket.

Hoi An was an international trading port for several centuries, dealing with Japanese, Chinese, Dutch and Portuguese merchants, before its river silted and the trade moved elsewhere. The old town survived largely intact. It is now a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of the most visited places in Vietnam.

The ticket system

Entry to the Ancient Town area is managed by a ticket that admits you to a set number of heritage sites chosen from a longer list — old merchant houses, assembly halls, the Japanese Covered Bridge, a museum or two. It is sold at booths around the perimeter of the old town.

Two things are worth asking at the booth, because both are reported differently by different visitors: how many sites your ticket covers, and how long it stays valid. Enforcement at the entrances also varies, which is the source of most of the confusion you will read online. The ticket is inexpensive, it funds conservation, and the heritage houses are worth going into.

What to see with it

Tan Ky House and Phung Hung House are two merchant homes that show how these buildings worked — deep, narrow plots, internal courtyards, and Japanese, Chinese and Vietnamese elements in the same structure. The Fujian Assembly Hall is a Chinese community hall with elaborate decoration. The Japanese Covered Bridge is the town's emblem.

The hours that matter

Early in the morning the Ancient Town belongs to people sweeping, setting up and buying breakfast. Through the middle of the day and into the evening it is busy. Late at night the day visitors have thinned out and the lanterns are lit.

If you remember one thing from this page, make it that. Hoi An gets described both as magical and as a tourist trap, and those descriptions are of the same streets at different hours.

The lantern nights

On the fourteenth day of the lunar month the old quarter dims its electric lighting and is lit by lanterns instead. It is beautiful and it draws a crowd. Check which date it falls on for your trip.

Eating

Hoi An has dishes strongly associated with the town and hard to find elsewhere. Cao lau is the signature — thick noodles, pork, greens and very little broth, said locally to require water from particular wells in the town. White rose dumplings are the other. Banh mi is everywhere here and cheap.

Com ga Hoi An, turmeric chicken rice, completes the set. All four are sold within a few streets of each other.

Shopping, and the pressure

Hoi An sells lanterns, leather, tailoring and art, and the central streets are a near-continuous retail frontage. Shops with prices marked on the goods generally hold to them; in the market and with street vendors, bargaining is expected.

The practical advice is the same as anywhere in Vietnam: settle the number before anything begins, and in dong rather than dollars. That is how you avoid an argument at the end.

Beyond the old town

An Bang beach is ten to fifteen minutes by bicycle. The rice paddies and vegetable village at Tra Que sit between the two and make a pleasant cycle. My Son Sanctuary, the Cham temple complex, is roughly an hour away by road and is the usual half-day trip.

For the beach and Tra Que, a bicycle is the right way to do it — the land is flat and the distances are short. Accommodation often lends them; ask. My Son is a car or tour trip, not a cycle.

Tailoring

Hoi An's tailors are a real industry rather than a gimmick. It needs time: allow for two fittings, which in practice means not leaving the order to your last day.

When to go, and the flooding

Roughly February to August is the drier stretch and the period the beaches work best. September to November is the central coast's storm season, and the streets of Hoi An nearest the river flood in some years — in a bad one they can be navigable by boat.

That is worth planning around rather than fearing. The town carries on through most of it, but a trip built on beach days in October is a gamble.

Getting there

Hoi An has no airport and no railway station. Most arrivals come via Da Nang, which has both — the airport is around forty-five minutes away by car, the train station a little further. Grab runs between the two, as do hotel transfers and a local bus.

From Hue, the two usual approaches are the coastal train to Da Nang and a car from there, or a private car the whole way over the Hai Van Pass. From Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City it is a flight to Da Nang; trains run too, and take far longer.


----- ARTICLE: Hoi An Tailors: How to Order Clothes That Actually Fit  (slug hoi-an-tailors-how-to-order)
EXCERPT: The industry is real and so is the variance. What to bring, how long it takes, and the questions that separate a good result from a bad one.

Hoi An's old town is full of tailoring shops, and clothes are made here fast. The results range from genuinely excellent to disappointing. Time, fabric and how clearly you communicate are the parts of that you can influence.

Allow enough time

This is the part most within your control. Overnight turnarounds are advertised and shops can produce them, but rushing leaves no room for the fittings that correct the fit.

Plan on a minimum of two full days in town, ordering on the first morning. Three is better. If your itinerary gives you one night in Hoi An, this is not the trip to have clothes made on.

What two fittings actually do

The first garment off the machine is usually close rather than right — a shoulder sits oddly, a sleeve is long, a waist needs taking in. The fitting is where that gets corrected, and a second fitting catches what the first one introduced. A shop that does not offer a fitting has no way to fix what the first attempt gets wrong.

Bring a reference garment

A useful thing to do. A jacket, a pair of trousers or a dress that already fits you well gives the tailor a physical specification rather than a verbal one, and removes a lot of the ambiguity about cut and fit.

Photographs help for style, less for fit. Bring both if you can: the garment for how it should sit, the picture for how it should look.

Fabric is where the price sits

Two quotes for a suit are not comparable until you know what cloth each one is for. Ask what the fabric is — wool, wool blend, linen, synthetic — and ask to see and feel the bolt rather than a swatch card.

Synthetic blends are cheaper and warmer to wear, which matters in the Vietnamese climate. Natural fibres cost more.

Questions worth asking

What is the fabric and where is it from? Is the jacket fused, half canvassed or fully canvassed — that is, is the front held in shape by glued interlining or by a stitched-in layer, the second being more durable and more expensive? How many fittings are included? Is the work done on site or sent out? What happens if it still does not fit at the final fitting?

A shop that answers those plainly is telling you something useful. A shop that deflects to how quickly it can be done is telling you something too.

Agreeing the price and the terms

Settle the full price before any measuring happens, in dong, and establish what it covers: the fabric, the lining, the fittings, any alterations after the first fitting. A deposit now and the balance on collection is a normal arrangement; if you pay the whole amount up front you have less leverage should the result be wrong.

Get the collection date alongside the price. A shop that is vague about when the garment will be ready is a shop you may be chasing on your last morning.

Choosing a shop

Read recent reviews rather than relying on how long a shop has been there — a reputation from five years ago is not evidence about the work being done this week. Walk in and look at the work in progress rather than the finished display pieces. Ask who will actually be sewing your garment.

If your accommodation recommends a tailor, ask whether they receive a commission. That does not make it a bad shop; it does mean the recommendation may not be disinterested.

What to do at the fitting

Stand normally rather than holding yourself straight, and move — sit down in the trousers, raise your arms in the jacket, reach forward. Fit problems show up in motion.

Say precisely what is wrong rather than that something feels off: the sleeve covers my knuckles, the waistband sits above my hip, the shoulder pulls when I reach. A tailor can act on that. "It doesn't look right" gives them nothing to change. And if it is still wrong at the second fitting, say so then, not after you have paid and left.

What travels well

Suits, shirts, dresses and coats are all made here. Lightweight linen and cotton suits are a Hoi An staple and genuinely useful if you live somewhere warm. Heavy winter coats are made too, and are harder to judge in the heat.

Shoes are also made to order. The same rules apply, with the added caution that leather needs breaking in and a single fitting may not reveal a fit problem.

Getting it home

Many shops offer shipping and will quote for it. Factor in that your own country's customs rules apply when the clothes arrive, and that a large order may be dutiable at your end.

Managing expectations

What is on offer is made-to-measure at a low price, produced fast, with quality that varies by shop. It is not Savile Row. Going in expecting the latter is the way to be disappointed; expecting a well-fitting linen suit for a good deal less than the equivalent would cost at home is realistic.


----- ARTICLE: My Son Sanctuary: What to Know Before You Go  (slug my-son-sanctuary-guide)
EXCERPT: Cham temple ruins in a jungle valley, bombed during the war and never fully restored. Go early or late — the middle of the day is the problem.

My Son is a complex of Hindu temple ruins in a valley roughly an hour west of Hoi An, built by the Cham over many centuries. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site and the Cham temple complex most visitors to central Vietnam see.

What you are looking at

The Cham kingdom held much of central Vietnam for a long period and was Hindu rather than Buddhist, and the architecture reflects that — the towers read closer to Indian and Khmer temple building than to a Vietnamese pagoda. They are built of fired brick, laid with almost no visible mortar, and the complex was dedicated largely to Shiva.

Groups of towers are labelled by letter. The groups usually pointed out to visitors are B, C and D, which are the better preserved; Group A is largely gone.

The war

The valley was used militarily during the war and was bombed, and a number of towers were destroyed. Craters remain visible on the site. Restoration has been partial, so much of what you see is ruin rather than reconstruction.

Go early

This is the practical heart of it. The site opens early, the coaches from Hoi An and Da Nang arrive through the morning, and the valley is hot and busy by the middle of the day. An early arrival gives you cooler air, better light and fewer people.

Sunrise departures are offered for exactly that reason. The alternative is late afternoon, once the coaches have gone. The middle of the day is the part to avoid.

Getting there

Roughly an hour from Hoi An by road. Options are a group tour, a private car with a driver, or riding — though a US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, driving without a Vietnamese licence can mean a fine, arrest, or jail, and your insurer may decline a claim after an accident.

Some tours return by boat along the Thu Bon river, which adds time. Entry is often ticketed separately from the tour price; ask which applies before you book.

What a visit involves

An electric buggy runs from the ticket area towards the temple groups, and from there it is walking on uneven ground between them. Allow two to three hours including the moving around. There is a small museum near the entrance worth ten minutes before you walk in.

Reading the site without a guide

Unguided, My Son is a set of brick towers with limited signage, and a good deal of what makes it interesting is not self-evident. A guide, hired at the entrance or included in a tour, is more worthwhile here than at sites that explain themselves.

If you go without one, the things to look for are the brickwork, laid with almost no mortar line; the sandstone lintels and pedestals, many of them carved; the kalan, or main sanctuary tower, at the centre of each group; and the linga and yoni stones that were the focus of worship. The Cham sculpture museum in Da Nang holds a large part of the statuary removed from here, and visiting it before or after changes what you see.

Practicalities

There is little shade in the valley and it gets hot. Bring water, a hat and sunscreen, and wear shoes you can walk on broken ground in. Insect repellent is sensible.

A Cham dance performance is staged near the entrance at set times. It is short and clearly presented for visitors; check the schedule if you want it.

When to go in the year

The valley is in the open and the ground between the groups is uneven, so the weather shapes a visit more than it does in a town. The drier stretch, roughly February to August, is hot but straightforward. September to November is the central coast's storm season, when the paths are muddy and a downpour can shorten the visit.

Is it worth it

My Son is far smaller than Angkor, and much of it is rubble, so if you have been to Cambodia it will feel modest. If you have not, it is an atmospheric and genuinely ancient site in a beautiful valley, and the damage is part of what it has to say.

Either way the early start is what makes the difference. My Son first thing and My Son in the middle of the day are different experiences.

Tour or private car

Group tours from Hoi An run on a fixed schedule, which usually means arriving when the other coaches do unless you specifically book the sunrise departure. A private car costs more and lets you set the hour, which on this site is the thing most worth paying for.

If you book a group tour, ask two questions: what time it leaves Hoi An, and whether the entry ticket is included. The departure time is what decides whether you arrive before or after the coaches.

Combining it

It pairs naturally with a slow afternoon back in Hoi An rather than with anything else demanding. Tours that bundle My Son with the Marble Mountains and a Da Nang city tour move you constantly and leave little time at each stop.


----- ARTICLE: Hai Van Pass: How to Cross It  (slug hai-van-pass-guide)
EXCERPT: The coastal mountain road between Hue and Da Nang, and the four ways to travel it. One of them you cannot legally do yourself.

The Hai Van Pass climbs over a spur of mountains between Hue and Da Nang, with the sea on one side. It is among the best-known stretches of road in Vietnam, and if you are travelling between those two cities you will cross it one way or another — so the question is how.

What is actually up there

The road tops out at the Hai Van Gate, an old stone fortification that guarded the route, with later military structures around it. The view back along the coast on a clear day is why people stop.

Weather matters more here than the height suggests. The pass makes its own weather and can be sitting in cloud when both cities are clear.

The tunnel

A road tunnel runs beneath the pass. It is faster, motorbikes are not permitted through it, and it sees none of the view. Much through traffic uses it by default — so if you want the pass, say so when you book.

By train

The railway runs along the coast below the road between Hue and Da Nang; allow around three hours. It costs very little, it goes where the road cannot, and it is one of the best rail stretches in Southeast Asia. Ask for a seat on the sea side when you book.

This is the simplest answer for most itineraries: it replaces a transfer you were likely making anyway, and the views from the track are arguably better than from the road.

By car with a driver

A private car over the pass, with stops, is a half day rather than a hop, and it lets you get out at the top and at the beaches on either side. Many drivers will carry your luggage directly between hotels, which makes it a transfer and a day out at once.

It is the option that gives you the most control over the day, and the one that suits families, groups, and anyone who wants the photographs without riding.

By motorbike, and the legal problem

The pass is well known as a ride, and the easy-rider model — riding pillion behind an experienced local while your bag goes by van — is a common way to do it. Riding pillion removes the licence problem. It does not remove the road risk: this is a demanding road with switchbacks, trucks, sudden cloud and wet surfaces, and road traffic is the main hazard for visitors to Vietnam.

Riding it yourself is the other problem. A US licence and an International Driving Permit are both invalid in Vietnam, driving without a Vietnamese licence can mean a fine, arrest, or jail, and your travel insurance may decline a claim after an accident.

If you ride pillion

Easy-rider operators run the route in both directions. Agree in advance what is included — the bag transfer, lunch, the stops — and expect the day to be longer than the distance suggests, because the stopping is the point.

How long it takes

Da Nang to Hue is about 100 kilometres. Through the tunnel, allow around two hours by car. Over the pass without stopping, allow a little more. With stops at the summit and a beach it becomes a half day, and an easy-rider trip takes most of one.

The train runs to a timetable and is not held up by road traffic. Plan the day around whichever you pick rather than treating any of them as a quick hop.

What to stop for

The summit is the main stop: the old gate, the bunkers, the stalls selling drinks, and the view in both directions when the cloud allows. Heading south, Lang Co beach and its lagoon sit at the northern foot of the pass and are worth stopping for. Heading north out of Da Nang, the Son Tra peninsula and Linh Ung Pagoda are a detour before the climb begins.

Expect to be approached by vendors at the summit. Expect also that the view may simply not be there — a grey crossing is a real possibility rather than bad luck.

Which to choose

Train if you want the cheapest and easiest version. Car with driver if you want to stop, or have luggage and company. Easy-rider if the road itself is why you came. The tunnel if you are in a hurry and do not mind missing it.

Timing and weather

September to November is the central coast's storm season, and it is the worst stretch for this crossing — cloud on the pass, wet and slick road surfaces, and a view that may not appear at all.

If the pass is the reason you are making this journey and the forecast is poor, the train is the fallback. It runs lower and closer to the sea, and it is not affected by the road sitting in cloud.

How you cover Vietnam's other long legs is a separate question, and one the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.
