--- best-time-to-visit-vietnam | handoff=none | swamp=none ---
TITLE: Best Time to Visit Vietnam
EXCERPT: There is no single best month for Vietnam. The country spans three climates at once, and the right answer depends on which third you are visiting.

Vietnam runs about a thousand miles from north to south, and that length is the whole answer to this question. At any given moment the country is running three different climates, so a month that is perfect in one region can be the worst of the year in another. The useful question is not when to visit Vietnam but which Vietnam you are visiting.

The short answer, if you want one
March and April are the best single compromise across the whole country. The north has warmed up and dried out, the centre is in its reliable dry run, and the south is still in its dry season. Prices sit in shoulder territory and the crowds are manageable. If you are flying from the United States and can only choose one window for a two-week trip that touches all three regions, this is it.
October and November are the other strong window, and in the north they are arguably better than spring: clear air, comfortable temperatures, and the Sapa rice terraces at their most photogenic. The catch is the centre, and it is a real one — see below before you commit.

Why most articles get this wrong
Search for this question and you will get a single month as an answer. That only works if your trip stays in one region. The moment your itinerary includes both Hanoi and Hoi An, you are trading one region's best weather against another's worst, and the trade is the actual decision.

The north: four real seasons
Hanoi, Ha Long Bay, Ninh Binh and Sapa get a genuine winter. From December to February expect temperatures in the 50s and 60s Fahrenheit with a persistent grey drizzle the Vietnamese call mua phun — not heavy rain, but damp, overcast and colder than almost any American expects of Southeast Asia. Sapa can approach freezing and occasionally sees snow.
Summer runs hot and wet: June through August brings the 90s with high humidity and the year's heaviest rainfall. The shoulders are the sweet spots. October and November are reliably clear and mild, and March and April warm steadily without the summer downpours.

The air-quality caveat nobody mentions
The State Department notes that air pollution is a significant problem in Vietnam’s major cities, especially Hanoi, and the worst stretch overlaps the cool dry months that are otherwise the north's best — roughly November through March, when still air traps pollution over the Red River delta. That is not a reason to avoid Hanoi in winter, but if you are asthmatic or travelling with someone who is, it belongs in the decision. Check a live air-quality reading rather than a figure printed in any article, including this one.

The centre: a short, violent wet season
Hue, Da Nang and Hoi An are driest from February to August, and that is when their beaches work. The complication is September to November, which is typhoon season on this coast. Hoi An's old town floods in most years, boat trips get cancelled at short notice, and Hue is the wettest major city in the country during this window.
This is the single biggest planning conflict in Vietnam. October and November are excellent in Hanoi and poor in Hoi An. If your trip spans both and must happen in autumn, put the centre early in the itinerary and keep a day of slack, or accept that you may lose a beach day to weather.

The south: wet and dry, not hot and cold
Ho Chi Minh City, the Mekong Delta and Phu Quoc are warm year-round, typically in the 80s and 90s, and the seasons are defined by rain rather than temperature. Dry season runs December to April. Wet season runs May to November, and it is less disruptive than it sounds: the pattern is a heavy afternoon downpour for an hour or two rather than all-day rain, and mornings are usually clear.
Travelling the south in the wet season is a reasonable trade for lower prices and thinner crowds, provided you plan activities for the morning. Phu Quoc is the exception where the sea gets rough enough to matter for boat trips.

Festival timing as a reason, not an obstacle
Two dates are worth bending an itinerary for. The Mid-Autumn Festival, usually in September, is at its best in Hoi An, where the old town is already lantern-lit and the festival amplifies it. The rice harvest in the northern terraces falls around September and October, which is when Sapa and Ha Giang look the way they do in photographs. Both sit inside windows where the regional weather happens to cooperate.

When not to go
Tet, the Lunar New Year, is the one period that can genuinely derail a trip. The dates move with the lunar calendar — in 2027 Tet falls on 6 February, with the public-sector holiday announced as 4 to 10 February — and the disruption runs wider than the official week. Domestic flights and trains sell out months ahead at multiples of normal fares, many family-run businesses close for several days, and cities empty while the countryside fills. Always verify the dates for your own year before booking.
The other date to know is the Reunification Day and Labour Day pair at the end of April, when Vietnamese travellers fill the beaches and hill towns and prices spike for a long weekend whose exact shape is set by government announcement each year.

TITLE: Vietnam Weather by Region and Month
EXCERPT: A month-by-month read of Vietnam's three climate zones, so you can see where the good weather actually is when you want to travel.

Vietnam's three climate zones rarely line up, so the practical way to read its weather is month by month and region by region rather than as a single national season. What follows is that grid, written for someone deciding when to fly rather than someone studying meteorology.

December to February: cold north, dry south
The north is cool and damp — Hanoi in the 50s and 60s Fahrenheit, grey more often than not, with a fine persistent drizzle. Sapa is genuinely cold and the mountain passes can be fogged in. Pack layers and a waterproof shell, which first-timers heading to Southeast Asia almost never do.
The centre is transitional and improving, with Hue and Hoi An drying out through January. The south is at its best: Ho Chi Minh City, the Mekong and Phu Quoc sit in the heart of the dry season with warm days, low humidity by local standards and reliable sun. This is peak season in the south and prices follow.

The Tet exception
Whatever the weather is doing, Lunar New Year overrides it as a planning factor. In 2027 Tet falls on 6 February with a public-sector holiday announced for 4 to 10 February, and the travel surge runs well past the official days. Check the dates for your year before fixing anything in February.

March to April: the national sweet spot
This is the window where all three regions are simultaneously good. The north warms into the 70s and sheds the winter drizzle, the centre is firmly dry with beach weather arriving, and the south is still dry before the May rains. Humidity is tolerable everywhere and the light is clear.
If your itinerary covers Hanoi, the central coast and Ho Chi Minh City in one trip, this is the period that asks you to compromise least. The trade is that others have worked this out too, so book accommodation earlier than you otherwise would.

May to August: hot everywhere, wet in two places
The north turns hot and humid, with the 90s common in Hanoi and the year's heaviest rain falling in July and August. Sapa stays cooler and becomes a genuine escape, and the rice terraces are green and growing through this period.
The centre is the surprise: this is its dry, hot season, and Da Nang and Hoi An have their best beach weather of the year. If your trip is beach-led and must happen in summer, the central coast is the answer. The south is in wet season, which in practice means clear mornings and a heavy afternoon hour of rain rather than washed-out days.

What the summer heat actually feels like
Temperature alone understates it. Humidity in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City in July regularly makes the mid-90s feel like well over a hundred, and midday sightseeing stops being enjoyable. The local rhythm adjusts for this — early starts, a long flat middle of the day, activity again after four — and copying it makes summer travel far more pleasant than fighting it.

September to November: the split
These months divide the country more sharply than any others. The north is excellent: October and November bring clear skies, comfortable temperatures and the harvest in Sapa. The south is drying out and improving through November.
The centre, meanwhile, is in its typhoon season. Hue and Hoi An take the brunt, Hoi An's old town floods in most years, and coastal boat trips including Ha Long cruises further north can be cancelled on short notice. Storms are forecastable a few days out but not bookable around months in advance, so autumn itineraries through the centre need slack built in.

Ha Long Bay has its own rules
Cruises run year-round, but the bay is weather-sensitive in a way the rest of the north is not. Summer brings the risk of storm cancellations, and winter brings the mist that makes the karsts atmospheric in photographs and invisible in person. October and November give the best odds of clear water and clear air, which is one reason the north's autumn is worth protecting in an itinerary.

How to use this grid
Work backwards from the region you most want to see rather than from the month. If Hoi An and the central beaches are the point of the trip, avoid September to November and you have eight good months. If Sapa and the northern mountains are the point, aim for September to November or March to April. If you want all three regions, March and April is the least compromised window, with October a strong second if you sequence the centre carefully.
One practical note that applies all year: Vietnam has a single time zone and does not observe daylight saving, so the gap to the United States shifts by an hour twice a year rather than staying fixed. Check it when you book rather than assuming.

--- traveling-vietnam-during-tet | handoff=turn | swamp=tet-timing ---
TITLE: Traveling Vietnam During Tet (Lunar New Year)
EXCERPT: Tet is not a festival you attend as a tourist — it is the country going home. Here is what actually closes, what sells out, and when it is worth it anyway.

Tet is the Lunar New Year and the most important date in the Vietnamese calendar, and the honest framing for a visitor is that it is not a festival laid on for the public. It is the entire country travelling home to be with family. Understanding that one fact explains everything else about what works and what does not.

When Tet falls, and why the dates move
Tet follows the lunar calendar, so it shifts every year, usually landing between late January and mid-February. In 2027 Tet day is 6 February, with the public-sector holiday announced as 4 to 10 February. Always confirm the dates for your own year before booking anything — they are set each year and widely misreported in travel articles written well in advance.
The official holiday is the smaller number. The practical disruption window is roughly a week either side, because people travel home before and back after. Vietnam Airlines, for instance, treats a span running into the third week of February as its return-surge period for routes from Ho Chi Minh City to the north.

What closes
Family-run businesses — which is most of the good food in Vietnam — close for several days, sometimes a week. Small restaurants, independent cafes, market stalls, tailors in Hoi An, local tour operators. Museums and official sites close on the main days. Hotels, international chains, larger restaurants and the bigger tour companies stay open, which is why a package trip feels the disruption far less than independent travel does.

What happens to transport and prices
Domestic flights and trains sell out months in advance and fares rise to multiples of normal levels. This is the part that catches people: it is not that travel is inconvenient during Tet, it is that the seat may simply not exist. Intercity buses fill too, and the roads around the major cities become genuinely congested in the days before.
Accommodation behaves differently by region. City hotels can actually be cheaper, because business travel stops and the cities empty. Coastal and resort areas go the other way as domestic holidaymakers arrive. Expect the reverse of the usual pattern.

Cash, and the one thing that always bites
Banks close, and ATMs in smaller towns run empty and are not refilled for days. Whatever you think you need in cash before the holiday starts, take more, and take it out in a city. This is the single most common practical failure for visitors during Tet, and it is entirely avoidable.

What Tet is actually like, if you go
It is quiet, and that surprises people expecting a street carnival. The public spectacle happens in the days before — flower markets selling kumquat trees and peach blossom, families buying in bulk, a genuine buzz in the markets — and then on the day itself the streets empty as everyone goes indoors to eat with family. Hanoi's Old Quarter at Tet is the stillest you will ever see it.
There are real rewards. Temples and pagodas are full and alive. The cities are navigable in a way they never otherwise are. If you have any Vietnamese connection, or are invited into a home, it is the warmest possible time to be there. And the flower markets in the week before are a genuine sight.

If you are going anyway
Book flights and intercity transport the moment your dates are fixed, months out. Stock cash before the holiday begins. Base yourself somewhere with enough open infrastructure — a larger city or a resort area rather than a small town. Lower your expectations about eating: have a hotel with food, or accept that your favourite-looking place will be shuttered. And learn chuc mung nam moi, which will be received very warmly.

A little etiquette goes a long way
Red envelopes of lucky money, li xi, are given to children and sometimes to elders; small crisp notes in a red envelope are the form, and the amount matters less than the gesture. Avoid giving anything black or sharp as a gift. The first person to enter a home in the new year is traditionally significant, so do not let yourself be the uninvited first foot through a neighbour's door on the morning of Tet. None of this is enforced on visitors, and nobody will be offended by a foreigner who gets it wrong — but knowing it changes how the week reads.

What this decision is really attached to
Notice what Tet actually does to a trip. It does not simply make one week inconvenient. It fixes your transport bookings months earlier than you would otherwise make them, which fixes your route before you have finished choosing it. It changes which direction you should travel the country, because a one-way north-to-south plan that depends on a mid-trip internal flight is far more fragile than one that does not. It changes where you can afford to stay, in the opposite direction from your instincts. And it decides whether you need a guided structure or can improvise.
In other words, a single date on the lunar calendar quietly reorders the sequence in which every other decision has to be made. How that sequence works, and which choices have to be locked before which, is set out in the complete Vietnam decision manual.

--- vietnam-festivals-calendar | handoff=seed | swamp=tet-timing ---
TITLE: Vietnam Festivals Calendar Beyond Tet
EXCERPT: Tet dominates the calendar, but a dozen other dates will shape what you see — and two of them close things just as firmly.

Tet absorbs most of the attention, and reasonably so, but Vietnam's year has a dozen other dates worth knowing. Some are worth planning a trip around. Two of them will close things and fill beaches whether you planned for them or not.

The two that affect your logistics
Reunification Day on 30 April and International Labour Day on 1 May fall together and function as a long domestic holiday. Vietnamese travellers fill coastal resorts, Da Lat and Sapa, prices rise sharply, and domestic flights and hotels book out. The exact length of the break is set by government announcement each year because it depends on how the dates land against the weekend, so check it rather than assuming a fixed span.
National Day on 2 September is the other one. It is a shorter disruption than the April–May pair but follows the same pattern: domestic travel surges and the beaches fill. Hanoi holds official ceremonies around Ba Dinh Square, which is worth seeing but closes roads.

Why these matter more than the cultural dates
Festivals mostly add something to a trip. Public holidays subtract capacity from it. If you are choosing between two weeks and the only difference is that one contains a national holiday, that is a real reason to pick the other.

The lunar dates worth planning around
Most Vietnamese festivals follow the lunar calendar, so their Gregorian dates shift each year and need checking for your own travel year.
The Perfume Pagoda festival runs from shortly after Tet through to around the third lunar month, drawing very large crowds to the boat-and-cave pilgrimage southwest of Hanoi. Going outside the festival means a calm trip; going during it means seeing something genuinely communal, with the crowds that implies.
Hung Kings Temple Festival, on the tenth day of the third lunar month, is a public holiday honouring Vietnam's legendary founders, centred on Phu Tho province north of Hanoi. It adds a day off to the domestic calendar.
Mid-Autumn Festival, on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month, usually September, is the one most visitors enjoy most. Lanterns, mooncakes, lion dances and children in the streets. Hoi An is spectacular for it, and Hanoi's Old Quarter fills with lantern sellers in the run-up.

Hoi An's monthly lantern night
Separately from any festival, Hoi An turns off its electric lights on the fourteenth day of each lunar month and lights the old town with lanterns. It happens every month, it is beautiful, and it is easy to catch by accident or to plan for with a calendar check.

Regional and seasonal events
Hue hosts a biennial festival of royal arts and performance in its citadel, typically in spring, which transforms the city when it runs. Da Nang's international fireworks competition brings weekend displays over the Han River across several months in summer, and fills hotels on those weekends.
In the far north, the ethnic-minority markets have their own rhythm — Bac Ha's Sunday market being the best known and the most worth building a Sapa itinerary around, since it only happens on Sundays. Harvest in the northern terraces falls around September and October and is the reason those months are the photographic peak for Sapa and Ha Giang.

Dates most visitors never hear about
Vu Lan, the Wandering Souls festival on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month, is the second most important family occasion after Tet. Pagodas fill, offerings are burned in the street, and roses are pinned to clothing — red for those whose mother is living, white for those whose mother has died. It is quieter than Tet and does not disrupt travel, but it is deeply felt.
The Lim Festival in Bac Ninh, shortly after Tet, is where quan ho call-and-response folk singing is performed in its home province, now recognised by UNESCO. In fishing communities along the central and southern coast, whale worship festivals honour the animal as a protector of fishermen, with dates varying village by village.

Lunar or solar — check which
Reunification Day, Labour Day and National Day are fixed solar dates and land on the same day every year. Almost everything else here is lunar and moves. When you read a date in any article, including this one, confirm whether it is fixed or floating before you plan around it.

How to use the calendar

How to use the calendar
Two passes. First check whether your dates collide with Tet, the April–May pair or National Day, because those affect whether you can travel at all as planned. Then, separately, see whether anything you would actively enjoy happens to fall inside your window, and treat that as a bonus rather than the organising principle.
The one case for inverting that order is Mid-Autumn in Hoi An or the Sapa harvest, both of which are good enough to build a trip around. Both also sit inside windows where the regional weather cooperates, which is not a coincidence — and the interaction between season, festival and region is one of the things the complete Vietnam decision manual lays out in sequence.

TITLE: What to Pack for Vietnam
EXCERPT: Pack for three climates, two dress codes and a lot of walking. The list that matters is short, and most of it is about rain, feet and temples.

Packing for Vietnam is harder than packing for a single-climate country, because a two-week trip from Hanoi to the Mekong can cross a cold damp winter and a tropical dry season in the same suitcase. The list below is organised around the few things that actually go wrong rather than around completeness.

Start with which Vietnams you are visiting
If your trip is entirely in the south, pack as you would for Florida in summer and stop reading the cold-weather section. If it includes Hanoi or Sapa between roughly November and March, you need real layers: a fleece or light down, a waterproof shell, and something warm for your hands in the mountains. Sapa can approach freezing and visitors routinely arrive unprepared because they packed for Southeast Asia in the abstract.
For a trip spanning both, the workable approach is lightweight layers rather than bulky items — long-sleeve base layers, a packable insulated jacket, and a shell that handles both the northern drizzle and a southern downpour.

Rain, and what actually works
A compact umbrella beats a rain jacket for city use in the south, where the rain is warm and heavy and a sealed jacket turns into a sauna. In the north's fine winter drizzle the jacket wins. Carrying both is reasonable and neither takes much space.
Cheap plastic ponchos are sold on every street corner for less than a dollar and are the standard local solution for a sudden downpour on a motorbike. Buy one there rather than carrying one.

Shoes are the thing people get wrong
You will walk a great deal on uneven pavements and you will take your shoes off repeatedly — entering temples, homes, many homestays and some small shops. Slip-on or easily unlaced shoes save real friction. Bring one pair of comfortable walking shoes and one pair of sandals, and skip the third pair you are considering.

Clothing for temples and the mausoleum
Day-to-day dress in Vietnamese cities is relaxed and Western, and shorts and t-shirts are unremarkable. Two exceptions need packing for. Temples and pagodas require shoulders and knees covered, so bring one lightweight long-sleeve layer and trousers or a long skirt that you can put on over what you are wearing. A scarf doubles as a shoulder cover and is the single most useful item on this list.
The Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum in Hanoi enforces a stricter code than anywhere else you are likely to go — no shorts, no bare shoulders, no hats inside, and cameras and phones are not permitted in the viewing chamber. If it is on your itinerary, plan an outfit for it.

The small things that matter
Vietnam runs on 220V at 50Hz, and sockets commonly take both US flat two-pin and European round two-pin plugs, so a US charger usually fits without an adapter. The voltage is the part to check: almost all phone and laptop chargers handle 100–240V and are fine, but a 110V-only appliance such as an older hair dryer will be destroyed. Read the label before packing it.
Bring insect repellent with DEET or picaridin — dengue is daytime-biting and urban, so this is not only a jungle concern. Bring any prescription medication in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the prescription, because some ingredients in common prescriptions, including certain ADHD treatments, are controlled in Vietnam. A small supply of rehydration salts and loperamide covers most of what goes wrong.

What not to bother with
Do not pack towels, basic toiletries, or large quantities of clothing. Laundry is cheap, fast and available everywhere, usually priced by the kilo and returned the same day, which makes a week's clothes sufficient for a month's trip. Do not bring a water filter bottle unless you are trekking; bottled water is universal and cheap. And do not bring much cash in US dollars beyond a modest emergency reserve — you will spend in dong.

If your trip has a motorbike or trekking leg
Riding pillion on the Ha Giang loop or trekking out of Sapa changes the list. Bring real closed shoes with grip rather than sandals, long trousers regardless of heat, and a windproof layer — mountain roads are markedly colder than the valley you left. Operators supply helmets, but the supplied ones are often token shells, and anyone riding for days should consider their own.
A dry bag or a few resealable plastic bags protect electronics from both rain and dust, and a power bank matters more here than in cities, because a day on the road is a day away from sockets while using your phone for photographs and navigation.

Connectivity
An eSIM installed before you leave is the lowest-friction option and works on landing; a physical tourist SIM bought at the airport requires passport registration and takes a few minutes. Either way, do not plan to rely on your US carrier's international roaming, which costs many times more for worse coverage.

One thing to leave room for
Hoi An's tailors are a genuine industry rather than a tourist gimmick, and a lot of visitors leave with more clothing than they arrived with. If that is likely, arrive with space or plan on a cheap extra bag bought locally.
