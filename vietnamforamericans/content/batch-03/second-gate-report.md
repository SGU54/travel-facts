Task A — In-context errors only

This audit uses the supplied text and facts.json. Agreement between articles establishes consistency, not independent verification.

1. Best Time to Visit Vietnam — manufactures an unavoidable weather conflict

“The moment your itinerary includes both Hanoi and Hoi An, you are trading one region's best weather against another's worst, and the trade is the actual decision.”

Contradicts its own answer:

“March and April are the best single compromise across the whole country.”

The weather article also explicitly describes March–April as simultaneously good in all three regions. Including Hanoi and Hoi An does not inherently require experiencing one region’s worst weather.

2. Best Time to Visit Vietnam — presents itinerary order as a remedy without establishing why

“If your trip spans both and must happen in autumn, put the centre early in the itinerary and keep a day of slack, or accept that you may lose a beach day to weather.”

The supplied account places September–November inside the central coast’s typhoon season. Nothing establishes that visiting the centre earlier improves conditions. Reducing the stated consequences—flooding and cancelled boats—to a lost beach day also understates the disruption already described.

The same misleading implication appears in Vietnam Weather by Region and Month:

“If you want all three regions, March and April is the least compromised window, with October a strong second if you sequence the centre carefully.”

Careful sequencing is presented as making October a strong nationwide alternative, although the article itself says storms cannot be planned around months ahead.

3. Best Time to Visit Vietnam / Vietnam Festivals Calendar Beyond Tet — contradict their central-coast weather warning

Best Time to Visit Vietnam:

“Both sit inside windows where the regional weather happens to cooperate.”

“Both” refers to Mid-Autumn in Hoi An, usually September, and the northern rice harvest. Yet the same article states:

“The complication is September to November, which is typhoon season on this coast.”

Vietnam Festivals Calendar Beyond Tet:

“Both also sit inside windows where the regional weather cooperates, which is not a coincidence — and the interaction between season, festival and region is one of the things the complete Vietnam decision manual lays out in sequence.”

The festival article repeats the misleading weather assurance directly inside its guide handoff. The claim that this alignment “is not a coincidence” adds an unexplained causal assertion.

4. Vietnam Weather by Region and Month — wrong month count and unsupported expansion of beach season

“If Hoi An and the central beaches are the point of the trip, avoid September to November and you have eight good months.”

Excluding three months leaves nine, not eight. More substantively, avoiding the worst season does not establish that every remaining month is good for beaches. The companion article identifies February–August—a seven-month period—as when these beaches work, while this article describes winter as transitional.

5. Vietnam Weather by Region and Month — inconsistent harvest window

“The north is excellent: October and November bring clear skies, comfortable temperatures and the harvest in Sapa.”

Compare Vietnam Festivals Calendar Beyond Tet:

“Harvest in the northern terraces falls around September and October and is the reason those months are the photographic peak for Sapa and Ha Giang.”

The weather article shifts the advertised Sapa harvest window into October–November. The overlap in October does not support promoting November as a harvest month. This is an internal timing inconsistency, without determining the correct harvest dates externally.

6. Traveling Vietnam During Tet — converts transport pressure into a universal outcome

“Domestic flights and trains sell out months in advance and fares rise to multiples of normal levels.”

This treats sellouts, their timing, and multiplied fares as general outcomes across flights and trains, without distinguishing routes, dates or services. The next sentence is more appropriately qualified:

“This is the part that catches people: it is not that travel is inconvenient during Tet, it is that the seat may simply not exist.”

Possible unavailability supports advance planning; it does not establish the preceding blanket claim.

The same escalation appears in Best Time to Visit Vietnam:

“Domestic flights and trains sell out months ahead at multiples of normal fares, many family-run businesses close for several days, and cities empty while the countryside fills.”

7. Traveling Vietnam During Tet — turns cash risk into certainty and an unsupported ranking

“Banks close, and ATMs in smaller towns run empty and are not refilled for days.”

“This is the single most common practical failure for visitors during Tet, and it is entirely avoidable.”

The first sentence generalizes a potential ATM problem into an expected outcome. The second asserts both a comparative ranking and complete preventability that the supplied account does not establish. Taking precautions can reduce exposure; it cannot guarantee that cash problems are entirely avoidable.

8. Traveling Vietnam During Tet — manufactures constraints in the handoff

“It fixes your transport bookings months earlier than you would otherwise make them, which fixes your route before you have finished choosing it.”

Advance-booking pressure becomes a compulsory timetable and a supposedly fixed route. Neither follows for every itinerary.

“It changes which direction you should travel the country, because a one-way north-to-south plan that depends on a mid-trip internal flight is far more fragile than one that does not.”

This compares having a flight dependency with not having one. It does not establish which geographical direction is preferable. The conclusion and its supporting comparison concern different decisions.

“It changes where you can afford to stay, in the opposite direction from your instincts.”

Earlier, city hotels only “can actually be cheaper.” Here a possibility becomes a definite affordability change, combined with an assumed reader expectation.

“And it decides whether you need a guided structure or can improvise.”

The article establishes that larger operators may remain open and independent travel requires preparation. It does not establish that Tet determines whether a guided trip is necessary. Planned independent travel is an obvious omitted possibility.

9. Traveling Vietnam During Tet — guarantees other people’s reactions

“None of this is enforced on visitors, and nobody will be offended by a foreigner who gets it wrong — but knowing it changes how the week reads.”

“Nobody will be offended” is an absolute assurance about individual responses. It also undercuts the preceding warning about the significance of the first person entering a home.

10. Vietnam Festivals Calendar Beyond Tet — asserts an unconditional holiday-duration comparison

“It is a shorter disruption than the April–May pair but follows the same pattern: domestic travel surges and the beaches fill.”

This describes National Day. The preceding paragraph explicitly says the April–May break varies with weekends and annual announcements. No year-specific comparison establishes that National Day necessarily produces a shorter disruption. Administrative holiday length and practical travel disruption are also not interchangeable.

11. Vietnam Festivals Calendar Beyond Tet — implies closures equivalent to Tet without establishing them

“Tet dominates the calendar, but a dozen other dates will shape what you see — and two of them close things just as firmly.”

The body describes crowded resorts, higher prices, booked accommodation and road closures. Those do not establish the broad business closures described in the Tet article. The excerpt creates an unsupported equivalence between different kinds of disruption.

12. What to Pack for Vietnam — prescription advice weakens the supplied warning

“Bring any prescription medication in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the prescription, because some ingredients in common prescriptions, including certain ADHD treatments, are controlled in Vietnam.”

The applicable facts.json statement is:

“Ingredients in some common prescription medicines, including for ADHD, may be illegal in Vietnam.”

“Bring any” with packaging and a prescription implies those precautions suffice. They do not resolve the supplied possibility that an ingredient is illegal. This is a misleading implication against an applicable fact, not a complaint about missing fact coverage.

13. What to Pack for Vietnam — promises connectivity and a universal roaming disadvantage

“An eSIM installed before you leave is the lowest-friction option and works on landing; a physical tourist SIM bought at the airport requires passport registration and takes a few minutes.”

Installation alone does not establish device compatibility, activation or usable service on arrival. “Works on landing” is presented as guaranteed.

“Either way, do not plan to rely on your US carrier's international roaming, which costs many times more for worse coverage.”

This makes both price and coverage universally worse without specifying the carrier or plan. No comparison in the supplied material supports that categorical recommendation.
