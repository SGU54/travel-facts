----- ARTICLE: Mekong Delta: Day Trip or Overnight?  (slug mekong-delta-day-trip-vs-multi-day-tour)
EXCERPT: The packaged day trip out of Ho Chi Minh City and the overnight to Can Tho are not two sizes of the same thing. They are different trips.

Almost everyone visiting Ho Chi Minh City is offered a Mekong Delta day trip, and a lot of people take it and come away unimpressed. The overnight version is a different experience, and the gap between the two is larger than the extra day suggests.

What the day trip is

A coach leaves Ho Chi Minh City in the morning for My Tho or Ben Tre, the nearest delta towns, roughly two hours away. The standard circuit is a boat across to an island, a horse cart, a coconut candy workshop, a bee farm with honey tea, a sampan paddle down a narrow palm-lined canal, lunch, and the coach back.

It is inexpensive, it is organised, and it delivers you back to the city for dinner. What it is not is the delta as a working place: the stops are built for coaches, the sampan stretch is short, and several of the activities are demonstrations rather than things happening anyway.

Why it disappoints people

Mostly because of what it is sold as. Marketed as "the Mekong Delta", it is a day of staged stops on the nearest fringe of it. Sold honestly as a day out of the city with boats, canals and coconut candy, it is reasonable value and a pleasant enough day.

What the overnight is

Can Tho is about three to four hours from Ho Chi Minh City, deeper into the delta and a proper city in its own right. You arrive in the afternoon, sleep there, and go out on the water before dawn.

The reason to do it is Cai Rang, the floating market, which operates in the early morning and is finished by the time a day trip could reach it. Wholesale boats moor with produce hung from a pole above the bow so buyers can see what they are selling; smaller boats work between them. By seven or eight it is thinning out.

The thing the day trip cannot do

This is the whole argument. Cai Rang happens at dawn, and dawn in Can Tho is roughly four hours' drive from Ho Chi Minh City. No day trip from the city reaches it while it is running. The "floating market" included in some day itineraries is a different and much smaller operation closer to the city.

So the choice is not between a short delta trip and a long one. It is between a day of staged stops and an early morning on a working wholesale market, and those are not the same thing scaled differently.

What the overnight costs you

Two days of the trip rather than one, and on a short itinerary those days come out of somewhere else. A week in Vietnam that includes Hanoi, the centre and the south does not have two spare days for the delta without cutting something.

It also costs more, though not dramatically — the extra is a night's accommodation and the longer transport.

Doing the overnight independently

Can Tho is served by frequent buses from Ho Chi Minh City's western bus station and by private car. Accommodation in the city covers every level. Boats to Cai Rang are arranged through hotels or directly at the Ninh Kieu quay the evening before, and leave around five in the morning.

A private boat for two or three people costs little and lets you set the time and the route, which matters here because the market's best hour is early.

The middle options

Two-day packaged tours out of Ho Chi Minh City to Can Tho exist and solve the transport. They are a reasonable compromise if you would rather not arrange the pieces, though they put you on the water at the operator's hour rather than yours.

Ben Tre and Vinh Long homestays are the other middle route: closer than Can Tho, quieter than My Tho, with an overnight in a family home and a morning on smaller canals. They do not get you to Cai Rang, but they do get you off the coach circuit.

So which

If you have two weeks or more, do the overnight to Can Tho. If you have one week and the delta is a curiosity rather than a priority, skip it entirely rather than spend a day on the coach circuit — that day is better used in the city or the centre. If you have one week and you really want to see the delta, the day trip is the honest compromise, taken for what it is.

What makes this hard is that it is not a standalone decision. Two days for Can Tho come out of Hanoi, the centre or the coast, and which of those you can afford to shorten depends on what else is already fixed — the internal flights, the season, the airports at each end. Working out which commitments foreclose which is what the complete Vietnam decision manual is built around.


----- ARTICLE: Chau Doc to Cambodia: The Mekong Border Crossing  (slug chau-doc-cambodia-border-crossing)
EXCERPT: The boat from the delta up to Phnom Penh is a genuine alternative to flying, and it has one requirement people miss.

From Chau Doc, in the far west of the Mekong Delta, boats run up the river to Phnom Penh. It is one of the more pleasant ways to leave Vietnam, and it turns a transfer into part of the trip. It also has a documentation requirement that catches people out.

The visa requirement

This is the part to get right before anything else. A Vietnamese e-visa names the port of entry and exit you selected when you applied, and a land or river border is not the same as an airport. If your visa does not name the crossing you intend to use, you have a problem at the border rather than a formality.

Check the gate named on your visa against the crossing the boat uses, and do that while you can still change it. Vietnam's e-visa cannot be extended once issued, so this is settled before departure, not on the day.

If you intend to come back

Leaving Vietnam for Cambodia does not by itself create a problem. Returning does: a single-entry visa is spent once you leave. If the plan is Vietnam, then Cambodia, then Vietnam again, you need either a multiple-entry visa or a second application, and the second one names the gate you will re-enter through.

What the journey is

Operators run boats from Chau Doc up the Bassac and Mekong to Phnom Penh, with the border formalities handled at a riverside post on the way. The crossing takes most of a day. Faster speedboat services and slower, more comfortable river cruises both exist, and the price range between them is wide.

The scenery is river life rather than spectacle — stilt houses, fish farms, cargo barges, floating villages. It is a slow day and it is the point of taking the boat rather than the bus.

Getting to Chau Doc

Chau Doc is in An Giang province near the Cambodian border, around six hours from Ho Chi Minh City by bus and less by private car. Most people arrive the day before and stay the night, because the boats leave in the morning.

It can also be reached from Can Tho in a few hours, which makes a natural sequence: Ho Chi Minh City, Can Tho for the floating market, Chau Doc, then the boat to Cambodia.

Chau Doc itself

Worth the evening you will spend there. Sam Mountain outside the town has temples and a view over the floodplain into Cambodia. There is a large floating fish-farm community on the river, and a market that reflects the Khmer and Cham populations of the province as much as the Vietnamese.

Booking the boat

Tickets are sold by the operators and through hotels and agencies in Chau Doc and Can Tho, usually a day or two ahead. Confirm three things when you book: which border post the boat uses, whether the Cambodian visa is arranged on arrival at the post or must be obtained in advance, and what time the boat actually departs.

Carry passport photographs and small US dollar notes. Cambodian e-visa and visa-on-arrival rules are set by Cambodia and change independently of anything on the Vietnamese side, so check them with the Cambodian authorities rather than with the boat operator.

What to expect at the post

The boat stops at the Vietnamese post for exit formalities and again on the Cambodian side for entry. Passengers disembark, queue, and wait for the boat to be cleared. It is unhurried rather than difficult, and the operator's staff walk the group through it.

Have everything to hand before you arrive: passport, the printed e-visa, the Cambodian documentation, photographs and cash. Fishing a passport out of a packed bag at the window is how a straightforward crossing becomes a slow one for everybody.

Which direction the boat runs

The same route operates in reverse, Phnom Penh down to Chau Doc, and that direction has the same visa logic with the sides swapped: your Vietnamese e-visa must name the river crossing as its port of entry, not an airport. Arriving by boat on a visa issued for Tan Son Nhat is the version of this problem that strands people furthest from help.

The alternatives

Flying from Ho Chi Minh City to Phnom Penh takes about an hour and costs little. Buses run the direct road route in six to seven hours through the Moc Bai crossing. Both are faster than the boat.

The boat is the slowest and the most interesting of the three. If the journey is dead time to you, fly. If a day on the Mekong sounds like part of the holiday, take the boat.

On the day

Expect the border formalities to take a while and to involve waiting on the boat and at the post. Have your passport, your Vietnamese visa details and your Cambodian documentation accessible rather than packed. Bring water and something to eat; the stops are not catered.

Whether to run Vietnam north to south or south to north in the first place — which this crossing effectively settles — is a question the complete Vietnam decision manual works through.


----- ARTICLE: Ben Thanh Market: What It Is Good For  (slug ben-thanh-market-guide)
EXCERPT: Saigon's best-known market is a tourist market, and knowing that is what makes it useful.

Ben Thanh is the market everyone in Ho Chi Minh City is sent to. It sits at the centre of District 1 under a clock tower that is one of the city's landmarks, and it has been there in some form since the French period. It is also, squarely, a market for visitors — and it works better once you accept that.

What is inside

The covered hall is divided roughly by trade. Clothing and fabric occupy a large share, then souvenirs — lacquerware, conical hats, embroidered linen, coffee, chopsticks, T-shirts. There is a jewellery and watch section, a dried goods and spice section, and a food court of small stalls towards the back.

The food is the part most worth your time. The stalls sell pho, bun, banh xeo, com tam and seafood at prices above street level but below restaurant level, and sitting at a stall counter is a pleasant way to spend an hour.

The coffee and spice stalls

Vietnamese coffee, dried fruit, cashews, pepper and cinnamon are among the better things to buy here, because they are compact, they travel, and the quality is reasonable. Check whether cashews and dried fruit are vacuum-sealed if you are flying with them.

Prices and bargaining

Prices are not marked on most stalls and the opening figure quoted to a foreign visitor is a starting point rather than a price. Bargaining is expected here and is conducted without hostility.

The useful habits: settle the number before anything is wrapped, agree it in dong rather than dollars, walk away if the gap is too wide — there are usually several stalls selling the identical item — and know roughly what the thing costs elsewhere before you start. A shop with marked prices nearby is a good calibration.

The night market

When the hall closes in the evening, stalls set up in the streets around it, concentrated along the two sides of the building. The night version is more food and souvenirs, less fabric and jewellery, and the atmosphere is better.

It is also busier and tighter. Keep your bag in front of you and your phone away; bag snatching and motorbike phone grabs are the common petty crimes in Vietnamese cities, and a crowded night market is exactly the setting.

What it is not good for

It is not where the city shops. Prices are higher than the neighbourhood markets, the goods are weighted towards souvenirs, and the pressure from stallholders is constant in a way it is not elsewhere.

If you want a market that serves local households, Binh Tay in Cholon is the wholesale market for the western half of the city, and the smaller district markets across town are where food is actually bought.

Tan Dinh and the fabric market

Tan Dinh market, north of the centre near the pink church, is the city's fabric market and is a different proposition entirely — rolls of cloth rather than finished clothing, and a local clientele.

The scams to know about

Nothing elaborate, and nothing that costs much if you are paying attention. The recurring ones are a price agreed in dollars then converted unfavourably, a quoted figure that turns out to be per item rather than for the set, and change counted back short in a crowd.

All three are solved the same way: fix the number in dong, repeat it back, and count your change before you move off. Stallholders who are asked plainly will answer plainly.

Practical notes

The main hall opens early and closes in the evening; the street market runs after that. It is hot and closely packed inside, and the aisles are narrow. Go early in the day if you want to move around it comfortably.

The market sits above a metro station on the city's first line, which makes it easy to reach from the north of District 1. Grab drops and collects on the surrounding streets.

What is worth the money

Coffee, pepper, cinnamon, cashews and dried fruit are genuinely good value and travel well. Lacquerware and embroidered linen are reasonable if you look at the quality rather than the price. Silk, watches and branded clothing are where the gap between what you are told and what you are buying is widest.

How to use it well

Treat it as an hour rather than an afternoon. Eat at the food court, buy coffee and spices, look at the lacquerware, and go. The things worth buying here are the consumables and the small goods; the clothing and the branded items are where the price gap and the quality questions are widest.

And it is worth seeing once regardless of whether you buy anything, because the building and the scale of it are part of the city's fabric. Just do not plan the day around it.


----- ARTICLE: Ho Chi Minh City Nightlife: Where to Go  (slug ho-chi-minh-city-nightlife-guide)
EXCERPT: Backpacker street, craft beer district, rooftops and sidewalk beer. Four different evenings, and they do not mix.

Ho Chi Minh City stays up later than anywhere else in Vietnam, and its nightlife divides into several quite separate worlds. Knowing which one you want saves you a disappointing evening in the wrong district.

Bui Vien and the backpacker streets

A pedestrianised street in District 1 lined with bars, hostels and street kitchens, loud, lit in neon, and the single most concentrated nightlife in the city. Music competes from both sides of the street, plastic stools spill onto the road, and it runs late.

It is young, it is noisy and it is not subtle. People either enjoy it enormously or leave after twenty minutes, and both reactions are reasonable. Keep your phone and bag secure; crowded nightlife streets are where the city's petty crime concentrates.

Bia hoi and sidewalk beer

The cheapest and most characteristic drinking in Vietnam happens on plastic stools on the pavement. Bia hoi — fresh, light draught beer brewed daily — is a northern institution more than a southern one, but sidewalk beer culture is everywhere in Ho Chi Minh City.

Look for the streets where people are sitting out with Saigon or 333 and a plate of something fried. It costs very little, it starts early in the evening, and it generally finishes earlier than the bars.

The craft beer scene

Ho Chi Minh City has a well-established craft brewing scene, concentrated in a cluster of taprooms in District 1 and spreading into the surrounding districts. The beers are made in Vietnam, often with local ingredients — passion fruit, lemongrass, coffee, pepper.

It is a different evening entirely from Bui Vien: seated, conversational, more expensive than sidewalk beer and considerably cheaper than the rooftops. A taproom crawl through District 1 is one of the better nights out in the city.

Rooftops

The city has built upwards and the hotels and towers have put bars on top of it. Rooftop bars range from the very formal in the landmark towers to relaxed terraces on smaller buildings, and the view is what you are paying for.

Dress codes apply at the higher-end ones, and the prices are international rather than Vietnamese. Go for a drink at sunset rather than an evening; the view is the product and it does not improve after dark.

The apartment block bars

An old apartment building on Nguyen Hue has been converted floor by floor into small cafes, bars and shops, reached by a narrow staircase and a creaking lift. It is a genuine oddity and worth an hour. Similar conversions exist elsewhere in the centre.

Eating late

The city eats at every hour, and the late-night food is part of the night out rather than something preceding it. Com tam stalls, banh mi carts, oc restaurants selling snails and shellfish by the plate, and the hotpot places that fill up after ten all run well past midnight.

Oc in particular is worth seeking out: a street of small restaurants with tanks out front, where you order by the plate and the beer keeps coming. It is loud, cheap, and more characteristic of a Saigon night than any bar.

Live music and clubs

Live music runs from jazz clubs with a long history in the centre to rock bars and acoustic sets in the craft beer taprooms. The clubs cluster in District 1 and run very late at weekends.

The city also has a strong cafe-into-the-evening culture, which is worth knowing if you are not drinking — a lot of social life here happens over iced coffee at eleven at night.

Where the districts sit

District 1 holds most of it: Bui Vien, the rooftops, the taprooms and the clubs are within a short Grab ride of each other. Thao Dien, in District 2 across the river, is the expatriate neighbourhood and has a quieter, more residential bar scene. District 3 and Phu Nhuan have local bars and cafes with fewer visitors.

What it costs

The range between the ends of this is very wide. A sidewalk beer and a plate of food is a few dollars for an evening. A taproom night is several times that. A rooftop cocktail is priced like a cocktail in any major city, and the gap is the single biggest decision you make about the evening.

Getting home

Grab runs all night and is the sensible way back. Agree nothing with an unmetered taxi at three in the morning, and be aware that the app fare is the fare.

Picking your evening

Sunset on a rooftop, dinner somewhere, and a taproom afterwards is the version that shows you the most of the city in one night. Bui Vien is better as a short visit than a destination unless that is specifically what you came for. And an evening on plastic stools with cheap beer and grilled food is the one most people remember, which is also the cheapest.


----- ARTICLE: The Best Rooftop Bars in Ho Chi Minh City  (slug best-rooftop-bars-in-ho-chi-minh-city)
EXCERPT: Which rooftops are worth the price, which view you are actually buying, and when to go up.

Ho Chi Minh City's skyline has grown fast, and the bars on top of it are one of the few things in Vietnam priced internationally. They are worth it once, at the right hour, in the right place. Here is how to choose.

What you are paying for

The view, and nothing else. The drinks are competent rather than remarkable at most of them, the food is secondary, and a cocktail will cost several times what the same drink costs at street level. What justifies it is the city from above at the moment the lights come on.

Which means the single most important choice is the hour, not the bar. Arrive thirty to forty-five minutes before sunset, get a table at the rail, and watch the change. Arriving at ten at night buys you an expensive drink in the dark.

The landmark towers

Bitexco Financial Tower has the Saigon Skydeck observation level and a bar above it. The building's helipad disc makes it the most recognisable thing on the skyline, and the view from it covers the river and the centre. It is the most touristed option and it is priced accordingly.

Landmark 81, in Binh Thanh, is the tallest building in the city by a distance, with observation levels and bars near the top. It is further from the historic centre, so the view is of the river and the new city rather than the colonial core. The height is the draw.

The hotel rooftops

The Rex Hotel rooftop is the one with the history — it was where the daily American press briefings of the war were held, and the terrace overlooks the Nguyen Hue walking street and the People's Committee building. It is lower than the towers, which puts you in the city rather than above it. The crown and the topiary are unchanged and faintly absurd, in a good way.

The Caravelle's Saigon Saigon bar is the other wartime correspondents' rooftop, on the Opera House square, with live music most nights and a similar mid-height view across the old centre.

Chill Skybar, on the AB Tower, is the loud end of the category: a large open deck, DJs, a dress code, and a wide view over District 1. It is as much a club as a bar after dark.

The mid-height terraces

Below the headline names there is a tier of smaller rooftops on boutique hotels around District 1 — less view, far lower prices, no dress code, and often a better evening. The Nguyen Hue walking street and the streets around the Opera House have several.

Which to choose

For the classic skyline photograph and the river: Bitexco. For the sheer height: Landmark 81. For the history and the walking street below you: the Rex. For music and a crowd: Chill. For a quiet drink without an international bill: one of the smaller hotel terraces.

If you only do one, the Rex at sunset is the one that gives you the city rather than a panorama of it — you can see the street.

The riverside alternative

Not every elevated view in the city is a tower. The Thu Thiem side of the river and the bridges across it give you the District 1 skyline from the outside rather than from inside it, at no cost at all, and the walking street at Nguyen Hue is itself a good place to see the buildings lit.

If the photograph is the point, consider that the best picture of the skyline is taken from outside it, not from on top of it.

Practical notes

Dress codes at the larger venues generally rule out shorts, vests and open shoes for men; the smaller terraces do not care. Reservations are worth making at weekends and are essential if you want a rail table at sunset.

Many of the bars have a minimum spend or a cover at peak times rather than an entry fee. Ask when you book. And check the forecast — the rooftops close or move inside in heavy rain, which in the May-to-November wet season is a daily possibility in the late afternoon.

The cheaper version

The observation decks at Bitexco and Landmark 81 charge admission but no drinks minimum, and some bars let you spend the equivalent of the deck ticket on a drink instead and skip the deck. Doing the maths on that is worth two minutes before you buy either.

Going with children or not drinking

The observation decks work for both. They have no dress code, no minimum spend and no age restriction, and the view is the same one the bar above is charging for. The bars themselves vary — the hotel terraces are relaxed about it, the club-style rooftops are not.

When to go

Sunset, year round. In the dry months from December to April the air is clearer and the view goes further. In the wet season the late afternoon storms often clear just before dusk, which produces the better skies of the two.
