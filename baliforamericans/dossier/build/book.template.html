The contract

What this book is for

What this book is forThe contract

A first trip to Bali is not hard. It is far, and the decisions that shape it are made in the wrong order, by people who have read everything.

Bali is among the easiest places an American can travel to, and among the most written about. The visa is bought on a website, the food is good at every price, a car and its driver can be had for the day, and the people are warmer to visitors than the volume of visitors would lead you to expect. Almost nothing about the trip is difficult. What it is, instead, is far (two flights and a hub each way, the body sixteen hours out of step, a calendar day lost to the date line) and small in a way that misleads: an island eighty-nine miles wide whose distances are counted in hours, where the base you choose decides what the trip contains and the sea decides whether the one boat runs.

This book exists because that is where the days go. The number of nights decides how many bases there can be. The hour the long flight lands decides where the first bed should be. The month decides which coast the surf is on and how rough the strait is. The last night decides how long the only drive with a deadline will take. The island leg, placed where the picture says it should go, puts the one weather-dependent link against the flight home. None of that is difficult. It is simply not answerable one article at a time, which is exactly how it is usually answered.

What we do and do not claim

Every call in here is argued in the open, with the arithmetic on the page, so that you can disagree with us and still come out ahead. That is the contract. Where we say inland first, we show the three pins that say so; where the call is close, we say it is close. A recommendation you cannot audit is worth nothing to someone spending four thousand dollars, and the number of places where a confident voice is repeating a decade-old blog post is high in Bali: the Nyepi date from last year, the scooter that "everyone rides", the ATM fee nobody can source, the dry season that is supposed to mean a calm sea. ●

We do not tell you which villa to book or which warung to eat at. Those are the two things every guide leads with and the two that age fastest. We print no drive time, because the site this book is built from refuses to, and it is right: the number would be wrong at the hour you read it. The back of this book says plainly what it refuses to do, and why.

Every load-bearing claim carries a mark that tells you what kind of claim it is, and which of the two documents it belongs in.

Travel writing rarely distinguishes between a rule written into a governor's circular, a fact that has been true for forty years, and a fee that was checked once in October. This book marks the difference on the claims a decision turns on, because the three age at completely different rates and you are entitled to know which one you are being handed.

A mark is a classification, not a citation: it says what kind of claim you are reading. Reasoning carries no mark, because reasoning is ours and is what you are paying for; what it rests on is marked once, at the end of the sentence that states it. The one surveyed claim in this book is the set of road distances, measured by the site on the road network in October 2026 and printed as miles; nothing in it was measured in minutes.

Where the reporting comes from

This book is built from seventy-seven articles published on baliforamericans.com, the same desk, not an outside authority. It works from what they establish and makes the connections no single article was in a position to make. Every one is listed at baliforamericans.com/sources, with the date it went up. Where a claim rests on something else (the State Department, the immigration directorate, the customs service, the CDC, the governor's circular, the holiday decree) the Field Sheet prints the address.

Why there are two documents

The book is built to stay true for about two years and contains no live fee or price, because a figure printed in a book is a lie with a long shelf life. Every volatile figure (the visa and levy amounts, the overstay fine, next year's Nyepi, the holiday cluster, the crossing times, the tax rates) lives in the Field Sheet, a short dated companion reissued every quarter. When the two disagree, the Sheet is right and the book is old. Read the book once before you book anything; carry the Sheet on the trip.

The ground

Short miles, long days

Short miles, long daysThe ground

The single fact that reorganises a first trip to Bali, and the reason the usual question has no answer.

Ask how long it takes to get from the airport to Ubud and you will be given a number. The number is honest for the person who measured it and wrong for you, because the question has no answer. The distance is about twenty-two road miles, which an American reads as half an hour. The time depends on the hour, the day, the rain, and whether a village has closed its road for a ceremony, and it can be several times what the mileage suggests. The site this book is built from measured every distance on the island and refused to print a single drive time. That refusal is the ground the book stands on. ▲

Bali is about eighty-nine miles from east to west and fifty from north to south, with the airport on a narrow neck of land in the south. From there it is about four road miles to Kuta, seven to Seminyak, eight to Nusa Dua, eleven to Sanur, twelve to Canggu and thirteen to Uluwatu; twenty-two to Ubud in the hills; thirty-five to Sidemen and the Gili port at Padang Bai; about fifty to Munduk in the mountains and sixty to Amed and Lovina on the far coasts. In miles, nothing is far. In hours, the island divides into three: a south where a short hop is an app ride, a centre that is a morning away, and a far side that is a day's travel and belongs to a stay rather than an outing. ●

The roads make it so. In the south they run through continuous built-up areas, narrow, shared with cars, trucks and a great many motorbikes that pass on either side; into the hills they are narrow and winding; everywhere a procession can close one for a period on no timetable a visitor can consult, and in the wet months heavy rain slows everything. The site's rule replaces the clock: a day holds three or four stops in one direction, chosen so the route never doubles back through the busy south, with the drive home counted as part of the day. A live map, set to the actual hour on the actual day, the night before, is the instrument; your driver's estimate is the second opinion, and the longer of the two is the one to believe.

Why that decides the base

Once distance is counted in hours, the base stops being a hotel choice and becomes the decision that contains all the others. The beach areas and the cultural centre are about twenty miles apart and a slow drive, so a single base means a long day trip to whichever one you did not choose; one such day is fine and three of them is a commute. The quiet regions are far enough that a day trip is impractical, so they are a stay or nothing, and a stay is three nights. The question to ask of every base is not what it is near but what it is far from, and the honest answer to that question is what turns a list of places into a sequence of nights. ●

Three fixed points then decide the order of those nights, and none of them is on the map. The hour the long flight lands, after two flights and a hub and a calendar day lost to the date line, decides how far the first bed can sensibly be from the terminal and what the first two mornings are good for. The sea under the one boat crossing, which answers to the harbour master and the swell and is roughest in the dry months, decides where in the trip the island nights can safely sit. The drive to the airport on the last morning, the only journey on the island with a penalty for being late, decides where the last night must be. Everything else about the itinerary is negotiable. These three are not.

What else the hours decide

Once the island is a set of hours rather than a map, most of the remaining decisions get easier, and several of them stop being decisions at all. The scooter that looked like the only way to reach dinner turns out to be a symptom of a villa in the wrong place. The four famous temples sort themselves by the base each is a short outing from, and few trips need all four. The volcano climb belongs to an Ubud morning, because the car comes in the middle of the night and comes earlier the further south you sleep. The entry stack, which fixes the trip's outer edge at thirty counted days and names the ticket out you must already hold, has to be done in an order that starts with the passport, months before any of this. ◇

Everything that follows is that single fact, worked out. Eleven decisions, each with the arithmetic on the page; the operations a first trip runs; and the failures, sorted by what they cost. The island is better than any plan you can make of it. The plan's job is to leave you free for that.

Before you fly

The things with deadlines

The things with deadlinesBefore you fly

Most of the preparation is optional. These are not, and each has a date attached — one of them months before the flight, one of them seventy-two hours before you land.

Bali asks less of an American traveller in advance than most of Asia does, and the few items with deadlines are the ones that look most like details. {{claim:passport-6m}} That single test puts a State Department renewal clock in front of everything else, because no visa cures a short passport and every later form is filled against the passport's number.

The passport deserves the first slot because it is the only item that can refuse you with no remedy at the border. The US Consular Agency in Bali is blunt that immigration will not let you in otherwise, and adds the detail many travellers miss: the endorsement pages at the back do not count as blank pages, so the count is of visa pages with nothing on them. A passport replaced after the e-VOA was bought leaves a visa issued for a number you no longer carry; renew first and buy the visa second. An emergency passport does not work with the visa on arrival at all. ◇

The clinic

{{claim:vaccines}} Hepatitis A and typhoid are both food-and-water borne, which is the relevant route here: {{claim:water}} A great deal of good eating is done at small places, and the habits are the same at every price. The CDC adds that the typhoid vaccine is not fully protective, so the food and water habits still matter. No one needs antimalarials for a normal Bali trip, which contradicts the blanket advice given about Southeast Asia: {{claim:malaria}} Dengue has no vaccine available in the United States and its mosquitoes bite by day, so repellent is a breakfast-terrace concern, not a jungle one. The CDC's Zika notice for Bali is the item anyone pregnant or planning a pregnancy reads before booking. The rabies decision is the one with a series of shots in front of the flight: for remote parts of the island, small children who reach for animals, or a long stay, it is a conversation with the clinic; for everyone, the rule after a bite or scratch is a wash and a doctor the same day. ◇

The medicine bag

This is the deadline that does not look like one. Indonesia sorts medicine by class, allows ninety days of ordinary prescriptions with the prescription and sixty of psychotropics, and bans the narcotic class outright with or without a prescription; the State Department names ADHD medicines among the ones that are illegal and notes that customs publishes no list to look yours up. The work is therefore yours, weeks out: find your drug's class, agree an alternative with your doctor if it is a stimulant or a strong painkiller, pack everything in its original packaging with a prescription per item, and go through every pocket and toiletry bag for cannabis and CBD products, which are illegal in every form however they were bought at home. ◇

What does not need doing

You do not need to register your phone with customs: a tourist roaming on a US SIM or using a local SIM bought at an operator's outlet is outside the rule, and the site reads a foreign travel eSIM as the roaming case. You do not need an International Driving Permit unless there is a chance you will rent a car, and if there is, you need it from home, because it is issued in the United States and a rental is the kind of decision that gets made on the spot; it adds no motorcycle class to a car licence. You do not need drivers, day tours, cooking classes or the volcano climb booked from abroad; they are arranged on the island a day or two ahead, with the weather in hand. You do not need a packing list for one climate if the trip stays in the south, and you do need a sweater for Munduk or a Batur summit, a sarong and a light cover for every temple gate, and a refillable bottle, because the island's rules ban single-use plastics and the State Department's advice is sealed water. ●

Day zero

The first six hours

The first six hoursDay zero

Nobody writes the arrival day, which is the one where a plan is most easily lost: two flights, a hub, a date line, and a body sixteen hours out of step.

The arrival day is written off before it starts, and the useful thing is to write it off on purpose. {{claim:timezone}} The flight west crosses the date line, so a Friday evening departure can land on a Sunday; the hotel night in between is paid for and never slept in, and the body arrives wanting to sleep through the Balinese afternoon and wake in the small hours. Fatigue from the journey passes after a night; the jet lag takes two days. Plan nothing for the arrival day beyond reaching a room.

What stands between the gate and the room is four things in order, and a traveller who did the paperwork from home has little left to do at any of them. The visa comes first: an e-VOA goes to immigration with the passport and the record; a visa bought at the counter means a payment line before the immigration line, with a card ready and cash as the backup, because no official list says what the counter accepts on a given day. Immigration checks the passport, the visa and the arrival card's QR code, which lives as a screenshot because airport Wi-Fi is a poor thing to depend on at that moment. Read the stamp before walking away, because the thirty days are counted from it. ◇

Customs is the last official step and the arrival card already carries the declaration. The allowance is one litre of alcohol per adult, with the excess destroyed rather than taxed, and a bottle bought at the hub counts toward it. Medicine is a red-channel item, which is where declared things are looked at and not an accusation; the prescriptions ride in the hand luggage for exactly this minute. Nothing about a personal phone needs the registration desk. ◇

Money and signal

The two things most travellers want before leaving the terminal are rupiah and data. The State Department says the ATMs accept US cards and may charge, and advises machines in secure locations and a check for tampering; the site's advice is enough rupiah at the airport for the first day's transport, a meal and small purchases, and the rest later at a bank branch, counted by the zeros. A travel eSIM set up at home works when the plane lands; a local SIM has to be bought and registered at an operator's counter first; and the ride app you set up while your US number still received texts is the one that will work in the hall. ●

Getting out

Transport from the airport comes in three forms, and the one the site recommends for a first arrival after a journey of this length is the one booked before you flew: a car arranged with the hotel or a driver, the name and the meeting point in writing. It costs more than an app ride and removes every decision from the moment you are least able to make one. The second is Grab, which runs pickups from its own lounge at the international terminal, booked in the app; the third is the official taxi counter with a working meter. The person who approaches you in the hall with a price is the case the State Department's advice to avoid unlicensed taxis at airports was written about; walk past. ◇

How far to go

The distance between the terminal and the first bed is a choice made when you booked, and it interacts with the hour. A daytime landing can go anywhere, including the twenty-two miles to Ubud, because the day is already written off and the dawn waking of the next two will be spent among the terraces. A dusk landing goes to the south coast, four to eleven miles away, with Ubud waiting for the morning. A landing late at night goes to the shortest drive there is, Kuta, Jimbaran or Sanur, and moves on after breakfast. The CDC's advice after landing is daylight and the local schedule, and it is easy to follow here: spend the first mornings outside, eat at Balinese hours from the first meal, keep naps to twenty minutes, and do not ride or drive anything on the first two days, because jet lag affects concentration and the roads ask for all of it. ●

The rhythm

How a day in Bali runs

How a day in Bali runsThe rhythm

The day has a shape here, and fighting it costs you the two hours that matter most, and the road home.

Bali gets up early and so, for two days, will you. A traveller from the United States wakes at five whether they want to or not, and the island at that hour is the version the photographs were taken of: the terraces before the coaches, the spring temple before the queue, the east-facing beach with the sun coming up over the water and the outriggers already back. By mid-morning the day-trip boats have landed, the viewpoints are shared, and the light has gone flat. The gap between six and nine is the difference between a place you will remember and a queue you will photograph, and it costs nothing but a decision made the night before. ●

The afternoon is the road's hour, and the site's advice is to be off it. Late in the day, beachgoers, commuters and sunset-seekers all move at once through the same narrow roads, and the sunset spots on the west-facing coast draw everyone to the same place at the same minute. If you want to be somewhere for it, arrive well ahead, stay for dinner nearby, and let the crowd leave before you do. A day out leaves early so that its return drive falls before this, which is also why the site's rule is three or four stops in one direction rather than six from a car window.

The heat is the other half of the rhythm. The Denpasar normals hold between seventy-nine and eighty-three degrees in every month, the wet months slightly warmer and heavier, and the CDC's advice is shade between ten and four. The local day adjusts for it, out early, slow in the middle, out again toward evening, and copying that rhythm makes the island far more pleasant than fighting it. A pool stops being a luxury here and becomes the thing that makes the afternoon work, which is why the question of whether a villa's bedroom closes and cools is in the where-to-stay chapter and not the packing list. ●

The two hours worth protecting

Build each day around one early anchor and let the rest be loose. The first-timer's instinct is four sights moved between efficiently; the better shape is one thing at opening, an unhurried middle, and an evening allowed to be unremarkable. Bali rewards the loose afternoon more than most places, because the density of small good things, a lane behind the paddies, a shrine with nobody at it, a warung that makes one dish, means walking produces finds that planning cannot. The alarm that matters is the five o'clock one, two or three times in a trip: for the volcano, for the terraces, for a boat that leaves in the morning because the afternoon sea is the one that cancels.

The set pieces

The five operations everyone runs

The five operations everyone runsThe set pieces

Each is a fixed thing on the island with a fixed way of going wrong. Not the sights; the procedure around them.

Every first trip runs the same five operations: a temple, a beach club, a volcano, a waterfall and a sea crossing. None is difficult and each has a small procedure that decides whether it is the day you came for or the one you tell people about afterwards. The sights are in every guide. The procedures are what the guides leave out.

The temple

The rules are written down. Under the governor's 2025 circular a foreign visitor may not enter a temple's inner and middle courtyards unless there to pray in Balinese dress, so every visit is to the setting, dressed for it: shoulders and knees covered, a sarong and sash where the gate offers them, no climbing on structures for the picture, no stepping over offerings for the angle. The four famous temples are in four corners of the island and sort themselves by base. Uluwatu, on its cliff seventy-five metres above the sea, is the one that fits a beach holiday; sunset is the popular hour, the kecak is staged in the evening, and the ride back is arranged before arriving. Tanah Lot is seven miles up the coast from Canggu and at its best in the morning, when the light is on the rock and the approach is not a queue. Besakih, the mother temple, is a mountainside complex of more than twenty temples that belongs to an Ubud or Sidemen day; a ceremony may close parts of it, so ask at the entrance what is open. Lempuyang is a day's driving from the south for one photograph taken by numbered turn with a mirror held under the phone, and worth it only from Amed. The smaller sites near Ubud, the spring temple at Tirta Empul, the cave at Goa Gajah, the steps down to Gunung Kawi, are often the better morning. ◇

The beach club

A beach club is a day venue with a pool, a view and a bar, where you pay through what you eat and drink, and the arrangement has four questions that the site puts to any reservations desk: what the minimum spend is and whether it is per person or per bed; whether it is counted before or after the ten percent tax and the service charge; how many people may use the bed; and from what hour until what hour it is yours, since a booking that ends before sunset on a west-facing coast is worth less than it looks. A seat at the bar or a table in the restaurant often carries no minimum at all, and the beach in front belongs to everyone. The site's view is that one such afternoon is plenty on a trip of a week or two, and that the ride home from a cliff-top club on the Bukit is arranged before arriving, because everyone leaves at once. ●

The volcano

The Batur sunrise climb is a short night followed by a short walk: about two hours from the lakeside village of Toya Bungkah with a required local guide, by headlamp, in a line, to a cold summit at about seventeen hundred metres, with the descent in daylight and often a hot spring after. The procedure is all in the hours before it. The pickup comes in the middle of the night and comes earlier the further south you sleep, so the climb belongs to an Ubud morning, the second or third, never the first, with the rest of that day kept free. The questions for the operator are the pickup time and the length of the drive, the size of the group, whether a headlamp is provided, and what happens if the mountain is closed or the weather is bad; the mountain is an active volcano and a trek that is closed for safety is closed. The caldera rim at Penelokan gives the view without the climb. ●

The waterfall

Almost every waterfall on the island sits at the bottom of a valley, so the day is a walk down, time at the water and a hot climb back; Nungnung's roughly five hundred steps are the famous case and not the only one. The site's rule is two or three in one direction, paired with a terrace or a temple rather than a fourth fall, with shoes that grip and can get wet, small notes for the booth, and an early start before the best-known ones fill. Each is run locally and charges its own fee, which the book does not print. The one procedure that matters is the water: after heavy rain a river can rise quickly and the CDC's advice on floodwater applies, so the swim waits a day. ●

The crossing

The sea crossing has a procedure because it is the one operation on the island with no fallback. A morning boat, in daylight, booked directly or through the accommodation with a named boat and time; a life jacket for every passenger, counted; the seats and the people counted too, and a walk away from an overloaded boat; the forecast asked for the evening before; shorts, sandals and a dry bag for the wet landing at Lembongan; luggage you can lift over your head, with the large suitcase left at a mainland hotel. If the sea looks rough from the beach, if the boat is crowded beyond its seats, or if departure is being pushed late into the afternoon, the site's advice is to wait for another boat or another day, because a lost ticket is a small cost, and the harbour master's message to operators was not to force a departure in poor weather. Passengers with flights to catch are part of that pressure, which is why the flight is never the next morning. ◇

Where to stay

Nine bases, scored the same way

Nine bases, scored the same wayWhere to stay

Not hotels. The part of the island to sleep in, judged on what the position saves you each day and what it costs you each night.

Where you sleep changes a Bali trip more than it does in most places, because every base comes with a list of what it is far from, and the list is the decision. The beach bases are far from the terraces, Ubud is far from the surf, the quiet regions are far from the airport, and a villa that is cheap because it is isolated costs a ride every time you want a meal. The question to ask of every position is the same: what does it save me each day, and what does it cost me each night.

The south

South Bali is several different places sharing one coast. Kuta, Legian, Seminyak and Canggu run north from the airport along the west-facing beach, from the most developed and cheapest at the Kuta end to the more upscale at Seminyak and the newer surf stretch at Canggu; the difference is character and price rather than geography. Sanur and Nusa Dua face east, away from the sunset, so the sun comes up over the sea and the evenings are quiet. The site's view is that Seminyak asks the least of a newcomer and has the most on foot; that Sanur is the most relaxed and the best placed for Ubud and the boats; and that Kuta is the practical first or last night at four miles from the terminal and the least distinctive for longer. In Badung regency the local tax on rooms is ten percent and a service charge may be added, so two listings are compared on the total. ◇

Ubud

Ubud is fourteen villages with one name, and the quiet people imagine is in the villages around a centre that travel guides describe as crowded, with very bad traffic. Stay in the centre if you want to walk to dinner and to the sights, and ask whether the room faces the road or a garden behind; stay in the villages if you want rice fields and birdsong, and ask about the shuttle, its hours, and whether the lane has a sidewalk and lighting, because distance in Ubud is measured in rides. For a first visit of three nights the site's view is the edge of the centre, a ten-minute walk from the main streets with fields beyond. Ubud is cooler at two hundred metres, open-sided rooms come with mosquitoes. ●

Four checks everywhere

Bali's rules require visitors to stay in licensed accommodation; the site has no public register to point you to, so ask the property for its licence details in writing. Pay by card through a platform, because the classic rental fraud is a listing copied from a real property at a low price with payment requested by bank transfer, and a transfer to a stranger is the money hardest to recover. Ask whether the bedroom closes and cools, since the CDC's advice against dengue is a room that does; whether the pool is fenced, if there are children; how the drinking water is supplied; and how far the nearest road a car can reach is, because some villas are reached only by a footpath. On arrival, photograph anything already damaged, with the date visible. ◇

The days, as operations

The days, as operationsThe plan

An itinerary tells you where to be at eleven o'clock. An operation tells you what kind of day you are running, which is more useful, because the specific temple matters far less than whether the day has one anchor or four. Four shapes work, and the mistake is almost always treating a transfer day, or a boat day, as a free one.

The ten-night spine

Fly into Denpasar and out of it, on one ticket, landing by day. Three nights in Ubud or its villages, the first written off to arrival; the centre on foot on the first full day while the body wakes at dawn, a driver for the terraces and the spring temple on the second, the volcano on the third morning if it is the point. On the fourth day a driver to Sanur and the morning boat to Nusa Lembongan, with the large suitcase left behind; two full days there, one of them on Nusa Penida by local boat; the morning boat back on the seventh day, with three or four nights still in hand. Then a southern beach base that suits you: Seminyak for dinner on foot, the Bukit for cliffs and surf, Sanur itself for calm and no transfer after the boat. Uluwatu at sunset from there, or Tanah Lot from Canggu, and the last night within a dozen miles of the terminal, with no boat and no long drive before the flight. ●

Seven nights is the same spine with the island cut: three nights in Ubud and four by the sea, with a Penida day trip mid-trip or a rest day in its place, and the question is only which coast. Fourteen nights is the spine with the far side added: four in Ubud, three in Munduk and the highlands, three in the east at Sidemen or Amed, four on the coast, moving one way round the island so that no drive doubles back through the south; the islands can replace either the highlands or the east, between Ubud and the final coast base, where a delayed boat costs a beach day. The honeymoon version starts slower, with four or five nights near Ubud and nothing that has to be done.

The joints

Look at the joints rather than the days. The arrival hour decides whether night one is in Ubud or four miles from the terminal. The boat's morning departure decides where the night before it is spent, which is why Sanur or Ubud, thirteen miles from the harbour, precede it. The return boat's day decides how many nights of slack stand between it and the flight, and the answer is at least one, two in July and August. The transfer between the highlands and the east is the longest drive of the trip, ninety-three miles from Amed to Munduk, and wants an early start and a driver who treats it as a sightseeing day. The last night decides the last morning. Each choice is small and each sets the terms of the next, which is what distinguishes a fortnight that works from one with the same list of places in a different order. ●

Jet lag, honestly

Twelve to sixteen hours is about as large as the offset gets, so there is no gentle version of the adjustment and the first two days are compromised whatever you do. Plan them as on-foot days with nothing that cannot be abandoned, get daylight, eat at Balinese hours from the first meal, keep naps to twenty minutes, and do not schedule the thing you most want to see on day one. The same applies at the other end: the CDC's two days before anything important includes the Monday meeting, so the trip lands at home a day before work, which is one more reason the nights on the island are fewer than the days off. ●

Eating

The rule that outlives the list

The rule that outlives the listEating

Bali is a Hindu island in a Muslim country with pork at the centre of its table and shrimp paste at the bottom of its sauces. The rule is simple; the list it would generate is wrong within a season.

Balinese cooking is its own cuisine within Indonesia, and its difference is pork: a Hindu island where babi guling, the spit-roasted pig, is the national dish and beef is rarely eaten. Most dishes begin with a spice paste of garlic, shallots, chilli, ginger, turmeric and palm sugar that usually contains shrimp paste, ground in where it cannot be seen. Coconut is everywhere, peanuts are in the sauces, rice comes with nearly everything. A diner who knows those five things, the paste, the coconut, the peanuts, the chilli and the rice, can read most of a menu. ●

The rule

The site names no restaurants and quotes no prices, and this book inherits both refusals, because a list is wrong within a season and the rule that generated it is still right in five years. The rule is the CDC's, with the island's own addition: food cooked to order and served hot, at a busy place that turns it over; nothing that has sat on a buffet; sealed drinks, no ice where you are unsure, and fruit you peeled yourself. Small local eating houses, the warungs, are where most of this food is served, and the CDC's caution about street vendors applies there with the same test: cooked in front of you, arriving hot. Ask your accommodation where local people go for each dish, since the best version of a thing is often at a place that makes little else. ◇

The hidden-ingredient rule is the one that matters to anyone with an allergy or a diet, because the ingredients that cause trouble are the invisible ones. A plate with no seafood on it can be built on shrimp paste; a vegetable dish can carry pork rind or meat stock; kacang covers peanuts and also other nuts and beans; soy sauce is commonly brewed with wheat. The site's advice is a printed card in Indonesian stating the allergy, that it is serious, and the ingredients to avoid, checked by a fluent speaker before you travel, plus the handful of phrases that cover most situations: saya alergi akan, I am allergic to; saya tidak makan babi, I do not eat pork; tanpa, without; saya vegetarian. A confident "no problem" is treated with the same caution as no answer. ●

The bill, and the glass

A menu price in the resort south and in Ubud is often not the price. The local tax on restaurant food and drink is ten percent in Badung and Gianyar, a service charge may be added at the business's own rate, and bars and clubs fall under a higher entertainment category that was set at forty percent and contested; the line at the foot of the menu says whether the additions are included, and the site's advice is to read it before ordering. A simple warung often includes everything in the listed price. Where a service charge appears, part of what you paid already reaches the staff and a tip is a personal extra; where none does, a modest amount in rupiah, handed to the person, is the gesture. ◇

The glass has its own rule. {{claim:methanol}} The protection is in what you choose and where: bottled beer, canned drinks, wine from a bottle you watched being opened, and spirits only from a sealed, labelled bottle, never from a jug or a refilled water bottle. Arak is part of the island's culture and wanting to taste it is reasonable; the site's advice is a sealed bottle or nothing. The symptoms are the trap, because they start like a hangover and can take a day to appear: a hangover far worse than the drinks, or any change in vision, is a hospital, and the word to say there is methanol, because the CDC says the antidotes work when given early. ◇

The one that puts people in hospital

Traffic, not crime, is what injures visitors, and the scooter is where it concentrates. The State Department describes roads shared by cars, trucks and large numbers of motorcycles that weave between vehicles and pass on either side, and a tourist on a rented scooter with no local riding experience is the most exposed person on them. Two things compound it, and they are the whole of Decision 05: the car-only licence most Americans hold does not match a motorcycle under the traffic law, and without a valid one the insurer may decline the bill. The bill itself is the second half of the risk: {{claim:medical-pay}} An ambulance can take forty-five minutes or longer, so the site's advice is to ask the accommodation on the first day which clinic it would send a guest to and how you would get there at night, and to treat a taxi as the ambulance. ◇

The one in the glass

Drink safety is the Bali risk the State Department describes most bluntly, and it covers two dangers at once: alcohol mixed with methanol, which cannot be seen, smelled or tasted in a mixed drink, and spiking, reported in Bali, Lombok and the Gili Islands along with sexual assault. The three official rules cost nothing: watch the drink being prepared, accept none from strangers, leave none unattended. The site's own rules go further, sealed and branded, spirits only from a labelled bottle, no free shots, and treat a hangover out of all proportion or any change in vision as an emergency, because the symptoms can take a day or more to appear and the antidotes work when given early. Anyone who drank from the same bottle is at the same risk whether or not they feel ill yet. ◇

The one nobody expects

{{claim:cbd}} For a traveller from a state where cannabis is legal that is the mechanism to understand: a cartridge in a jacket pocket is imported the moment it passes through the airport, and the person carrying the bag is the person who imported it. The same strictness reaches prescriptions, where the State Department names ADHD medicines and notes that no list exists to check against; the medicine bag is settled with a doctor weeks out, not at the border. The US Consular Agency in Jimbaran reports a current enforcement campaign on traffic violations, visa violations and drug possession by foreigners, and the practical reading is simple: the rules are being applied. An American arrested here is subject to Indonesian courts, and the agency is not one. ◇

The one that takes your phone

Violent crime against foreigners is rare. Losing a phone is not. It leaves your hand while you stand at the kerb watching for the car you booked, and the motorbike is gone before you have registered it. The defence is unglamorous: stand back from the road while you wait, put the phone away once the car has arrived, keep bags on the side away from the traffic, and match the plate and the driver's photo to the app before getting in, which the State Department says most trips go without incident when you do. {{claim:ambulance}} The 112 line is free and works without credit; save the numbers before you land. ◇

The ones that are not a decision

Dogs and monkeys are not petted, however tame they look, and any bite or scratch that breaks the skin is washed with soap and running water and seen the same day, whatever the animal looked like, because rabies is fatal once symptoms appear and treatment given promptly prevents it. Mosquitoes are met with repellent by day as well as dusk and a bedroom that closes, since dengue has no vaccine and its warning signs come after the fever breaks. The sea is met with the procedure in the set pieces, and with the two warnings the site repeats from its sources: rip currents at Dream Beach, and fatal falls at Devil's Tear, where the barriers are there for a reason and a wave larger than the ones before it gives no warning. None of these is a reason to stay home. Each is a reason to make the decision once, at home, and then stop thinking about it.

Within what remains, the unit that matters is the move. A hotel change costs about half a day of packing, a drive and a check-in, and the site's working minimum is three nights in a place, which gives two full days there; a base of two nights gives one, which is rarely enough to justify the change. That rule is where the ceiling comes from: nights divided by three is the upper limit on bases, and it is an upper limit, not a target. ●

The island's geography does the rest. The beach areas and the cultural centre are about twenty road miles apart and the drive between them is slow, so a single base means a long day trip to reach whichever one you did not choose. One such day is manageable. Three of them is a commute, and the reason most trips of a week or more split the stay. The quiet regions are further again: Munduk is about fifty-one road miles from the airport, Amed about sixty-one, and neither works as a day trip from anywhere a first visit stays. They are a stay or nothing, and a stay is three nights. ●

So each length has a shape, and the shape is not negotiable by enthusiasm. Five nights is one base and the beach, and most Americans should not fly this far for it. Seven is Ubud and one coast, with one hotel change and no slack if a day goes to rain or a stomach. Ten adds a third region (the Nusa islands are the closest, since the boats leave from Sanur) and one spare day, which is the length the site recommends for a first trip from the United States, and roughly what two weeks off work becomes once the travel days are counted. Fourteen adds a fourth base and opens the highlands and the east. Beyond that, the trip is long enough to need a visa extension decision, which is Decision 04's problem.

The honest move is to write the nights down before writing any places, and to accept what the number refuses. A traveller who lists Ubud, Seminyak, Uluwatu, the Gilis and Munduk against nine nights has not planned a trip; they have planned five arrivals.

The call

Count nights on the island, divide by three, and that is your ceiling on bases. Seven nights is Ubud and one beach base and nothing else; ten is the first length at which a third stop fits without rushing the first two; fourteen is the length the island is built for, and the first at which the far side (Munduk, Amed, Sidemen) stops costing more days than it gives. A fifth base in two weeks is a trip spent in cars. If the leave is a week, take the narrow trip on purpose or wait until you can take longer: a rushed week at the far end of a journey this long is the version people regret.

The reason this is a decision and not a preference is that the three pins are not symmetrical. Jet lag from the United States is about as strong as jet lag gets, and it has one useful property: it wakes you early. The site's day-plans use it deliberately, opening in Ubud so that the first mornings fall among rice terraces and spring temples before the coaches arrive, and so that the thing you most want to see is not scheduled for a version of yourself that is not fully present. Spend those mornings on a west-facing beach and they are spent asleep, because the beach's hour is sunset and the body's is five a.m. ●

The sea is the second pin and the least forgiving. Every island in a Bali trip is reached by small fast boat across open water, and the harbour master's office for the Sanur route issues a sailing approval for each departure and has told operators not to sail in poor weather. No cancellation rate exists, so none is printed; what the site does print is the margin: at least one full day between the boat back and an international flight, two in July and August or the wet season. The trap is that the dry season is not the calm season. {{claim:strait}} A boat landing at Sanur at midday with a flight that evening looks comfortable on paper and assumes the morning sailing ran. From the Nusa islands, if it did not, the only way back is still by sea.

The third pin is the only drive on the island with a deadline. The airport sits on a neck of land in the south, four road miles from Kuta, seven from Seminyak, eleven from Sanur, twenty-two from Ubud and sixty-one from Amed, and every one of those passes through the busiest roads on the island. The site prints no drive times because none would be true at the hour you read it; what it does say is that the leaving time is an output of where the last night is, not a separate choice. A last night on the coast makes departure day short and dull. A last night in the hills, or on an island, makes it long and tense. ●

Put the three together and the order writes itself, which is why the site's own week, ten-day and fortnight routes all run the same way: Ubud, then the island or the far side, then the coast. The exception the site allows is a late-night arrival, where a drive inland in the dark after twenty-four hours of travel argues for a first night on the coast instead, close to the terminal, and a move inland after breakfast. That is a one-night adjustment, not a reversal of the order.

Everything else about the itinerary is negotiable. These three are not, and a booking made in the wrong order cannot be corrected by anything bought later.

The call

Inland first, the island in the middle, the airport's coast last. Open in Ubud or its villages, where the dawn waking of jet lag is spent among terraces and temples that are at their best at that hour and at their worst at eleven. Put the boat, if there is one, in the middle third, so the return crossing has at least one full day of slack behind it, two in July, August or the wet months. Finish on the south coast, seven to eleven road miles from the terminal, with no boat and no long drive between the last full day and the flight. The same ten nights in the reverse order spend the dawn hours asleep on a beach, put the only weather-dependent link against the flight home, and end twenty-two miles inland on the morning of a hard deadline. {{claim:crossing}}

Ask when to visit Bali and the internet answers with a month, which only works if a trip has one calendar. It has four. The rain follows one, the crowds follow a second, the surf moves from one coast to the other with the wind, and the sea under the boats is roughest in the very months the rain is lightest. Most people choose July because it is dry, and arrive in the island's fullest, roughest and most expensive weeks.

The reason this is a decision and not a lookup is that the four calendars are measured on different things and do not line up. Temperature is not one of them: the Denpasar station's normals hold between about seventy-nine and eighty-three degrees in every month, so the only thing the season changes is rain, and it changes that a great deal. The normals come from one station at sea level by the airport; they say nothing about Ubud, the mountains or the north, and nothing about how many days it rains or for how long, so the site prints the totals and refuses to describe a typical day. ●

The crowd follows the school year, not the sky. July and August are the driest months and the northern summer vacation, so weather and calendars point at the same eight weeks; late December is wet and full at once. The site has no official visitor or price statistics by month and gives none, which is itself the finding: the shoulder windows of May, June and September are drier than most people assume and emptier than July, and the gain is space, not a number. ●

The surf is the third calendar, and it is the one that moves the base. The wind reverses between seasons: the dry-season trades blow offshore on the west-facing coast and hold the waves up at Canggu and the Bukit; the wet-season north-westerlies do the same on the east, at Keramas, Sanur and Nusa Dua, and crumble the famous west-coast breaks. A surfer's January base and July base are on opposite sides of the island, and the drive between them takes time in the island's traffic. {{claim:surf-coast}}

The fourth calendar is the one first-timers never consult. Rain and swell are separate things, and the travel-guide sources the site relies on describe the sea between Bali and Lombok as roughest in July and August, when the swells are strongest; the fast-boat accident at Sanur in which two passengers died happened in August. A dry-season trip is not a calm-sea trip, and a July itinerary with an island leg needs the larger buffer, not the smaller one.

Finally the island's own dates, which move every year. In 2027 Nyepi falls on Monday 8 March inside a national holiday cluster that runs without a working day from the sixth to the fifteenth, and Galungan decorates the roads on 13 January and 11 August. None of these is a reason not to go; each is a reason to check the decree before the flights, which is Decision 08's subject.

The call

Take May, June or September if the dates are yours; if they are not, let the month choose the coast and the length. The rainfall normals and the crowd calendar point at the same eight weeks of July and August, and the sea under the boats is at its worst in exactly those weeks, so the dry peak buys sun at the price of company and a rougher strait. A surfer's base follows the wind: west and the Bukit in the dry season, Sanur and the east in the wet. A wet-season trip is a fine trip on a fortnight and a poor one on a week built around beaches, boats and a sunrise climb, because a short trip has no days to give to the weather. And any March trip is checked against Nyepi before the ticket is bought. {{claim:rain}}

  • then take the wet season → a fortnight, a traveller who values green and quiet over guaranteed sun, and a base with plenty under cover

Paperwork — What does the entry stack commit you to, and in what order?Decision 04 of 11

The visa question looks like one form and turns out to be a stack: a passport test, a visa, a provincial levy, an arrival card and a customs declaration, run by different offices on different websites with different deadlines. Each is easy. What goes wrong is the sequence, because one step has to be settled months ahead and one cannot be done until the last three days, and because several of them silently fix the outer edge of the trip.

The stack, in the order it must be done · official fees live on the Field Sheet
The stepWhat it fixes, and when it can be done
Passport ◇six months beyond the landing date, two blank visa pages; endorsement pages do not count; renew before buying anything
Visa, the e-VOA ◇same visa and price as the airport counter; thirty days, extendable once; apply after the flights and the final passport; valid for entry ninety days
Levy ◇a provincial charge on a separate site, by card, once per trip; a voucher per person; it can be asked for at a temple gate

The stack is five systems because it is three governments' worth of rules. The visa is national immigration law, the same for Bali as for Jakarta; the levy is a Bali provincial charge that has nothing to do with the right to enter; the arrival card is a national declaration to immigration, customs and health at once; and the customs allowances are a third office again. Paying for one does not cover the others, and many first-time visitors pay for the visa and assume they are done. {{claim:levy}}

The ordering problem starts with the passport, because it is the only item nothing else can fix. The visa does not cure a short passport; the levy and the arrival card are filled against the passport number; and a passport renewed after the e-VOA was bought leaves a visa issued for a document you no longer carry. The site's four-step check — find the expiry, add six months to the arrival date, count the empty visa pages leaving out the endorsement pages, confirm it is a full-validity passport — belongs before the flight purchase, for every traveller in the group, because a child's passport is issued for a shorter period and can be closer to the line than it feels. An emergency passport does not work with the visa on arrival at all. ◇

The visa fixes the outer edge of the trip in two ways most people notice late. It is thirty days, which is shorter than most calendar months, so a trip planned as "a month" can run past it by a day or two, and an expired visa is an overstay whatever the intention. {{claim:overstay}} And immigration expects an outbound ticket from Indonesia, which a one-way into Bali does not satisfy and a domestic hop to Lombok does not either; the ticket out of the country is the one to carry. A stay past thirty days means an extension, which since May 2025 includes a photograph and an interview at an immigration office, and an office is shut on the holiday clusters. {{claim:visa-ext}}

The arrival card is the step that cannot be done early. {{claim:arrival-card}} For an American the window is measured from the local date of landing, which is a day later than the date of departure, so the practical slot is the evening before leaving home or the layover airport. The card carries the customs declaration, and medicine is one of the items that sends a passenger to the red channel; declaring it is the expected behaviour and the inspection is short when the prescription is in your hand. Alcohol bought at the hub counts toward the one-litre allowance, and the two-for-one at the departure airport is a bad deal for a solo traveller, because the excess is destroyed on the spot. {{claim:customs}}

Three small government payments on three websites is also three sets of lookalike sites. The official addresses end in the government's own domains; a page that quotes more than the official amount is a third party's page, and the State Department's Indonesia page is the safest place to start from for all of them.

The call

Settle the passport first, then the shape of the trip, then the stack, in that order. {{claim:passport-6m}} If it fails that test the trip has a renewal in front of it, and the visa must be bought against the new number, not the old. Buy the e-VOA on the official site once the flights and the passport are final: it is the same visa as the airport counter at the same price, and it removes a queue from the worst hour of the journey. Pay the levy from home on the province's own site. Leave the arrival card for the last two days, counted from the local date you land, and screenshot the code. Declare the medicine. And read the stamp before walking away from the desk, because thirty days is counted, not rounded. {{claim:visa-required}}

When the other call is right
  • then buy the visa at the airport → a trip booked at short notice, an application that failed, or unease about a card on an unfamiliar site; it is the same visa, after a longer queue
  • then plan the extension in → a stay past thirty days known in advance; set the office visit about two weeks before day thirty, in a base near an immigration office
  • then get the IDP as well → any chance of renting a car; it is issued at home, and the decision to rent gets made on the spot
When ours is wrong for you
  • then leave before day thirty rather than extend → a route that passes through another country anyway; each re-entry is a new visa, a new card and the same passport rules
  • then carry the levy voucher on paper → a day of temple visits far from signal; the stated penalty is refusal at an attraction, not at the airport
  • then do the card at the hub → a routing with a long daytime layover inside the window; a flat battery at Denpasar is the case the printout is for
◆ The visa fee, the extension fee, the overstay fine, the levy and the customs figures are set in rupiah or by regulation and move; the Field Sheet carries each with the address it was read from and the date. The rule that does not move is the order.

Ride it yourself, ride pillion, or hire the driver?

The road — Ride it yourself, ride pillion, or hire the driver?Decision 05 of 11

The scooter is the cheapest way around Bali and the one every first-timer is handed within an hour of arriving, which is why the question is usually asked as 'is it safe'. It is the wrong question. The right one is a chain: the licence class you hold at home decides whether you are legal, being legal decides what a roadside stop costs, and both bear on what your insurer says after a crash. Every link was fixed before you left the United States.

Five ways to cover a road · scored on the chain, not on price

Rent a scooter ◇legal only with a motorcycle endorsement translated by an IDP; otherwise unlicensed, and the insurer may read the policy after the crash

The licence is the first link, and it is the one the rental shop will never check. A rental agreement is not a licence check; being handed a scooter is not evidence that you may ride it. Indonesia's traffic law requires a licence matching the type of vehicle driven and treats motorcycles as their own class, separate from cars. An American with a motorcycle endorsement at home and an International Driving Permit that reflects it holds what the law describes. An American with a car licence does not, and no official page the site could find says otherwise. {{claim:licence-fine}} Those are statutory maxima, not a roadside price list, and the State Department's advice if fined is to ask for an official receipt; it notes that travellers occasionally report requests for unofficial payments. ◇

The second link is physical, and the island's roads make it. Bali is where the State Department's description of Indonesian traffic is met every day: narrow roads through continuous built-up areas, shared with cars, trucks and large numbers of motorcycles that weave between vehicles and pass on either side. A car puts a steel body between you and the same traffic. The site's advice is blunt about experience: if you have never ridden a motorbike at home, Bali is a poor place to learn, and left-hand driving, heat and jet lag make it worse. ●

The third link shows up only when something goes wrong, and it is the expensive one. A travel policy may make cover for a motorbike accident depend on the rider being licensed for that vehicle; it may attach conditions about helmets, engine size, riding as a passenger and alcohol. The site cannot tell you what your policy says and neither can this book. What it can say is what the bill looks like without it: {{claim:medical-pay}} An unlicensed rider's position after a crash is weaker still, because {{claim:presumed}}

The middle options are better than the argument usually allows. Both apps offer a ride on the back of a motorbike, which gets you through traffic without your holding the handlebars or the liability, and a passenger in a hired car needs no licence of any kind: the requirement falls on the person driving. For a full day out, anything with luggage, any group with children and any journey after dark or in rain, a car with a driver is the site's answer, with the price, the hours, the stops and whether the car is a registered tourist vehicle agreed in writing the day before. For short hops in the south, the apps show the fare before you confirm. ●

Which leaves the base. The scooter looks attractive when the villa is far from everything, and a remote base makes it tempting in exactly the proportion that a central one makes it unnecessary. That is why this decision sits beside Decision 02 and not beside the packing list: a traveller who chooses a walkable base has already made it.

The call

Do not rent a scooter on a car-only licence. A driver for the days out, an app for the short hops, and your own two feet where the base allows it. {{claim:licence}} A permit translates the licence you hold; it cannot add a class. If your US licence carries no motorcycle endorsement the site's reading of the statute is that you are unlicensed on a scooter, and the question to put to your insurer in writing is whether you are covered riding one with the licence and permit you actually hold. The honest answer for most Americans is that the scooter is not a transport decision at all. It is a base decision: a villa so far from dinner that only a scooter reaches it is a villa in the wrong place.

When the other call is right
  • then rent → a licensed, experienced rider making short local trips from a base, in daylight, sober, helmet fastened, scooter photographed from every side, passport never left as the deposit
  • then self-drive a car → an IDP in hand and a reason, such as a family with its own car seat; book through an established company, as the 2025 circular requires
  • then ride pillion on an app bike → a solo traveller in the south with a short hop and no luggage; the helmet is the law, and the fare is on the screen
When ours is wrong for you
  • then even the driver is the wrong answer → Nusa Lembongan, where cars are not allowed without village permission; walk, cycle, or hire a ride, and apply the same licence law to the island's motorbikes
  • then no app either → the Bukit at sunset, a remote waterfall, a temple at dusk: arrange the ride back before you set out, because the app may match a ride out and not one home
  • then not today → the first two days, in rain, or after a drink; jet lag affects concentration, wet roads are harder, and the circular prohibits riding under the influence

The island leg

A day trip, island nights, the Gilis, or none — and where in the trip?

The island leg — A day trip, island nights, the Gilis, or none — and where in the trip?Decision 06 of 11

Every Bali itinerary of ten nights or more grows an island leg, and most of them put it at the end, because the beach is where a trip is supposed to finish. The leg is reached by small fast boat across open water, it has no fallback except a flight from Lombok, and the sea that decides whether it runs is roughest in the months the rain is lightest. Where it sits in the trip is therefore worth more than which island it is.

Four island legs · the days they take and the exposure they carry
Island legWhat it costs in days, and what the sea can take from it
Nusa Penida day trip from Sanur ●one day and two crossings; covers one side of the island in the day-trip crowd; needs a base on Sanur's side
Nusa Lembongan, two or three nights ●two crossings, days apart; a car-free island with Penida ten minutes away by local boat; a wet landing
Nusa Penida nights ●the cliffs early and late and both sides on separate days; rough roads, simpler services, luggage across on the boat
The Gilis, by boat or via Lombok ●two travel days whatever the route; the open strait or a flight plus a drive; earns its place on a fortnight or a return visit

The arithmetic starts with what each leg costs in days. A Penida day trip is one day and two crossings, and the site is firm about what it covers: one side of the island, west or east, and not both, because the roads are rough and the viewpoints are far apart. Island nights cost the crossings plus the transfer days around them, which on Lembongan is a morning boat out and a morning boat back with two full days between; a single night is spent mostly on boats and transfers. The Gilis cost two travel days in either direction whatever the route, because the short part of the journey is the crossing and the long part is getting to a port or an airport and from the far end to a bed. On a seven-night trip that leaves five days for everything else; on fourteen it leaves twelve. ●

Then the exposure. The Sanur route crosses the Badung Strait and comes under the harbour master's office, which issues a sailing approval for each departure and has told operators not to send boats out in poor weather, pointing them at the meteorological agency's wave forecasts. The state news agency reported waves above two and a half metres as the line at which boats on the route are at risk, and reported the capsizing at the harbour entrance in August 2025 in which two passengers died. A cancelled boat is the system working. The site invents no cancellation rate; what it says is that sea conditions do not always clear overnight, which is why it does not count an afternoon as a buffer. ◇

The placement follows from the two together. An island leg in the middle third of the trip turns a cancelled return crossing into an extra beach day with days of slack behind it. The same leg at the end turns every swell into a threat to the flight home, and from the Nusa islands the only way back is by sea. The Gilis have one more option, which is to cross to Lombok, drive the better part of two hours and fly; some travellers take the boat out, when a delay costs beach time, and fly back, when it would cost more. A domestic flight is a separate ticket with its own baggage rules, and the site's advice is never to put one on the same day as the flight to the United States. {{claim:domestic-bags}}

Which island is the smaller question. Lembongan is about eight square kilometres, nearly free of cars, built for visitors and ten minutes from Penida by local boat; Penida is thirty times the size, wilder, with rough roads and simpler services. The site's view is Lembongan for a first island stay, with Penida as a day from there, and that the Gilis, flatter and sandier and further, belong on a longer trip. Two spots carry specific warnings: rip currents at Dream Beach, and fatal falls at Devil's Tear, where the barriers are there for a reason. ●

The manta trip off Penida belongs to the same arithmetic. It is a small boat on the exposed side of the island, it is moved or cancelled when conditions are poor, and no operator controls a wild animal; it wants a spare day after it and suits confident swimmers who do not get seasick. Everyone else gets a better morning at the calmer snorkelling stops the same boats make, which many people find the better part anyway.

The call

On ten nights, three on Nusa Lembongan with a day on Penida, placed in the middle third with the boat back at least one full day before the flight. On a week, a Penida day trip mid-trip or nothing. The Gilis on a fortnight or a return visit. {{claim:crossing}} Lembongan is the easier first island stay and the natural base for a Penida day, and the whole leg hangs on the morning boat from Sanur, so it favours a trip that passes through Sanur's side anyway. The buffer is the site's own margin: one full day between the return crossing and an international flight, two in July, August or the wet months. A boat landing at midday before an evening flight is not a buffer; it is a bet that the morning sailing ran. {{claim:strait}}

When the other call is right
  • then nights on Penida → ten nights or more and photographs without the day-trip crowd are the point; both sides on separate days, and a car with a local driver on the rough roads
  • then the Gilis → a fortnight or more, with the leg in the middle; fly via Lombok in July, August or the wet season, or if small fast boats are not for you
  • then the day trip after all → a week, a base on Sanur's side, a morning boat and a return that lands well before dark, with nothing fixed that evening
When ours is wrong for you
  • then no island → a wet-season week, a traveller uneasy in small boats, or limited mobility; the wet landing and the rough paths are real, and Sidemen or Amed give the quiet without the sea
  • then the island nearer the end → a Gili leg flown back from Lombok two days before the international flight; the strait is out of the chain, the domestic flight is not
  • then Lembongan as the beach base itself → a ten-night trip that would otherwise end in the south anyway; return to Sanur with two nights left, and spend them there without a transfer
◆ Crossing times, ports and the wave forecast are on the Field Sheet with their sources; none is a timetable. The buffer is the site's own margin, not an official rule, and it is the part of this decision that survives any change in the operators.

Bali is small by American standards and every sight looks like a short drive from every other. In miles that is true: the airport is twenty-two from Ubud, Ubud is twenty-seven from the volcano, the four famous temples are in four corners of an island eighty-nine miles wide. In hours it is not, and the site refuses to print a single drive time because the number would be wrong for most of the people reading it. The day is planned from a rule, not a clock.

The reason the day is a decision is that the site's distances are stable and the hours are not, and a first-timer reads the one as the other. The airport is four road miles from Kuta, seven from Seminyak, twelve from Canggu, eleven from Sanur and thirteen from Uluwatu, and a newcomer reads those as ten-minute drives. The roads in the south run through continuous built-up areas and are shared with motorcycles that pass on either side; the routes into the hills are narrow and winding; a procession or a temple ceremony can close a road for a period on no timetable a visitor can consult; and heavy rain slows everything, so a drive in January wants more margin than the same drive in July. None of that is bad luck. It is structural, and the site's method is to check a live map set to the hour you will actually travel, and to ask someone who makes the journey every week. ●

The day therefore holds fewer stops than the map suggests, and the stops have to lie in one direction. A base in the far south puts the central highlands at the end of a long drive; Uluwatu to Ubud is about thirty-four miles through the busiest roads on the island, and a traveller who wants several days in the hills does better to move base than to commute. The same arithmetic sorts the four famous temples: Uluwatu and Tanah Lot are easy from the south, though not together in one sunset; Besakih and Lempuyang belong to Ubud and the east. Few trips need all four, and Lempuyang, in the site's view, is worth the journey for travellers already staying around Amed or Sidemen and hard to justify from the south for one photograph that is taken by numbered turn, with a mirror held under the phone, after a wait that can run to hours. ●

The volcano is the day that most depends on the base. Travel guides put the Batur climb at about two hours from Toya Bungkah with a required local guide, and the sunrise schedule means being collected from the hotel in the middle of the night; the further south you sleep, the earlier the car comes, and the site's advice is not to attempt it from the far south at all. From Ubud the drive is shorter by a wide margin, and a trip of a week can fit it on the second or third Ubud morning, never the first, with the rest of that day kept free. Cloud decides the view, and the caldera rim at Penelokan gives it without the night walk. {{claim:batur}}

Waterfalls follow the same logic with stairs added. Almost every one sits at the bottom of a valley, so the day is a walk down, time at the water and a climb back in humid air, and the site's rule is two or three in one direction, paired with a terrace or a temple rather than a fourth fall. The Ubud group is the easy set; the dramatic ones are in the north and belong to a stay in Munduk, not a day from the coast. Swimming after heavy rain is the thing to skip, since a river can rise quickly and the CDC's advice on floodwater applies. ●

The driver is the instrument. A car and its driver for the day, with the price, the hours, fuel, parking, the list of stops and the pickup time in a message you can both refer to, is how the site's day-plans are run, and the local knowledge of which order to do the stops in is the thing you are paying for. A driver you have used before is also the right person for the airport run, which is the one day with a deadline.

The call

Three or four stops, in one direction, chosen so the route never doubles back through the busy south, with the drive home counted as part of the day. That is the site's rule and it replaces the clock. Group the sights by the base they are a short outing from: the terraces, the spring temples and the volcano from Ubud; Uluwatu from the Bukit or Seminyak; Tanah Lot from Canggu; Besakih from Ubud or Sidemen; Lempuyang only from Amed. Leave early, so the return drive falls before the late afternoon, when beachgoers, commuters and sunset-seekers are all moving at once. And check a live map for the actual hour and the actual day, the night before, then believe the longer of its answer and your driver's. {{claim:distances}}

  • then one stop → the Batur climb, the manta boat, a Penida day; each is a whole day with the rest of it kept free
  • then do it from the south → Uluwatu at sunset from the Bukit, Tanah Lot from Canggu; the rest of the island waits for the Ubud nights

Calendar — Your dates touch March. Through Nyepi, or around it?Decision 08 of 11

Nyepi looks like a single date to avoid or to seek out, and the blogs treat it as a curiosity: a day of silence, lights off, a quiet hotel. For a traveller from the United States it is a different object. The airport halts regular flights for twenty-four hours, mobile data is switched off, the date moves every year, and in 2027 it falls inside a national holiday cluster that runs nine days without a working day. The flight that crosses the date line is the one most exposed to it.

Date in 2027What it does to a trip
Monday 8 March · Nyepi ◇regular flights stop from six to six; mobile data and broadcasts off; residents and visitors stay inside their accommodation
Wednesday 10 – Thursday 11 March · Eid al-Fitr ◇a national holiday, with collective leave on the 9th, 12th and 15th: no working day from Saturday the 6th to Monday the 15th

The reason Nyepi is a decision rather than a note is that it is the only day on the Indonesian calendar that comes with island-wide restrictions on visitors, and the restrictions land on exactly the systems a long-haul traveller depends on. Under a circular from the communications ministry, mobile data and broadcast television and radio are switched off for twenty-four hours from six in the morning, with hospitals and vital services exempt and fixed-line connections not covered; the operators text customers beforehand. For Nyepi 2026 the airport's general manager announced that all regular commercial flights would halt for the same twenty-four hours, and the annual joint appeal from Bali's religious and provincial authorities asks residents and tourists alike not to travel or leave their accommodation. Ride apps need data, so the edges of the window are the only hours a car can be booked. ◇

The six-to-six window is what makes an American itinerary the exposed one. The closure reaches into the nights on both sides, so a flight due to land late the evening before, or to leave early the morning after, sits close to the edge. The journey from the United States crosses the date line and takes more than a day, so a connection that slips by a few hours can turn a safe arrival into one on the wrong side of the closure, with the visa clock and the onward-ticket rule unaffected by the reason. That is the site's argument for a full day of margin on the way in, and for sleeping the Nyepi night in a place chosen for it, with meals and water arranged, since nobody goes out for either. ●

The 2027 cluster changes the arithmetic around it. The national holidays are fixed each year by a joint decree of three ministers, published the year before; the 2027 list was signed in September 2026, and it puts Eid al-Fitr two days after Nyepi with collective leave on the days between and after, so that the holidays and weekends run without a working day from Saturday 6 to Monday 15 March. The site has no official source for which shops or attractions close on which holiday and states none; what the calendar itself settles is that government offices are shut, which matters to anyone whose first thirty days end near the cluster and who needs the immigration office for an extension. {{claim:galungan-2027}}

Galungan is the opposite case and the one people confuse with it. It follows the 210-day Pawukon calendar, so it comes twice in most years and in a different month each time; it is not a national holiday, the site found no restriction on visitors attached to it, and what changes is that Balinese families are at home or at the temple, so a particular driver or guide may not be working. The sensible version of the day is a morning in a village looking at the penjor, a light itinerary, and bookings confirmed a few days before. A procession on the road has the right of way; wait for it.

The rule the site gives is short. Look up that year's Nyepi first, because it is the only date that can stop an arrival or a departure. Then look at the national list for long weekends, and at the Galungan dates if you would like to see the festival. The decree is the source; the blog that gives last year's date is how a trip lands on the wrong side of the window.

The call

Around it, unless you are coming for it; and if you are, land a full day before, not the evening before, and sleep that night somewhere you would be content to spend twenty-four hours. {{claim:nyepi-2027}} A day lost that way is a seventh of a one-week trip, and the 2027 cluster means the week around it is a holiday for the whole country, with rooms and transport booked earlier and side trips treated with more care. Work out the arrival date in Bali time, since the calendar date you leave home is not the date you land, and check it against the decree, not a blog: Nyepi fell on 19 March in 2026 and on 8 March in 2027. Download the maps and tickets the day before; plan any ride for the edges of the window; and confirm your own flight with the airline, since the 2027 airport hours were not announced when the site wrote this. {{claim:holidays-2027}}

  • then go through it → a day with no traffic and no aircraft is rare anywhere, and some travellers plan for it on purpose; arrive a full day before, book early, and choose the hotel for the night it is
  • then plan around the cluster, not just the day → a stay whose first thirty days end in March; start the extension earlier, because the office is shut for most of that week
  • then choose a Galungan week → the penjor are up, the restriction does not exist, and the only cost is a driver who may be with his family
When ours is wrong for you
  • then move the dates → a one-week trip; the closure takes a seventh of it, and the holiday cluster around it takes some of the rest in rooms and traffic
  • then at least move the flights → the dates are fixed by work but the arrival and departure are not; shift both a full day clear of the window and confirm with the airline
  • then keep the boat out of that week → an island leg against a national holiday cluster on both sides of the closure; the margin the sea needs is the margin the calendar has already spent

The flight

Which hub, one ticket or two, and what hour do you land?

The flight — Which hub, one ticket or two, and what hour do you land?Decision 09 of 11

The flight looks like a price comparison and is three decisions wearing one fare. The hub decides how long the final leg is and how many flights a day could rescue a missed one. The ticket decides who owns the problem when the first flight is late. And the arrival hour, which nobody compares, decides whether the first night is spent four miles from the terminal or twenty-two miles inland in the dark after twenty-four hours of travel.

Through Hong Kong, Taipei, Tokyo or Seoul ●the Pacific line from the West Coast; five to eight hours in

The hub is a real choice because the site's schedule snapshot for Bali's airport lists dozens of international routes and no American city among them, so every itinerary has a connection and the question is only where. From the West Coast the East Asian hubs sit on the straight line to Indonesia; from the East Coast and the Midwest, both directions are possible. The Department of Transportation's tests for any connection apply to an Asian hub as much as a domestic one: prefer the least congested connecting airport, consider its weather, and ask what happens if the first flight is delayed. The site gives no minimum connection time in minutes because none is official for each hub; its rule is a connection that would still work if the first flight landed an hour late, and anything tighter is a risk you are choosing. {{claim:hubs}}

The ticket decides who owns that risk. {{claim:bags-dot}} With two separate bookings the question of what happens when the first flight is late has two airlines to answer it, and the site could find no official source on how any airline treats a passenger stranded by another's delay, so it will not describe one; the advice is to ask the second airline before buying, and to assume the bag will not be transferred, which means collecting it at the hub, entering that country, and checking in again as a new passenger. The Montreal Convention lets a passenger claim delay expenses afterwards, with receipts. It is not a seat on the next flight. ◇

The return is the same exposure in reverse, and the more expensive one, because the short flight out of Bali has to arrive in time for the long flight home and a missed long-haul seat is the dearer thing to replace. A split ticket wants at least as much room on the way back as on the way out, which is one more reason the site's day-plans end on the coast near the terminal and keep boats and domestic flights out of the last day. ●

The arrival hour is the decision nobody compares, and the site is explicit that it matters most. Bali is eight hours ahead of UTC with no clock change, so a journey that leaves on a Friday evening can land on a Sunday, and the hotel night in between is paid for and never slept in. The CDC's advice after landing is to follow the destination's schedule and get daylight, which a daytime arrival allows and a midnight one does not. The site's own routes assume a morning or midday arrival and open in Ubud; its one allowed reversal is for a late-night landing, where the first night goes to the shortest drive from the terminal and the move inland waits for the morning. ●

Two further items ride on the flight booking and are missed if it is treated as an errand. The visa application asks for the departure date and a booked return flight, and immigration can ask for the outbound ticket on arrival, so a one-way into Bali needs its second booking before the first is useful. And the layover country has entry rules of its own: a transit that stays inside the terminal is one thing, a bag collected at the hub is an entry, and an entry stamp uses part of a page that Indonesia's two-blank-page rule was counting on.

The call

One ticket, through the hub with the shortest final leg your home airport allows, on a connection that would survive an hour's delay, landing at an hour you have already matched to the first night's distance. {{claim:nonstop}} For a first trip the site's advice is the through ticket; two bookings move the risk of a missed flight and a stranded bag from the airline to you, and the only safe way to use them is an overnight at the hub, which costs a hotel night and requires that you can enter that country. Then read the arrival time in Bali's clock: a daytime landing can go anywhere; a dusk landing goes to the south coast; a late-night landing goes to the shortest drive there is, and moves on after breakfast. {{claim:timezone}}

When the other call is right
  • then two tickets → an experienced traveller who has priced the saving against a night at the hub and can enter that country; leave many hours, confirm the cabin allowance of the second airline, and carry the second booking's confirmation
  • then a stopover on purpose → a fortnight or more, where a night in the hub is a small share of the holiday and puts part of the jet-lag adjustment somewhere worth seeing
  • then fly east → an East Coast or Midwest departure where the Gulf routing is as short and the connection is looser
When ours is wrong for you
  • then not Singapore after all → the long first flight is the problem for you; Tokyo or Seoul split the journey, at the price of a longer second leg
  • then not an evening landing → a solo traveller or a family who would rather pay for a daytime arrival than negotiate a dark terminal and a transfer at midnight
  • then no layover city at all → a one-week trip; a night at the hub is a seventh of it, and most travellers push through
◆ The hub list and the second-leg hours are a schedule snapshot dated October 2026 and are on the Field Sheet as such; routes change by season. The ticket rule and the arrival rule do not.

Health

What breaks the chain: the medicine bag, the deposit, or the policy?

Health — What breaks the chain: the medicine bag, the deposit, or the policy?Decision 10 of 11

Each piece of the health question is manageable on its own: a stomach bug is fluids, a mosquito is repellent, a scratch is a clinic, a pill is a prescription. What a traveller needs is how they connect when something goes wrong, because the connections are where the money is. The pill that was legal at the pharmacy is a narcotic at the border; the clinic wants a deposit before it looks at you; the policy reads its scooter clause after the crash.

The chain, link by link · what each one costs if it fails
The linkWhat the site could establish, and what it refuses to
The medicine bag ◇ninety days of prescribed medicine; sixty of psychotropics; the narcotic class not at all, prescription or not
The border ◇medicine is a red-channel item, declared and shown with the prescription; no list exists to look yours up
The clinic ◇a deposit before treatment, mostly cash; an ambulance can take forty-five minutes, so a taxi is the ambulance

The first link is the one most likely to catch an American, because it is decided by class and not by legality at home. Indonesia's food and drug agency writes its guidance by class of drug: ordinary prescription medicine for up to ninety days' use with a prescription, psychotropic medicine for sixty, and narcotic-class medicine and precursors not at all, with or without a prescription. The uncomfortable part is that customs publishes no list of prohibited medicines and the agency names classes, not products, so no article can tell you drug by drug. {{claim:cbd}} A gummy for sleep or a cartridge in a jacket pocket is the case that catches people, and it is a criminal matter, not a customs one. ◇

The clinic is the second link, and the State Department's description is the one to plan from: routine care in the major cities, adequate care harder to find in rural areas, most foreigners with the means leaving the country for anything beyond basic procedures, and a bill that starts before the treatment. {{claim:medical-pay}} The site quotes no price for a consultation, a night or a deposit, because it has no official source and the figures online vary widely; what it says is to know how you would raise a deposit at short notice, which cards you carry, and what your daily withdrawal limit is. {{claim:ambulance}}

The policy is the third link and the one that is read too late. Insurance in Indonesia is usually a way of getting money back, not a card that makes a bill disappear, so the claim is built from the medical report, the itemised bill, proof of payment and, after an accident, a police report. Two clauses decide whether it helps at all. The scooter clause may make cover depend on the rider being licensed for that vehicle, which for a car-only licence is Decision 05's problem. The evacuation clause matters because the State Department says an injured traveller will likely need to reach a major city and patients pay all transfer costs; what triggers it, who decides, and where it would take you differ from one policy to the next, and a policy bought for cancellation alone may contain very little medical cover. ◇

The animal link is the one with a decision in front of the flight. The CDC frames the pre-travel rabies vaccine as two questions, whether your activities raise the chance of contact and whether you could reach prompt treatment, and says vaccines are typically available after an exposure; the vaccine does not remove the need for treatment, it simplifies it. Whatever was decided at home, the rule on the island is fixed: wash the wound with soap and running water and go to a clinic the same day, whether or not the animal looked healthy. Monkeys at the temples are the common case, and the forest's rule is no food in hand and bags closed. {{claim:rabies}}

The habits close the chain rather than start it. Travellers' diarrhoea is the commonest thing that goes wrong and is mostly fluids; the site prints no percentage of visitors who get it, because none is official. The mosquito question in Bali is dengue and Zika, not malaria: {{claim:malaria}} Dengue's mosquitoes bite by day, there is no vaccine available in the United States, and the warning signs come in the two days after the fever breaks, which is the wrong moment to stop watching. The drink question is methanol and spiking, and the State Department's three rules cost nothing: watch it poured, accept none from strangers, leave none unattended. ◇

The call

Settle the chain at home, in this order: the medicine's class, the policy's two clauses, the clinic's address, and then the habits. {{claim:meds}} {{claim:adhd}} So the work is yours: find which class your own prescription falls in, ask your doctor what you would switch to, carry everything in its original packaging with the prescription and a letter by generic name, and leave every cannabis and CBD product behind. Read the scooter clause and the evacuation clause of any policy before paying, because {{claim:evacuation}} Ask your accommodation on the first day which clinic it would send a guest to and how you would get there at night. Then the habits: sealed water, food served hot, repellent by day as well as dusk, no touching any animal, and spirits only from a sealed, labelled bottle.

When the other call is right
  • then get the rabies vaccine → remote parts of the island, small children who reach for animals, or a long stay; it is a series of shots and a question for your insurer as well as your doctor
  • then switch the prescription → a stimulant or a strong painkiller; the alternative is agreed with your doctor at home, since a banned drug cannot be replaced on the island
  • then buy the policy for the evacuation alone → a trip with diving, a volcano climb or an island leg; check the activity list and the altitude and depth limits by name
When ours is wrong for you
  • then skip the clinic visit → a scratch that did not break the skin, washed anyway; anything that did is seen the same day, and children and older travellers sooner
  • then not the pre-travel vaccine → a short stay in the resort south with no animal contact planned; the post-exposure route is the one the CDC says is typically available
  • then the antibiotics stay in the bag → a mild stomach bug; the CDC reserves them for severe cases, and the decision belongs to your own doctor before the trip, not a pharmacy counter abroad
◆ The advisory level, the CDC's Zika notice, the emergency numbers and the consular agency's number are on the Field Sheet with their addresses; notices change, so read the date on the one you open. The medicine classes are the agency's; the drug-by-drug answer is the reader's to get, and the site says so.

Money — Which card, how much cash, and where do the small percentages land?Decision 11 of 11

Bali is cheap, which is why nobody looks at the money carefully. The site refuses to print a daily budget and quotes almost no price, and what it finds instead is a structure: no single item is large, and six small percentages land on the same dollar. Two of them are decided at home, by which card you carry and what your bank charges. The rest are decided by reading the foot of the menu before ordering.

The structure starts with what an American cannot use. The QR codes at every counter belong to the national standard run under the central bank, and the island's 2025 rules tell visitors to pay with them and to transact in rupiah. The cross-border version that lets a foreigner scan one with a home banking app connects a short list of Asian countries, and the United States appears nowhere on the central bank's page; a phone camera reads the code and moves no money. The site could not settle from an official source whether a short-stay American can open and fund a local wallet, so it refuses to build a trip on a workaround. That leaves cards and cash as the two working methods, which is the whole of the decision. ◇

The two percentages decided at home are the easy ones. A foreign transaction fee is set by your own bank, applies to every purchase in rupiah including the visa and the levy paid from your kitchen table, and is removed by carrying a card that does not charge it. Dynamic currency conversion is the terminal's offer to bill you in dollars at its own rate, and the site's advice is the same every time: choose rupiah. Neither is a Bali fee; both are choices made before the plane leaves. ●

The percentages on the bill are regulations, and they explain why the menu price is rarely the price. {{claim:tax}} A service charge is a different thing from the tax: under an Indonesian labour regulation it must be distributed to the staff, half shared equally and the rest by seniority and performance, and the regulation sets no rate, so the figure is the business's own. A price quoted "plus plus" carries both. The site's practical rule is to look for the line at the foot of the menu, to ask if it is not there, and to compare rooms and villas on the final figure for your dates. The entertainment category is the outlier, set at forty percent by the two regencies' 2023 regulations from January 2024 and publicly contested since; the site cannot tell you how any venue is classified, only to read how it states its prices. ◇

Cash is where the counting happens. Prices are written in thousands and millions and sometimes with the thousands dropped, so a hundred and fifty is a hundred and fifty thousand, and the notes are a factor of ten apart on one digit. The State Department says ATMs accept US-issued cards and may charge a conversion or transaction fee, advises machines in secure locations such as major bank branches, and the site has no official source for operator fees or withdrawal limits and quotes none; the half you can check before you fly is your own bank's schedule. At the changer, agree the amount first, count the notes yourself in front of the cashier, and do not let anyone recount them after you have. {{claim:cash-declare}}

Tipping is where the structure becomes manners. Where a service charge is on a hotel bill, part of what you paid already reaches the staff and an extra is a personal thank-you; where none appears, nothing has gone to them beyond wages. A driver who spent the day with you, a guide on a climb, a therapist, a porter: cash in rupiah, in the hand. The agreed price for anything without a posted one is the whole price, and a tip on top is a gift, which is why the site's advice is to agree the number before the day starts rather than negotiate it at the end. Foreign coins are the least useful thing to leave anyone.

The call

Two cards on different networks, one of them without a foreign fee; rupiah cash counted by the zeros for everything small; and no plan that depends on a QR code. {{claim:qris}} So the official payments go on the card from home, the large items go on the card on the island, and the small ones go in cash withdrawn at a bank branch or changed at a counter that shows its licence. {{claim:changer}} When the terminal offers dollars, decline. Read the foot of the menu for the line that says whether tax and service are included, and compare two rooms on the total, since the margin between them can be that line alone. Tip in rupiah, in the hand of the person you mean to thank, and only where no service charge has already done part of the job.

When the other call is right
  • then carry more cash → the far side of the island, the Nusa islands and the Gilis, where card acceptance is not something to count on and the ATM may be a boat away
  • then take the card to the counter anyway → the official sites: the visa takes Visa, Mastercard and JCB; the levy adds American Express; both charge in rupiah
  • then pay the villa by card through a platform → any deposit to a private host; a bank transfer to a stranger is the money the site says is hardest to recover
When ours is wrong for you
  • then not the ATM at the airport for the whole trip → enough for the first day's transport and a meal, then a bank branch; the site's advice is secure machines and a check for tampering
  • then no tip at all → a bill that already carries a service charge at a hotel restaurant; the staff share in it by regulation
  • then not in dollars → a seller who quotes a price in US dollars; the island's rules require rupiah, and the rate they apply on the day is theirs
◆ The tax rates, the entertainment rate and its status, the cash-declaration threshold and the customs allowances are on the Field Sheet with their sources. The exchange rate is not printed anywhere in this book: check it the week you travel and make one rule of thumb for what a hundred thousand rupiah is worth.

Back matter

What this book will not tell you

What this book will not tell youBack matter

The refusals are the product. They are what keeps the rest of it true.

Five things this book deliberately does not do. Each is a thing most guides lead with, and each is a thing we would have to get wrong in order to include.

  • It does not name hotels or villas. We have not slept in them. An assessment of a room we have not been in is a repackaged booking-site listing with a confident voice on top, and you can read those for free. What the book does instead is tell you which part of the island to sleep in and in what order, which is the decision that survives a change of ownership, and the four questions to put to any listing before paying.
  • It does not name restaurants, beach clubs or boat operators. A list is wrong within a season: the cook moves, the club changes its minimum, the operator changes its boat. The rules that generated the list are still right in five years (food cooked to order and served hot, a minimum spend asked per person or per bed before or after tax, a named boat with a life jacket per seat) so the book gives you the rules.
  • It does not print a drive time. The site measured every road distance on the island and refused to print a single duration, because the number would be wrong at the hour you read it. The book inherits that refusal. Miles are stable and are printed; hours are checked on a live map at your hour, the night before.
  • It does not print current fees or prices. Fees belong in the Field Sheet, which is dated and re-issued. Where a number is structural (the visa fee set in rupiah, the levy, the customs allowances, the tax rate) the Sheet carries it with the date it was read. The book carries no exchange rate and no daily budget, because the site has no official source for one and will not invent it.
  • It does not republish anyone's ratings. Aggregate scores from the booking platforms are contractually and editorially radioactive, and reprinting them would add nothing you cannot see for yourself.

Closing

The island, not the plan

The island, not the planClosing

There is a moment, usually on the second or third morning, when the planning stops mattering. It tends to arrive somewhere ordinary: a lane behind the paddies at six, with the offerings being set out on the doorsteps and nobody else about; an east-facing beach where the sun comes up over the water and the fishing boats are already back; a village road with a procession crossing it, and the car switched off to wait. Nothing about it is a sight. It is an island going about its day, at an hour most visitors are still asleep.

That is the thing the arithmetic is protecting. Every decision in this book exists to subtract a small anxiety from the fortnight (the boat that was not booked against the flight, the licence that was never tested, the last night that was close to the terminal) so that more of it is available for the parts that cannot be decided in advance: the warung with one dish and a queue, the beach you walked down four hundred steps to and had to yourself, the afternoon the rain came and you let it.

Work the sums out before you go. Then put the book down and stop optimising. The island keeps its own time, and it rewards the loose afternoon more than almost anywhere.
