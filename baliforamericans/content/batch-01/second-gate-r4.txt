=== ARTICLE 1: Do Americans Need a Visa for Bali? [do-americans-need-a-visa-for-bali] — handoff tag: turn, swamp: entry-stack ===
EXCERPT: Yes, US passport holders need a visa for Bali. Here is which one, how to get it, and the passport rules that turn Americans away at the airport.
<p>Yes. A US passport holder needs a visa to enter Bali, and for a vacation it is an easy one to get: you buy it online before you fly or at the airport when you land. What catches Americans out is rarely the visa itself. The trouble comes from the passport rules and from two other things you have to do that are not the visa at all.</p>

<h2>The short answer for US passport holders</h2>
<p>Americans visiting Bali as tourists need a visa, and the one that fits a normal vacation is Indonesia's B1 tourist visa, widely called the visa on arrival. The State Department puts it plainly: a visa is required. A US traveler can <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.type">buy it online in advance or at the airport on arrival</span>, and both routes produce the same visa.</p>
<p>Bali is a province of Indonesia, so everything here is Indonesian immigration law. There is no separate "Bali visa": the visa you need is the national Indonesian one.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Passport and boarding pass on a table, travel-planning feel, no readable personal details -->

<h3>What the visa lets you do</h3>
<p>The B1 visa covers tourism, visiting friends or family, business meetings and medical treatment. It does not cover work: <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.activities_allowed">selling goods or services, or being paid by anyone in Indonesia</span>, is prohibited. Paid work is not allowed on this visa, and the US Consular Agency reports a current enforcement campaign in Bali that includes visa violations.</p>

<h2>What the Bali visa costs and how long it lasts</h2>
<p>The Indonesian tourist visa for Americans costs <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.fee">IDR 500,000</span>, set in rupiah, so the dollar amount on your card statement moves with the exchange rate on the day you pay. The visa gives a stay of <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.max_stay_days">30 days, extendable once for another 30 days</span>.</p>

<h3>Thirty days is counted, not rounded</h3>
<p>Thirty days is not "a month", and the penalty for getting it wrong is steep: overstaying can bring a fine of <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.overstay_penalty">IDR 1,000,000 per day</span>, along with possible detention, deportation or a ban on coming back. If your trip runs close to the limit, count the days on a calendar and build in a margin.</p>

<h3>Staying longer than 30 days</h3>
<p>If you want more time, you extend once, for another 30 days. The official fee for that extension is <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.extension_fee">IDR 500,000 per application</span>. Start the process well before your first 30 days run out. An expired visa is an overstay, whatever your intentions were.</p>

<h2>Buying the visa online or at the airport</h2>
<p>American travelers can buy the Bali visa on Indonesia's official e-visa website before departure, or pay for it at the visa counter in the airport before reaching immigration. The State Department notes that the airport route can mean long lines. Buying online moves that step to your kitchen table, which matters most after twenty-plus hours of travel.</p>

<h3>The online route</h3>
<p>The official site is run by Indonesia's Directorate General of Immigration. You pay by Visa, Mastercard or JCB card, and one application can cover up to five travelers, which helps families. Once issued, the visa is good for entry for <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.validity_to_enter">90 days</span>, so there is no reason to leave it to the last week. You will be asked for your departure date and a booked return flight.</p>
<p>Use the official address only. Search results for this visa also show agencies that charge a service fee for filling in the same form.</p>

<h2>Passport rules that turn Americans away</h2>
<p>A US passport must be valid for at least <span class="fact" data-fact="documents.passport_validity">6 months beyond your arrival date and have 2 blank pages</span> to enter Bali. The US Consular Agency in Bali is blunt about it: immigration will not let you in otherwise. A visa does not fix a short passport.</p>

<h3>The blank-page detail people miss</h3>
<p>The endorsement pages at the back of a US passport do not count as blank pages. If your passport is nearly full, count the visa pages only. Check this before you book anything, while there is still time to renew.</p>

<h3>Emergency passports</h3>
<p>The visa on arrival is not available to a traveler on a limited-validity emergency passport. In that case you need <span class="fact" data-fact="documents.emergency_passport">a visa obtained before arrival</span> from an Indonesian embassy or consulate. This mostly affects people who lose a passport shortly before the trip and get a quick replacement.</p>

<h3>What immigration asks to see</h3>
<p>Beyond the passport, the officer can ask for <span class="fact" data-fact="documents.onward_ticket">an outbound ticket from Indonesia</span>. Have the booking on your phone and, ideally, on paper. You must also leave Indonesia on the same passport you entered with, which matters to dual nationals who carry two.</p>

<h2>Two things that are not the visa</h2>
<p>Getting into Bali takes three separate steps, and the visa is only the first. Indonesia also requires an arrival card from every traveler, and Bali charges its own tourist levy. Each lives on a different official website, and paying for one does not cover the others.</p>

<h3>The arrival card</h3>
<p>Every traveler completes an arrival card through Indonesia's All Indonesia system. It is free, it produces a QR code that you show to immigration and customs, and the form only opens shortly before you travel: fill it in during the last two days before you land.</p>

<h3>The Bali tourist levy</h3>
<p>Bali charges foreign visitors a levy of <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.tourist_levy">IDR 150,000 per person</span>, separate from the visa and paid cashless. It has its own rules and its own website, covered in our guide to the Bali tourist levy.</p>

<h2>What this decision actually sits inside</h2>
<p>Notice what happened across all of this. The visa question looked like a single form, and the answer turned out to be a stack: a visa, a passport test, an arrival card and a levy, run by different offices with different deadlines. Each one is easy. What goes wrong is the sequence: some steps cannot be done early, and one has to be settled months ahead, when you renew a passport.</p>
<p>The same stack also fixes the outer edge of your trip: how many days you can stay, and what proof of leaving you must already hold when you arrive. That ordering, from passport check to landing, is laid out step by step in <span class="guide" data-guide="name">the complete Bali trip guide</span>, next to the other connected decisions a first trip turns on.</p>

=== ARTICLE 2: The Bali Tourist Levy, Explained [bali-tourist-levy-explained] — handoff tag: seed, swamp: entry-stack ===
EXCERPT: Bali charges every foreign tourist a one-time levy that is separate from the visa. Who pays, how to pay it, and how to avoid the look-alike websites.
<p>Bali charges foreign tourists a one-time levy, separate from the visa, and you can pay it online before you leave home. The amount is small. What matters is knowing that it exists, paying it on the real website, and keeping the proof, because a tourist who has not paid can be refused service at the island's attractions.</p>

<h2>What the Bali tourist levy is</h2>
<p>The Bali tourist levy is a fee the provincial government charges international visitors to fund the protection of Balinese culture and the natural environment. The Bali tourist levy costs <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.tourist_levy">IDR 150,000 per person</span> and is set in rupiah, so its dollar value shifts with the exchange rate on the day you pay.</p>
<p>The levy is a Bali rule, not a national one. It rests on provincial regulations and applies to international tourists who travel to Bali, apart from a short list of exemptions, whichever airport or port that tourist used to enter Indonesia. Flying into Jakarta first does not change it.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Balinese temple gate or shrine with offerings, calm daylight, no recognizable faces -->

<h3>How it differs from the visa</h3>
<p>The visa is issued by Indonesia's national immigration service and lets you into the country. The levy is collected by Bali's provincial government and has nothing to do with your right to enter. They are paid on different websites, to different authorities, and paying one does not cover the other. Many first-time visitors pay for the visa and assume they are done.</p>

<h2>Who has to pay the Bali tourist levy</h2>
<p>International tourists who travel to Bali for tourism pay the levy, with a short list of exemptions, and the official guidance says plainly that cruise passengers who come ashore are included. The levy is paid <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.tourist_levy_terms">once per trip</span>, not once per day or per hotel, so a three-week stay costs the same as a long weekend.</p>

<h3>Who is exempt</h3>
<p>The exemptions are narrow and none of them describes an ordinary vacationer. The official list covers <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.tourist_levy_exemptions">holders of diplomatic and official visas, transport crew, holders of Indonesian stay permits, and holders of family unification, student and golden visas</span>. If you are flying in on a tourist visa, you pay.</p>

<h3>Leaving and coming back</h3>
<p>The official wording is that the levy is paid one time while traveling in Bali, before the person leaves Indonesia. Read plainly, a side trip from Bali to Lombok or the Gili Islands and back sits inside the same trip, because you have not left the country. A separate vacation next year is a new trip and a new payment.</p>

<h2>How to pay the Bali tourist levy</h2>
<p>The Bali tourist levy is paid cashless through the province's Love Bali system, and the simplest time to do it is online before you fly. Travelers can also pay at counters at Bali's airport and seaport on arrival, or through hotels, travel agents and attractions that are registered in the system. Cash is not accepted anywhere.</p>

<h3>Paying online before you go</h3>
<p>On the Love Bali website you enter your passport details and arrival date, then pay. Accepted methods include Visa, Mastercard, American Express and JCB cards. For an American, a credit card is the practical choice; the site also lists bank transfer and Indonesia's QR payment standard, and the United States is not among the countries connected to that QR scheme.</p>
<p>Use a card that does not charge a foreign transaction fee if you have one. The charge is processed in rupiah, and a card with a three percent foreign fee adds a little to every such payment on the trip.</p>

<h3>Your proof of payment</h3>
<p>After paying, you receive a levy voucher with a QR code by email. Save it somewhere you can reach without signal: a screenshot in your photos works. The levy is charged per person, so check that you have a voucher for each traveler in your group before you close the browser.</p>

<h3>Paying when you land</h3>
<p>If you forget, there are payment counters at the airport. This works, but it adds one more line at the end of a very long travel day, right after immigration and baggage claim. Paying from home costs nothing extra and removes the step.</p>

<h2>What happens if you do not pay</h2>
<p>A foreign tourist who has not paid the Bali levy can be refused service at tourist attractions under the Bali governor's 2025 circular on visitor conduct. In practice that means the voucher is something you may be asked to show at a temple or a ticket booth, not only at the airport.</p>

<h3>Checks happen away from the airport</h3>
<p>Because the stated consequence is <span class="fact" data-fact="visa.tourist_levy_enforcement">being refused service at tourist attractions</span>, the realistic place to be asked is at the entrance to somewhere you have driven an hour to see. Having the QR code on your phone turns that into a ten-second check.</p>
<p>Nothing official says how often the check happens, and that is a poor reason to skip the payment. The levy is small, the rule is clear, and the same circular that sets the penalty also tells residents how to report tourists who break the rules.</p>

<h2>Avoiding fake levy websites</h2>
<p>The only official place to pay the Bali tourist levy online is the provincial government's Love Bali site, at lovebali.baliprov.go.id. Search results for the levy also show third-party sites that take the payment on your behalf and add their own fee. The official address ends in baliprov.go.id, the domain of the Bali provincial government.</p>

<h3>How to tell the real one</h3>
<p>Check the address bar before you type a passport number. The official site charges the official amount. If a site quotes a higher total than the figure above, you are not on the government's page.</p>
<p>The same caution applies to the visa and the arrival card, which have their own official sites and their own imitators. Three small government payments, each with a separate website, is the kind of chore that is easy to get slightly wrong, and it is set out in order in <span class="guide" data-guide="name">the complete Bali trip guide</span>.</p>

=== ARTICLE 3: Bringing Medication into Bali and Indonesia [bringing-medication-into-bali] — handoff tag: seed, swamp: health-chain ===
EXCERPT: Most prescriptions can come to Bali with the right paperwork, but Indonesia bans one class of medicine outright. What to carry, and what to check before you fly.
<p>You can bring your own medication into Bali, and for most prescriptions the rule is simple: carry the prescription, keep the supply to what the trip needs, and expect customs to look. The hard edge is that Indonesia bans one whole class of medicine outright, and some drugs that are routine in the United States fall on the wrong side of that line.</p>

<h2>The rule in one paragraph</h2>
<p>Medication for personal use can enter Indonesia in a traveler's own baggage without advance approval from the health authorities, subject to conditions: a doctor's prescription, a declaration form, and limits that depend on the class of drug. Indonesia's food and drug agency allows a supply of up to <span class="fact" data-fact="safety_health.medication_import">90 days' use for prescribed medicine</span>, with a shorter limit for one class and an outright ban on another.</p>
<p>The agency responsible is BPOM, Indonesia's equivalent of the FDA. Its guidance is written by class of drug, not by brand name, and that is the detail that matters most for Americans, as you will see below.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Prescription pill bottles and a blister pack beside a travel bag, neutral background, no readable patient names -->

<h3>What "personal use" means here</h3>
<p>The allowance covers medicine you carry with you, in your own bags, for your own treatment. The rule described here is for medicine carried in a passenger's baggage, not for anything mailed or couriered. Whatever you need for the trip should travel with you from the first day.</p>

<h2>What counts as acceptable paperwork</h2>
<p>Prescription medicine brought into Indonesia from abroad must be supported by a doctor's prescription or a hospital recommendation, according to the food and drug agency; over-the-counter products have a small separate allowance, covered below. The agency also describes a special form with supporting documents for personal-use medicine, so the prescription is the document to have ready.</p>

<h3>What to carry</h3>
<p>The documented requirements are the prescription and the agency's form. Beyond that, our advice is practical, not official: keep each medicine in its original pharmacy packaging with your name on the label, carry a copy of the prescription for each item, and ask your doctor for a short letter listing each drug by its generic name, the dose and the condition treated. US brand names are often different abroad.</p>
<p>Keep everything in your carry-on, together, where you can hand it over in one motion. Loose pills in a weekly organizer are the worst case: nothing ties them to you or to a prescription.</p>

<h3>Over-the-counter medicine</h3>
<p>Ordinary non-prescription medicine has its own small allowance. BPOM permits over-the-counter products without a prescription for at most three days' need. For a longer trip, that means a large stock of non-prescription products is outside the allowance.</p>

<h2>The three classes and their limits</h2>
<p>Indonesia sorts medicine into classes with different limits. Ordinary prescription medicine is allowed for up to 90 days of use. Psychotropic medicine is allowed for up to 60 days of need. Narcotic-class medicine and precursors may not be brought in from abroad at all, with or without a prescription.</p>

<h3>Why this is the part to check first</h3>
<p>The ban on the narcotic class has no exception for a valid American prescription. A drug that your doctor prescribes and your pharmacy fills without comment can still be one Indonesia will not admit. The State Department says the same thing from the other side: <span class="fact" data-fact="safety_health.drug_laws">some prescription medications, including those for ADHD, are illegal in Indonesia</span>.</p>

<h3>No official list exists to look your drug up</h3>
<p>Here is the uncomfortable part. Indonesian customs does not publish a list of prohibited medicines, and the food and drug agency's guidance names classes, not products. We are not going to tell you that a particular US prescription is allowed or banned, because no official source we could open says so drug by drug.</p>
<p>What you can do is find out which class your own medication falls into. BPOM publishes a contact for individual questions about bringing medicine in for personal use, and your prescribing doctor can tell you whether a drug is a controlled substance and what the non-controlled alternatives are. Do this well before you travel.</p>

<h2>What happens at customs</h2>
<p>Medicines are one of the items that send an arriving passenger to the red channel at Indonesian customs, where baggage can be physically inspected. Declaring your medication is the expected behavior, not an admission of anything. Medicine that does not meet the rules can be seized and destroyed.</p>

<h3>Declare it</h3>
<p>The arrival card you complete before landing includes the customs declaration. Answer the medicine question honestly, then go where the officers direct you with your prescriptions in hand. Be ready to say what each medicine is and to show the prescription for it.</p>

<h3>If your medicine is refused</h3>
<p>The agency's wording is that non-compliant medicine can be seized and destroyed. That is why the realistic backup plan has to exist before you fly: know the generic name of what you take, ask your doctor what you would switch to if it were unavailable, and do not count on buying a replacement for a banned drug once you are there.</p>

<h2>Why the stakes are higher than a lost bottle</h2>
<p>Indonesia's drug laws are strict, and the State Department's warning covers prescriptions too: some medications that are legal with a US prescription are illegal in Indonesia. The State Department warns that convictions can bring heavy fines and long jail sentences. Marijuana, cannabis and CBD products are illegal in Indonesia, including products that are legal in your home state.</p>

<h3>CBD and medical marijuana</h3>
<p>This catches Americans more than any pill does. A CBD gummy for sleep or a cannabis vape cartridge is illegal there, and a prescription or medical card from a US state does not change what the State Department says about Indonesian law. Leave all of it at home, and check jacket pockets and toiletry bags before you pack.</p>

=== ARTICLE 4: What You Can Bring into Bali: Customs, Alcohol and Vapes [bali-customs-alcohol-and-vape-rules] — handoff tag: none, swamp: none ===
EXCERPT: Indonesian customs sets fixed allowances for alcohol, tobacco and vapes, and destroys anything over the limit. The numbers to know before you shop duty-free.
<p>Indonesian customs sets fixed allowances for alcohol, tobacco and vapes, and the rule for going over them surprises people: the excess is destroyed, not taxed and returned to you. There are also rules for the value of what you carry, for cash and for medicines. Knowing the numbers before you shop at duty-free saves both money and an awkward moment at the airport.</p>

<h2>The allowances at a glance</h2>
<p>Indonesian customs sets a per-person allowance for goods, alcohol, tobacco and e-cigarette products. An adult arriving in Bali may bring <span class="fact" data-fact="customs.alcohol_allowance">1 liter of alcohol</span>, a fixed quantity of tobacco or vape products, and personal goods up to a set value free of duty. The allowances are set per traveler, so each adult should carry their own.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Personal goods:</strong> <span class="fact" data-fact="customs.duty_free_goods_value">USD 500 per person per arrival</span>, duty-free</li>
  <li><strong>Alcohol:</strong> 1 liter per adult aged 21 or over</li>
  <li><strong>Tobacco:</strong> <span class="fact" data-fact="customs.tobacco_allowance">200 cigarettes, or 25 cigars, or 100 grams of tobacco</span></li>
  <li><strong>Vapes:</strong> <span class="fact" data-fact="customs.vape_allowance">30 ml of open-system liquid, or 12 ml of closed-system liquid</span>, for travelers 18 or over</li>
</ul>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Suitcase open on a bed being packed for a tropical trip, clothes and sunglasses, no logos -->

<h2>Alcohol: one liter, and a different age line</h2>
<p>An adult traveler may bring one liter of alcoholic drink into Indonesia free of excise, and the allowance applies only to passengers aged 21 and over. That is a single standard bottle of spirits or a little more than one bottle of wine per adult. A second duty-free bottle is over the limit.</p>

<h3>What happens to the extra bottle</h3>
<p>Customs does not charge you duty on the excess and let you keep it. Alcohol, tobacco or vape products above the allowance are <span class="fact" data-fact="customs.excess_destroyed">destroyed on the spot by the customs officer</span>. The two-for-one offer at the departure airport is a bad deal for a solo traveler flying to Bali.</p>

<h3>Couples and groups</h3>
<p>The allowance is per adult, so two adults traveling together can bring two liters between them. It is sensible for each person to carry their own bottle through customs. Travelers under 21 have no alcohol allowance, so a 19-year-old cannot carry a bottle for the family.</p>

<h2>Cigarettes and tobacco</h2>
<p>The tobacco allowance for an adult arriving in Indonesia is 200 cigarettes, or 25 cigars, or 100 grams of sliced or other tobacco. Those are alternatives, not a combined total. A traveler who brings more than one type gets a proportional share of each, so a full carton plus a box of cigars is over the limit.</p>

<h3>The one-carton rule of thumb</h3>
<p>Two hundred cigarettes is one standard carton. If you smoke, one carton per adult is the number to remember, and as with alcohol, the excess is destroyed, not taxed.</p>

<h2>Vapes and e-cigarettes</h2>
<p>E-cigarette products have their own customs allowance in Indonesia, available to passengers aged 18 and over. A traveler may bring 30 ml of liquid for open, refillable systems, or 12 ml for closed pod or cartridge systems, or 140 sticks or 40 capsules of solid heated-tobacco product.</p>

<h3>Thirty milliliters is less than it sounds</h3>
<p>A single common bottle of e-liquid in the United States holds 30 ml or more, and many hold 60 ml or 100 ml. One large bottle can put you over the allowance by itself. Closed pods are measured the same way: add up the milliliters printed on the packs before you fly.</p>

<h3>THC cartridges are a different matter entirely</h3>
<p>Nothing in the vape allowance covers cannabis. Marijuana, cannabis and CBD products are illegal in Indonesia, and the State Department warns that drug convictions there can bring heavy fines and long jail sentences. A THC cartridge left in a jacket pocket is a criminal problem, not a customs one. Check every bag and pocket before you leave home.</p>

<h2>The USD 500 goods allowance</h2>
<p>Personal goods worth up to USD 500 per person, per arrival, enter Indonesia free of import duty and tax. Above that value, customs charges 10% import duty plus value-added tax on the excess. The figure is set in US dollars in the regulation itself, so this is one Indonesian number that does not move with the exchange rate.</p>

<h3>Your own laptop and camera</h3>
<p>The allowance is aimed at goods you are importing, such as new purchases and gifts, and the customs rules treat goods for personal use differently from goods that are not. The red-channel list includes goods that are not for personal use, so several identical new items in sealed boxes are the kind of thing that invites questions in a way your own used laptop and camera do not.</p>

<h3>Gifts for people in Bali</h3>
<p>If you are carrying gifts, keep receipts handy and keep the total under the allowance, which is counted per person and per arrival.</p>

<h2>Cash, and the declaration threshold</h2>
<p>Cash is not capped at Indonesian customs, but it must be declared above a threshold. Money or other payment instruments worth <span class="fact" data-fact="customs.cash_declaration">IDR 100,000,000 or more</span>, in rupiah or the foreign-currency equivalent, must be declared on entry and exit. Failing to declare carries a penalty of 10% of the amount.</p>
<p>That threshold is far more cash than a vacation calls for, so for most travelers the rule is simply something to know exists. It is a declaration duty, not a limit: declared money is allowed in.</p>

<h2>The customs form and the two channels</h2>
<p>Arriving passengers declare their goods electronically before landing, as part of Indonesia's arrival card, and then walk through either a green channel or a red one. The red channel means physical inspection. Customs lists the things that send a passenger there, and several are easy to be carrying without thinking about it.</p>

<h3>What sends you to the red channel</h3>
<p>The official list includes goods above the duty-free value, animal and plant products, medicines, cash at or above the declaration threshold, goods not for personal use, and a few categories no tourist should be near, such as weapons. Being sent to the red channel is not an accusation. It is where declared items are looked at.</p>

<h3>Fill the form in honestly</h3>
<p>The declaration is part of the arrival card that every traveler completes in the last two days before landing. Answer the questions as they apply to you. Declaring what you carry is the expected behavior, and it keeps the inspection short.</p>

=== ARTICLE 5: Renting a Scooter in Bali: Licence, Insurance and Police Stops [renting-a-scooter-in-bali] — handoff tag: turn, swamp: scooter-liability ===
EXCERPT: Any shop in Bali will rent you a scooter. Whether you are legally allowed to ride it is a separate question, and it decides what a police stop or a crash costs you.
<p>Renting a scooter in Bali is easy, and that ease hides the real question. Being handed the keys says nothing about whether you are legally allowed to ride, and the gap between "they rented it to me" and "I was licensed to ride it" decides what a police stop or a crash will cost you.</p>

<h2>What an American legally needs to ride a scooter in Bali</h2>
<p>An American riding a scooter in Bali needs an International Driving Permit carried together with a US license, and Indonesian law requires that the license match the type of vehicle. The State Department says <span class="fact" data-fact="getting_around.driving_permit">a US driver's license alone is not recognized in Indonesia</span>, and a rental agreement does not change that.</p>

<h3>The permit</h3>
<p>The International Driving Permit is a booklet that translates your license; it is issued in the United States before you leave, and AAA, which issues it, lists Indonesia among the countries that honor it. Arrange it before you leave home. The permit has no force on its own, so the plastic license from your state travels with it.</p>

<h3>The part most visitors miss: the license class</h3>
<p>Indonesia's traffic law requires every driver to hold <span class="fact" data-fact="getting_around.licence_must_match_vehicle">a license matching the type of vehicle driven</span>, and it treats motorcycles as their own license class, separate from cars. A scooter is a motorcycle under that law. Follow the logic through: a US license that covers cars only does not match a scooter, and a permit cannot add a class your license does not have.</p>
<p>No official page we could find says a car-only license is enough for a scooter in Bali, and the statute points the other way. If your US license has no motorcycle endorsement, treat yourself as unlicensed on a scooter. That is the honest reading of the law.</p>

<!-- IMAGE_SLOT_1: Row of rental scooters parked on a Bali street with helmets on the seats, no readable plates or faces -->

<h2>What the rental shop will and will not check</h2>
<p>A rental agreement is not a license check. Do not read being handed a scooter as evidence that you may legally ride it. Your license status, your insurance and your riding experience are yours to sort out before you reach the counter.</p>

<h3>What to do before you ride away</h3>
<p>Photograph the scooter from every side, including existing scratches. Ask for the vehicle registration, since drivers must carry it. Test both brakes and the lights, and get a helmet that fits and fastens for you and any passenger.</p>

<h3>Never leave your passport as a deposit</h3>
<p>If a shop asks to hold your passport, decline and offer a cash deposit or a photocopy instead. Your passport is the one document you cannot replace quickly, and handing it over weakens your position in any dispute about damage.</p>

<h2>Police stops and what the law says about fines</h2>
<p>Police checks on foreign riders in Bali are part of a current enforcement campaign that the US Consular Agency describes as focused on traffic and vehicle violations, among other offenses. Indonesia's traffic law sets a maximum of <span class="fact" data-fact="getting_around.traffic_penalties">4 months' detention or a fine of IDR 1,000,000 for riding without the required license</span>, with lower maximums for not carrying one and for riding without a helmet.</p>

<h3>What officers look for</h3>
<p>The rules themselves are clear: a helmet for rider and passenger, a license and permit, the registration, and sober riding. Bali's 2025 rules for foreign visitors repeat that list and add that riders must not carry more passengers than the vehicle allows.</p>

<h3>If you are fined</h3>
<p>The State Department's advice is to ask for an official receipt and follow the local procedure; it notes that travelers occasionally report requests for unofficial payments. The amounts above are legal maximums, not a roadside price list.</p>

<h2>If you crash</h2>
<p>A crash is the risk that matters most on a scooter in Bali, and the legal position of a foreign rider is weaker than most expect. The State Department's guidance is that drivers involved in a traffic accident should stay at the scene and notify the police.</p>

<h3>Who gets blamed</h3>
<p>The State Department notes that in collisions between cars and motorcycles, the driver of the larger vehicle is often presumed responsible. That presumption is no shield for a rider who was not licensed to be on the scooter in the first place.</p>

<h3>The question to put to your insurer before you go</h3>
<p>Ask your travel insurer, in writing, one specific question: "Am I covered for injuries while riding a rented scooter in Indonesia if my US license has no motorcycle endorsement?" Policies differ, and only your own insurer can tell you where you stand. Get the reply by email and keep it.</p>

<h2>Whether you should rent one at all</h2>
<p>A scooter makes sense in Bali for a rider who already holds a motorcycle license at home, has ridden in traffic, and is staying somewhere the distances are short. For a first-time rider, Bali's roads are a poor place to learn; ride-hailing apps and cars with drivers are the alternatives.</p>

<h3>If you ride</h3>
<p>Wear a helmet with the strap fastened and closed shoes. Do not ride after drinking; Bali's own rules for visitors prohibit it. Avoid riding at night on roads you do not know.</p>

<h2>What this decision actually sits inside</h2>
<p>Notice what happened across all of this. The question started as "can I rent a scooter" and the answer was never about the scooter. It was a chain: the license you hold at home decides whether you are legal, being legal decides what a police stop costs, and both bear on what your insurer will say after a crash. Each link was fixed before you left the United States.</p>
