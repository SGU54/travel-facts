Bali belly is travelers' diarrhea, and the CDC calls it the most common travel-related illness. In an otherwise healthy adult it is rarely serious, but it can take days out of a trip. Prevention is about what you eat and drink, treatment is mostly fluids, and a doctor is needed when there is blood, fever, dehydration or no improvement.

What Bali belly is

Bali belly is the local name for travelers' diarrhea, a stomach illness picked up from contaminated food or water. The CDC lists Asia, with the exceptions of Japan and South Korea, among the highest-risk regions for it. The CDC also says that in otherwise healthy adults diarrhea is rarely serious or life-threatening, though it can make a trip very unpleasant.

Where it comes from

The source is contaminated food or drink. The State Department says that in many areas of Indonesia tap water is not safe to drink, and that impure water can cause diseases including typhoid, hepatitis and dysentery. Water reaches a traveler through ice and washed salads as well as through a tap.

Why we give no odds

Travel sites often quote a percentage of visitors who get sick in Bali. We have found no official figure for Bali, so we are not going to print one. The honest summary is the CDC's: travelers' diarrhea is common in this part of the world, and the precautions below lower the risk without removing it.

How to prevent Bali belly

Preventing Bali belly comes down to a short list of habits from the CDC. Eat only food that is cooked and served hot. Avoid food that has been sitting on a buffet. Drink only beverages from factory-sealed containers, avoid ice because it may have been made from unclean water, and wash your hands often with soap and water.

Water and ice

The State Department's advice for Indonesia is to drink bottled water from a major brand with the seal intact, and it notes that many restaurants and hotels serve tap water unless bottled water is specifically requested. It suggests asking for no ice. The CDC adds that teeth should be brushed with bottled or disinfected water.

Food

The CDC's guidance is to avoid raw foods and fresh salads, which may have been washed in contaminated water, and to prefer food that arrives hot. It advises against food from street vendors, and says that anyone who chooses to eat street food should apply the same rules. Fruit you peel yourself is the easy exception.

Hands

The CDC's instruction is to wash your hands often with soap and water, especially after using the bathroom and before eating. This is the least glamorous precaution and one that is easy to keep on a trip. Our advice is to carry hand sanitizer for the moments when there is no sink, such as a market or a boat.

How to treat Bali belly

Treating Bali belly starts with fluids. The CDC's advice is to drink lots of fluids to stay hydrated, and in serious cases to use oral rehydration solution to replace what is lost. Dehydration is what turns an unpleasant day into a medical problem, particularly in Bali's heat, so drinking comes before everything else.

Over-the-counter medicine

The CDC notes that several drugs, such as loperamide, can be bought over the counter to treat the symptoms of diarrhea. Our advice is to bring a small supply from home along with oral rehydration salts, since the State Department warns that counterfeit medication is common in Indonesia and may have the wrong strength or dangerous ingredients.

Antibiotics

According to the CDC, a doctor may prescribe antibiotics for travelers' diarrhea, but they should be considered only for severe cases. That is a conversation to have with your own doctor before the trip, not a decision to make at a pharmacy counter abroad.

Rest, and safe drinks only

While you are ill, every drink should be a safe one: sealed bottled water, oral rehydration solution, or tea served steaming hot. Our advice is to cancel the day's plans without guilt. Pushing on to a long drive or a boat crossing with a stomach bug makes both the illness and the day worse.

When to see a doctor

A doctor is the right next step for Bali belly when the illness is more than mild. Our advice, which is general and not a substitute for medical care, is to seek help for blood in the stool, a high fever, repeated vomiting, signs of dehydration, or symptoms that are not improving. Our advice is to have children and older travelers seen sooner.

What a clinic visit involves

The State Department says routine medical care is available in Indonesia's major cities, and that hospitals and doctors often require payment or a sizable deposit before service or admission. Most care providers accept only cash, and credit card payment is not always available. Take your passport, your insurance details and a way to pay.

If it continues after you fly home

Our advice to anyone still unwell after the trip is to see a doctor and to say where they traveled. A stomach problem that outlasts the trip needs that visit, because the cause may be something a short illness would not suggest.

What to pack for your stomach

A stomach kit for Bali is small and worth the space. These are our own suggestions, based on the CDC's treatment advice: oral rehydration salts, an over-the-counter diarrhea medicine such as loperamide, hand sanitizer, and any prescription your own doctor recommends for the trip. Keep it in your day bag, since that is where you will be when it is needed.

Medicine rules still apply

Whatever you pack has to enter Indonesia legally. Prescription medicine needs its prescription, with a limit of 90 days' use for prescribed medicine, and the State Department says all prescription medication must be declared on the arrival card. Over-the-counter medicine has its own smaller allowance.
