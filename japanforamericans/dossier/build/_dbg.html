The contract

What this book is for

What this book is forThe contract

A first trip to Japan is not hard. It is over-decided — and the decisions are made in the wrong order.

Japan is among the easiest countries an American can travel in. You need no visa for a short visit, the trains run to the second, the crime rate is low enough that the question barely arises, and the food is excellent at nearly every price. Almost nothing about the trip is difficult. What it is, instead, is over-decided: a dozen choices that each look small, presented in isolation by a hundred articles, and connected to each other in ways that only become visible after you have already committed to three of them.

This book exists because those connections are where the money and the days go. The season you pick sets the booking deadline. The booking deadline decides how many cities you can commit to. The number of cities decides whether a rail pass pays. Whether the pass pays decides whether you spend two hundred dollars you did not need to — and, worse, whether you then bend the route to justify the purchase. None of that is difficult. It is simply not answerable one article at a time, which is exactly how it is usually answered.

What we do and do not claim

Every call in here is argued in the open, with the arithmetic on the page, so that you can disagree with us and still come out ahead. That is the contract. Where we say buy, we show the sum that says buy; where the sum is close, we say it is close. A recommendation you cannot audit is worth nothing to someone spending four thousand dollars, and the number of places where a confident voice is simply repeating a decade-old blog post is higher in Japan than almost anywhere. ●

We do not tell you which hotel to book or which restaurant to eat at. Those are the two things every guide leads with and the two that age fastest — a list of names is wrong within a season, while the rule that generated the list keeps working. The back of this book says plainly what it refuses to do, and why.

Marks, and the two documentsHow to read it

Every claim in this book carries a mark that tells you how much weight it will still bear next year.

Travel writing rarely distinguishes between a fact that has been true for forty years and a price that was checked once in spring. This book marks the difference on every claim, because the two age at completely different rates and you are entitled to know which one you are being handed.

The book you are reading is built to stay true for about two years. It contains no live prices, because a price printed in a book is a lie with a long shelf life. Every volatile figure lives instead in the Field Sheet — a short dated annex, re-issued quarterly, that carries the numbers and nothing else. When the two disagree, the Sheet is right and the book is old.

Read the book once before you book anything. Carry the Sheet on the trip.

The ground

Two legs, and a pass that needs four

Two legs, and a pass that needs fourThe ground

One number reorganises a first trip to Japan, and almost nobody arrives knowing it.

The trip that nearly every first-timer takes has a shape so consistent it can be described in a sentence: fly into Tokyo, spend several days, take one Shinkansen to Kyoto, spend several more, fly home. Some variant of that is what the itinerary articles converge on, what the week allows, and what people come back having enjoyed. It contains exactly two long train journeys. ●

The Japan Rail Pass needs about four to pay for itself. ●

That collision is the single most useful thing to know before you plan anything else, because it is counter-intuitive in a specific and expensive way. For twenty years the pass was the correct reflex, and the entire English-language internet learned to recommend it. Then in October 2023 the seven-day ordinary pass rose from roughly ¥29,650 to ¥50,000 — about seventy per cent in a single step — and the advice did not move. ◇

Why the sum is the interesting part

Divide the pass price by the fare it is meant to save you and the answer falls out. A reserved seat from Tokyo to Kyoto is about ¥13,850, so the pass needs a little under four journeys of that length before it breaks even — roughly two round trips inside a seven-day window. A trip with two long legs in it is not close to the line. It is at half. ◇

The second-order cost is the one that does the real damage, and no price comparison catches it. A pass already paid for is an argument in your pocket for using it, and routes quietly bend to justify the purchase: a day trip added because it is free, a city inserted because the train is covered, a fortnight that was going to be two calm bases turning into five hotels and a lot of luggage on platforms. The purchase should follow the route. It usually leads it.

What the corridor looks like

Japan for a first-timer is not the archipelago of the map. It is a corridor — Tokyo and Kyoto, a little over two hours apart on the fastest services the pass does not cover, with everything else reached as a day trip from one end or the other. Nara is forty-five minutes from Kyoto. Hakone, Nikko and Kamakura are inside two hours of Tokyo. ● Those spokes run on local and rapid lines at a fraction of bullet-train prices, which means the pass does not help with them either.

Once the corridor is the mental model rather than the country, most of the remaining decisions in this book get easier, and several of them stop being decisions at all.

Before you fly

The four things with deadlines

The four things with deadlinesBefore you fly

Most of the preparation is optional. These four are not, and each has a date attached.

Japan asks very little of an American traveller in advance — no visa, no vaccination paperwork, no entry fee. ◇ What it does have is a small set of tasks whose cost rises sharply the later you do them, and which are easy to leave until the week before because nothing forces them earlier.

What has a deadline · counted back from departure
TaskWhen
Rooms, if you are travelling in bloom season ●~6 months
Rooms, late November foliage ●2–3 months
A ryokan you actually want ●2–3 months
Transit card created and topped up ▲before you board

The transit card is the one that looks trivial and is not. Creating a Suica in Apple Wallet takes four minutes at home and can fail in a way that takes an afternoon to solve in Tokyo, because a US-issued Visa is refused for top-ups while Mastercard and American Express are accepted. ▲ The failure produces an unhelpful error rather than an explanation, and the place people discover it is a ticket gate with a queue behind them. Do it on the sofa.

What does not need doing

You do not need cash in advance at an airport exchange desk, you do not need a pocket wi-fi device booked from abroad in most cases, and you do not need a rail pass ordered before you have counted your legs. Each of these is sold hard to people planning a first trip and each is, for the corridor-shaped trip most people take, either unnecessary or actively worse than the alternative available on arrival.

Day zero

The first six hours

The first six hoursDay zero

Nobody writes the arrival day, which is the one where a plan is most easily lost.

You land after eleven hours in the air, somewhere between mid-afternoon and late evening, and what happens next is decided almost entirely by two things you chose months earlier: which airport you fly into, and what time the wheels touch. Neither feels like an itinerary decision at the time of booking. Both are.

Haneda sits close to central Tokyo and is reached in roughly thirty minutes. ● Narita is a genuine distance out, and the transfer runs sixty to ninety minutes depending on which service you take. ● On a normal day that difference is an hour. On the day you have not slept properly, are carrying everything you brought, and are trying to work out a ticket machine in a language you do not read, it is the difference between an evening and a lie-down.

The order that works

Clear immigration, ignore the exchange desks, and go straight to the train — assuming the transit card on your phone already works, which is why it is the one task on the pre-departure list with a hard deadline. If it does not, buy a physical card from a station machine and feed it cash: the machines take notes, carry an English interface, and sidestep the entire card- network problem in one step. ●

Then do nothing ambitious. The first evening is for a short walk, a convenience-store meal that will be better than it has any right to be, and an early night against the jet lag. Japan is thirteen or fourteen hours ahead of the American east coast, and the trip is long enough that one surrendered evening costs nothing while one badly-handled first night costs two days.

The rhythm

How a week in Japan runs

How a week in Japan runsThe rhythm

The day has a shape here, and fighting it costs you the two hours that matter most.

Japanese sights open early and empty out late, and the gap between eight in the morning and ten in the morning is the difference between a temple you will remember and a queue you will photograph. This is the most reliably useful habit a first-timer can adopt, and it costs nothing but a decision made the night before. ●

The reason is structural rather than cultural. Tour groups, school trips and day-trippers from the other city all arrive on the same rough schedule, which means the famous sites load from mid-morning and stay loaded until late afternoon. Arrive at opening and you have perhaps ninety minutes of something close to solitude at places that are unusable by eleven.

What closes, and when

The closures that catch people
WhatWhen
Museums, very widely ●Mondays
Small and family-run restaurants during Obon ●mid-August
Independent shops, offices, some museums ●29 Dec – 3 Jan
Temple and shrine grounds (most) ●rarely close at all

The New Year window is the one Americans walk into unaware, because late December reads as an ordinary travel period from the United States and nothing on the calendar warns you. ● Shrines are extraordinarily busy with the first-visit tradition, department stores shut on the first, and a great deal of the small, good, independent layer of the country simply stops for several days.

The two hours worth protecting

Build the day around one early anchor and let the rest be loose. A first-timer’s instinct is to schedule four sights and move between them efficiently; the better structure is one thing at opening, a long unhurried middle, and an evening that is allowed to be unremarkable. Japan rewards the loose afternoon more than most countries, because the density of small good things — a shop, a shrine, a counter with six seats — means walking produces finds that planning cannot.

The set pieces

The three operations everyone runs

The three operations everyone runsThe set pieces

Three experiences appear on essentially every first itinerary. Each has an order of operations, and each is commonly run backwards.

A first trip converges on a small number of unavoidable set pieces. They are unavoidable for good reasons — they are genuinely extraordinary — but each one has a way of being done that works and a way that produces the photograph everyone else has and very little else.

The shrine at Fushimi

The vermillion gate tunnels above Fushimi Inari are the most photographed thing in Kyoto and, for most of the day, the most crowded. The operation that works is to go at or before opening — the grounds are open continuously rather than gated by hours ● — and to keep climbing. Crowd density falls off sharply with altitude, and the intersection partway up is where most visitors turn back. ● Above it the tunnels are quiet, and the inscriptions on the gates, which are donations with dates and names on them, become legible as a record rather than a backdrop.

The bullet train itself

The Shinkansen is a set piece rather than merely transport, and it is worth treating as one on the leg that matters. Reserve a seat rather than taking the non-reserved car; on the Tokyo–Kyoto run, a right-hand seat gives you the Mount Fuji side on a clear day, and the ride is rated by a great many travellers as a highlight in its own right. ● It is also the one part of the trip where being early is pointless: the train leaves at the second it says it will, and the platform is organised so that queueing for ten minutes buys you nothing.

The bath

An onsen is the set piece most Americans approach with the most apprehension and describe afterwards with the most enthusiasm. The procedure is fixed and simple: wash thoroughly, at a seated station, before entering the water; bathe with no swimwear; keep the small towel out of the bath. ● The only genuine obstacle is tattoos, which are still commonly refused at public baths — the practical answer is a tattoo-friendly establishment or a private bath, arranged before you arrive rather than discovered at the door. ●

Where to stay

Four kinds of room, scored the same way

Four kinds of room, scored the same wayWhere to stay

Japanese accommodation is not a price ladder. The categories differ in kind, and the cheapest is often the right answer.

The room categories here do not map onto American expectations. A business hotel is not a budget compromise, a capsule is not a hostel, and a ryokan is not a boutique hotel with tatami. Judged on the criteria that matter on a trip — what the location buys you, what is included, and what it costs in daily friction — they sort differently than the nightly rate suggests.

The four categories, on identical criteria
CategoryWhat you are buying
Business hotel ●a small, spotless, well-located room
Western chain hotel ●space and familiarity, further out
Ryokan, half board ●the whole evening, not the room
Capsule ●a night, cheaply, in the centre

The business hotel is the workhorse and the right default for most of a first trip. The rooms are small by American standards and immaculately maintained, they sit next to stations because that is who they were built for, and for a traveller who is out from eight until nine they are excellent value. ● Choosing a larger room further out is the most common way a first-timer spends more money to have a worse trip.

The decision, in order

Pick the station first, the category second, the room last. In Tokyo that means somewhere on or one stop from the Yamanote loop; in Kyoto it means a major bus corridor rather than a subway stop, because the subway does not go where the temples are. ● Then choose the category from the list above, and only then compare rooms. Doing it in the other order — finding an appealing room and then discovering what it is connected to — is how people end up with three transfers before breakfast.

The plan

The days, as operations

The days, as operationsThe plan

Not an itinerary. Four shapes a day can take, and what each one costs.

An itinerary tells you where to be at eleven o’clock. An operation tells you what kind of day you are running, which is more useful, because the specific temple matters far less than whether the day has one anchor or four. These are the four shapes that work, and the failure each one avoids.

Four day-shapes
ShapeWhat it is for
One anchor, loose afternoon ●the default; most days should be this
Neighbourhood day, no trains ●jet lag, rain, the day after a long leg
Day trip, committed ●only on trips of ten days or more
Transfer day ●the long leg, plus one easy thing at the far end

The transfer day is the one most often mis-planned. A Tokyo–Kyoto leg is a little over two hours on the train and most of a morning once you count the station, the luggage and the check-in at the other end. Treating it as a half-day with one gentle thing scheduled after it produces a good day; treating it as a travel morning followed by a full afternoon of sights produces a bad one, and it is the same number of hours either way.

Jet lag, honestly

Japan is thirteen or fourteen hours ahead of the American east coast, which is close to maximal, and the first two days are compromised whatever you do. Plan them as neighbourhood days with nothing that cannot be abandoned, get outside in daylight, and do not schedule the thing you most want to see on day one. ● A first trip that front-loads its highlights spends them on a version of itself that is not fully present.

Eating

The rule that outlives the list

The rule that outlives the listEating

Japan is the hardest country in the world to eat badly in, and the easiest to eat mediocrely in — by trying too hard.

Every guide to Japan opens its food chapter with names. The names are the part that expires: the chef moves, the queue arrives, the lease ends, and a list assembled in one spring is quietly wrong by the next. What does not expire is the structure of eating here — which kinds of places exist, what each is for, and how you get into them — and that structure is unusually legible once someone draws it for you.

The four layers

Japanese eating stacks into four layers that operate on completely different rules, and most first-timers only know about the top one.

What each layer is for
LayerHow you get in
The destination restaurant ●booked, often months, sometimes impossible
The counter — ramen, sushi, tempura ●walk in, often a ticket machine
The izakaya ●walk in, small plates, drinking
Depachika and konbini ●no booking, no language, no wait

The counter layer is where a first trip’s best meals almost always happen, and it is the one the planning instinct skips because it cannot be booked from Ohio in February. A ramen shop with a vending machine at the door is not a downgrade from a restaurant — it is the form the cuisine takes at its most serious, and the machine exists because the cook would rather be cooking than taking your order. ●

Ordering when you cannot read the menu

This is the anxiety that keeps people in hotel restaurants, and it dissolves faster than anyone expects. Ticket machines have photographs, and increasingly an English button. Plastic food models in the window are a genuine ordering system, not a curiosity — you may point at one. Many counters serve one thing, so sitting down is most of the order. And the reliable fallback everywhere is to look at what the person beside you is eating and indicate that you would like the same. ●

Slurping noodles is normal and faintly encouraged. Tipping is not done anywhere in the country, and leaving money on a table causes confusion rather than pleasure. ● Payment usually goes onto a small tray at the register rather than into a hand.

What to book

Two meals for a fortnight is the right order of magnitude — the one or two evenings you would be genuinely sorry to miss, and nothing else. A kaiseki dinner, a specific sushi counter, the ryokan meal that comes with the room: those are worth the calendar. Everything else is better found than scheduled, because the upside is concentrated in exactly the places that do not take a reservation from abroad, and a restaurant that eagerly accepts foreign bookings months out is telling you something about who its customers are. ●

The failure mode here is not hunger. It is spending an extraordinary food country executing a spreadsheet, and arriving home having eaten well but never once walked into somewhere because it smelled good.

Money

Where it leaks

Where it leaksMoney

Japan is not an extractive destination. What it has instead is a handful of small, avoidable leaks.

It is worth saying plainly, because it contradicts the reflex an American brings from other famous destinations: nobody in Japan is trying to take your money. There is no tipping, no haggling, no dual-pricing, no menu with a different number for you, and the scams that organise a trip to certain other capitals essentially do not feature. ● The corpus behind this book contains almost no warnings of that kind, which is itself the finding.

What exists instead is leakage: money that goes nowhere useful because of a decision made early and never revisited.

The four leaks, largest first
LeakWhere it is dealt with
A rail pass on a route that does not need one ◇Decision 02
Peak-season rooms bought for a probability ●Decision 04
A ryokan judged on its rate rather than its inclusions ●Decision 06
A bigger room further out, paid for in daily transfers ●Where to stay

Cash, still

Cards are widely accepted in the places a first trip spends most of its money, and the country has moved a long way toward contactless. The exceptions are specific and worth knowing: smaller and older restaurants, market stalls, temple and shrine entry fees and charms, and rural businesses are still commonly cash-only. ● Carrying a modest amount of cash removes the problem entirely, and the stored balance on a transit card covers a surprising share of the small purchases besides.

One administrative note

Tax-free shopping registration through the government’s pre-arrival web service has been changing, and the separate registration step it used to offer is being retired. ◇ This is exactly the class of fact that belongs on the Field Sheet rather than in a book: check the current process close to departure rather than trusting any printed description of it, including this one.

Route

What does a day trip cost you?

Route — What does a day trip cost you?Decision 01 of 08

Nobody plans a trip to Japan by deciding how many days not to spend in Tokyo. Yet that is the decision a day trip makes, and it is made by accretion — Hakone because of the Fuji view, Nara because of the deer, Nikko because it was on a list — until a five-day trip built around two cities has barely two days left standing in either of them.

Where the days go · five-day Tokyo + Kyoto trip
DAYS OUTDAYS LEFT IN THE CITY
5 days, no day trips ●4 full days in the two cities
5 days, one day trip ●3 full days in the two cities
5 days, three day trips ●barely 2 days in the two cities
10 days, two day trips ●6–7 full days in the two cities
The call

Under a week, take none. At ten days, take two. A day trip is not an addition to the trip, it is a substitution: the Shinkansen to Hakone and back is three hours of daylight before you have looked at anything. The test is not whether the destination is worth seeing — they all are — but whether you would rather spend that day somewhere new or spend it getting better at the city you already chose. First-timers reliably answer that question wrong in the planning and right in hindsight.

When the other call is right
  • then take it → ten days or more, and the second city already has its three full days
  • then take it → the day trip is the thing you came for (Fuji, the deer, Miyajima)
  • then take it → your base is Kyoto and the trip is Nara, which is forty minutes
When ours is wrong for you
  • then skip it → five days or fewer, whatever the blogs stack into them
  • then skip it → it is your first two days and you are still jet-lagged
  • then skip it → you are moving hotels the next morning anyway
● Nara from Kyoto is the one day trip that survives a short trip, because the round trip is under ninety minutes of travel and it runs on local lines you are already paying for.

Rail

Does the national rail pass pay for itself?

Rail — Does the national rail pass pay for itself?Decision 02 of 08

For twenty years the Japan Rail Pass was the first thing an American bought and the advice was sound. In October 2023 the seven-day ordinary pass went from about ¥29,650 to ¥50,000 — a rise of roughly seventy per cent in one step — and a great deal of the English-language internet has never been revised. The pass did not merely get more expensive. It stopped being the default answer.

The break-even, in legs · seven-day ordinary pass
LONG LEGSWHAT YOU PAY
one long leg — Tokyo → Kyoto, then fly home ◇about ¥13,850
two long legs — the classic first trip ◇about ¥27,700
four long legs — what the pass needs ●about ¥55,400
the seven-day ordinary pass ◇¥50,000
The call

Count your long journeys before you buy, not after. Divide the pass price by a Tokyo–Kyoto fare and it needs roughly four legs of that distance to break even — about two round trips inside seven days. The classic first trip makes two. The deeper cost is not the ¥50,000: it is that a pass already paid for quietly argues for more legs, and the route bends to justify the purchase instead of the purchase following the route.

When the other call is right
  • then buy it → the route reaches Hiroshima or returns north through the mountains
  • then buy it → four or more long legs inside a single seven-day window
  • then buy it → you want unlimited spontaneity more than you want ¥20,000
When ours is wrong for you
  • then skip it → Tokyo, Kyoto, home (the shape most first trips take)
  • then skip it → your long legs are spread across twelve days, not seven
  • then skip it → a regional pass covers the one area you move around in

Transit — Which card will load on an American phone?Decision 03 of 08

Japanese city transit offers three ways to pay and most visitors default to whichever one they read about first. That is a survivable mistake. The unsurvivable one is arriving with a phone that cannot top itself up, discovering it at a Shinjuku gate at nine in the morning, and losing the first afternoon to a problem that takes four minutes to solve on your own sofa.

Topping up a mobile Suica in Apple Wallet · US-issued cards
CARDWILL IT LOAD
US-issued Visa ▲refused — the common failure
US-issued Mastercard ▲works for Suica, not for Pasmo
US-issued American Express ▲works

The call

Create the card at home, load it with the card you intend to use, and watch the top-up complete before you fly. A US Visa is refused inside Apple Wallet while Mastercard and Amex go through, which is not documented anywhere official and produces a meaningless error rather than an explanation. If every card you own is Visa, the answer is not to keep retrying it at the gate: it is a physical card from a station machine, fed cash, which sidesteps the card networks entirely.

When the other call is right
  • then use the phone → you hold a Mastercard or Amex and set it up before departure
  • then use the phone → Express Mode means you tap without unlocking, even on a flat battery
When ours is wrong for you
  • then carry plastic → Visa-only, or an Android handset bought outside Japan
  • then carry plastic → you would rather feed a machine ¥3,000 than debug a wallet

Season

When should you come, and what does the date cost?

Season — When should you come, and what does the date cost?Decision 04 of 08

Cherry blossom is the reason most people first think about Japan and the worst single week to build a plan around. The bloom holds for roughly a week in any one city, the forecast that predicts it does not exist until February, and the rooms you would need were gone in October. You are asked to commit six months before anyone can tell you whether the thing you are committing to will be there.

The four windows · what each one demands
WINDOWWHAT IT ASKS OF YOU
late March – early April, bloom ●book ~6 months out, on faith
late April – early May, Golden Week ◇avoid moving cities entirely
second half of November, foliage ●book 2–3 months out, colour holds weeks
January – February, low season ●book weeks out, sites near-empty
The call

If the dates are yours to choose, take late November. Autumn colour lasts weeks rather than days, the weather is settled, the booking lead is a third of spring's, and you are not paying peak prices for a probability. Cherry blossom is worth the gamble on a second trip, when the cities themselves are no longer new and losing the flowers costs you an image rather than the trip. Choose the season deliberately; it decides the booking deadline, which decides everything downstream of it.

When the other call is right
  • then come in spring → the blossom is the trip and you accept the odds
  • then come in spring → you can book cancellable and move within a fortnight
When ours is wrong for you
  • then move the dates → Golden Week or mid-August Obon, if the plan involves moving cities
  • then move the dates → you want the famous gardens without the compressed crowds
◇ Golden Week is built from four fixed holidays between 29 April and 5 May, but the substitute-holiday rule changes the length of the run every year. Never freeze one year's dates into a plan — check the calendar for yours.

Base

Which station should you sleep next to?

Base — Which station should you sleep next to?Decision 05 of 08

In a compact European capital the question is which neighbourhood you want to walk out into. Tokyo does not work like that. It is a transit graph before it is a set of neighbourhoods, and the practical question is not which district charms you on a map but which line you will be standing on at eight in the morning and eleven at night.

Tokyo bases · what each one is for
AREAWHAT THE LOCATION BUYS
Shinjuku ●most connections, least calm
Tokyo Station / Marunouchi ●Shinkansen at the door, quiet at night
Shibuya ●nightlife and the Yamanote line
Asakusa ●cheapest, oldest, furthest from the rest
The call

Sleep on the Yamanote loop, and in Kyoto ignore the subway entirely. Almost everything a first trip does in Tokyo touches the Yamanote line, so a room within a few minutes of any of its stations removes a daily tax you would otherwise pay in transfers. Kyoto inverts the logic: it is a bus city with two subway lines that do not go where the temples are, so proximity to a major bus corridor beats proximity to a station.

When the other call is right
  • then take Shinjuku → you want one station to solve every journey and do not mind the scale
  • then take Marunouchi → you are taking the Shinkansen and value a quiet evening
When ours is wrong for you
  • then look again → the room is cheap because it is three transfers from the loop
  • then look again → you picked a Kyoto hotel by subway access and will be taking buses
● In Kyoto the practical unit is the bus corridor, not the station; a hotel near Shijo or Kawaramachi reaches the eastern temples without a transfer.

Is the ryokan worth it, and where should it be?

Sleep — Is the ryokan worth it, and where should it be?Decision 06 of 08

The ryokan is the one night of a Japan trip people describe years later, and it is also the one most often booked in the wrong city, on the wrong night, at a price the traveller never quite compares to anything. It is not a hotel with tatami. It is dinner, breakfast, a bath and a room sold as a single evening, which makes the nightly rate the wrong number to judge it by.

What the rate covers
NIGHTWHAT IS INCLUDED
business hotel, city centre ●room only
ryokan, half board ●room + kaiseki dinner + breakfast + bath
the comparison people make ●rate vs rate — wrong
the comparison to make ●rate vs room + two meals out
The call

One night, in an onsen town, not in Kyoto. A ryokan works when the building and the bath are the evening — which means arriving by mid-afternoon and going nowhere afterwards. That is a poor fit for Kyoto, where the evening is the city itself, and an excellent fit for Hakone, Kinosaki or the Japan Sea side. Price it against a hotel room plus two restaurant meals and the premium shrinks to something defensible.

When the other call is right
  • then book it → you can arrive by 15:00 and have nothing else scheduled that evening
  • then book it → winter, when the outdoor bath is the entire argument
When ours is wrong for you
  • then skip it → it is your Kyoto night and you wanted to eat in Gion
  • then skip it → you have visible tattoos and have not checked that specific bath's rule
● Tattoo policy is set per property and is still commonly enforced at public baths. Many ryokan solve it with a private or in-room bath — ask before booking, not on arrival.

Eating

How do you eat well without a reservation system you can use?

Eating — How do you eat well without a reservation system you can use?Decision 07 of 08

First-timers over-plan meals the way they over-plan sights, researching a specific restaurant for every slot on the itinerary — and in Japan that effort converts badly. The best places are small, often unlisted, frequently unbookable from abroad, and the ones that are bookable from abroad are disproportionately the ones that market to foreigners. Planning harder makes the food worse.

Four ways to eat, and what each delivers
APPROACHWHAT IT COSTS YOU
researched restaurant, booked from the US ●high effort, uneven result
depachika food hall, ground floor ●no booking, excellent, cheap
counter seat, walk in at opening ●no booking, the real thing
konbini, between meals ●very good, ¥500
The call

Book two meals for the whole trip and let the rest happen. Reserve the one or two dinners you would be sad to miss and leave every other slot open, because the upside in Japan sits at counters you cannot book and department-store basements no guide sends you to. The failure mode here is not eating badly — it is hard to eat badly — it is spending the trip executing a spreadsheet through a country that rewards walking in.

When the other call is right
  • then book ahead → a specific sushi counter or kaiseki room is a reason you came
  • then book ahead → a birthday, an anniversary, anything that cannot be improvised
When ours is wrong for you
  • then leave it open → you are filling slots because they are empty
  • then leave it open → the restaurant takes foreign bookings easily (ask why)
● Department-store basements — the depachika — take no booking, cost nothing to walk into, and carry prepared food of a standard that would be a destination restaurant elsewhere. It is the most reliable good meal in any Japanese city and almost no first-timer is sent there.

Which airport, and what happens in the first six hours?

Arrival — Which airport, and what happens in the first six hours?Decision 08 of 08

The airport looks like a price decision and behaves like an itinerary decision. Narita and Haneda are both fine airports with good transfers; the difference is what each one does to the evening you land, and a first evening lost to a ninety-minute train after eleven hours in the air is a real cost that never appears in the fare comparison.

Getting into central Tokyo
AIRPORTINTO THE CITY
Haneda ●~30 min, close to the centre
Narita ●~60–90 min depending on the service
either, after a 13:00 landing ●you get an evening
either, after a 20:00 landing ●you get a hotel bed
The call

Pay a modest premium for Haneda, and pay more attention to the landing time. Haneda is meaningfully closer and worth a small fare difference, but the variable that decides your first day is when the wheels touch — an early afternoon landing at Narita beats a late-evening landing at Haneda every time. Set the transit card up before you fly and the first hour on the ground is a train, not a queue at a machine you are too tired to read.

When the other call is right
  • then take Haneda → the fares are within a couple of hundred dollars
  • then take Haneda → you land late and want the shortest possible transfer
When ours is wrong for you
  • then take Narita → the saving is real and you land before mid-afternoon
  • then take Narita → the schedule fits the rest of the route better

Back matter

What this book will not tell you

What this book will not tell youBack matter

The refusals are the product. They are what keeps the rest of it true.

Four things this book deliberately does not do. Each is a thing most guides lead with, and each is a thing we would have to get wrong in order to include.

  • It does not name hotels. We have not slept in them. An assessment of a room we have not been in is a repackaged booking-site listing with a confident voice on top, and you can read those for free. What the book does instead is tell you which station to sleep near and why, which is the part that survives a change of ownership.
  • It does not name restaurants. A list of restaurants is wrong within a season — the chef moves, the queue finds it, the lease ends. The rule that generated the list is still right in five years, so the book gives you the rule. Decision 07 is that argument in full.
  • It does not print current prices. Prices belong in the Field Sheet, which is dated and re-issued. A price set in a book is a claim that stops being true quietly, and the reader has no way to tell when it did.
  • It does not republish anyone's ratings. Aggregate scores from the booking platforms are contractually and editorially radioactive, and reprinting them would add nothing you cannot see for yourself.

There is a fifth refusal that belongs here too, because it shaped this edition: where the corpus behind this book was thin on a subject, we left the subject out rather than writing around the gap. A chapter assembled to fill a page is detectable, and it devalues the chapters that were earned.

Closing

The country, not the plan

The country, not the planClosing

There is a moment, usually on the second or third morning, when the planning stops mattering. It tends to arrive somewhere ordinary — a station platform at seven in the morning, everyone moving in the same direction without hurry, a vending machine humming, the train arriving at the second it said it would. Nothing about it is a sight. It is just a country working, at a level of consideration that is difficult to explain to someone who has not stood in it.

That is the thing the arithmetic is protecting. Every decision in this book exists to subtract a small anxiety from the fortnight so that more of it is available for the parts that are not decidable in advance: the walk you took because the light was good, the counter with six seats you went into because it was raining, the temple you had entirely to yourself at eight in the morning because you were awake and nobody else was.

Work the sums out before you go. Then put the book down and stop optimising — the country is better than any plan you can make of it.
