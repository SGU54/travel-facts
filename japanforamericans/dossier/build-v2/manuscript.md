# What this book is for


Japan is among the easiest countries an American can travel in. You need no
visa for a short visit, the trains run to the second, the crime rate is low enough that the
question barely arises, and the food is excellent at nearly every price. Almost nothing about
the trip is difficult. What it is, instead, is over-decided: a dozen choices that each look
small, presented in isolation by a hundred articles, and connected to each other in ways that
only become visible after you have already committed to three of them.

This book exists because those connections are where the money and the days go. The season
you pick sets the booking deadline. The booking deadline decides how many cities you can
commit to. The number of cities decides whether a rail pass pays. Whether the pass pays
decides whether you spend two hundred dollars you did not need to — and, worse, whether you
then bend the route to justify the purchase. None of that is difficult. It is simply not
answerable one article at a time, which is exactly how it is usually answered.

# What we do and do not claim

Every call in here is argued in the open, with the arithmetic on the page, so that you can
disagree with us and still come out ahead. That is the contract. Where we say buy, we show the
sum that says buy; where the sum is close, we say it is close. A recommendation you cannot
audit is worth nothing to someone spending four thousand dollars, and the number of places
where a confident voice is simply repeating a decade-old blog post is higher in Japan than
almost anywhere. 

We do not tell you which hotel to book or which restaurant to eat at. Those are the two
things every guide leads with and the two that age fastest — a list of names is wrong within a
season, while the rule that generated the list keeps working. The back of this book says
plainly what it refuses to do, and why.

# Marks, and the two documents


Travel writing rarely distinguishes between a fact that has been true for
forty years and a price that was checked once in spring. This book marks the difference on
every claim, because the two age at completely different rates and you are entitled to know
which one you are being handed.

Anything carrying no mark is opinion, and we have tried to leave none of it in. The rule

# Why there are two documents

The book you are reading is built to stay true for about two years. It contains no live
prices, because a price printed in a book is a lie with a long shelf life. Every volatile
figure lives instead in the Field Sheet — a short dated annex, re-issued quarterly, that
carries the numbers and nothing else. When the two disagree, the Sheet is right and the book
is old.

Read the book once before you book anything. Carry the Sheet on the trip.

# Two legs, and a pass that needs four


The trip that nearly every first-timer takes has a shape so consistent it can
be described in a sentence: fly into Tokyo, spend several days, take one Shinkansen to Kyoto,
spend several more, fly home. Some variant of that is what the itinerary articles converge on,
what the week allows, and what people come back having enjoyed. It contains exactly two long
train journeys. 

The Japan Rail Pass needs about four to pay for itself. 

That collision is the single most useful thing to know before you plan anything else,
because it is counter-intuitive in a specific and expensive way. For twenty years the pass was
the correct reflex, and the entire English-language internet learned to recommend it. Then in
October 2023 the seven-day ordinary pass rose from roughly ¥29,650 to ¥50,000 — about seventy
per cent in a single step — and the advice did not move. 

# Why the sum is the interesting part

Divide the pass price by the fare it is meant to save you and the answer falls out. A
reserved seat from Tokyo to Kyoto is about ¥13,850, so the pass needs a little under four
journeys of that length before it breaks even — roughly two round trips inside a seven-day
window. A trip with two long legs in it is not close to the line. It is at half. 

The second-order cost is the one that does the real damage, and no price comparison
catches it. A pass already paid for is an argument in your pocket for using it, and routes
quietly bend to justify the purchase. A day trip gets added because it is free, a city
inserted because the train is covered, and a fortnight that was going to be two calm bases
turns into five hotels and a lot of luggage on platforms. The purchase should follow the route. It
usually leads it.

# What the corridor looks like

Japan for a first-timer is not the archipelago of the map. It is a corridor: Tokyo and
Kyoto, a little over two hours apart on the fastest services the pass does not cover, with
everything else reached as a day trip from one end or the other. Nara is forty-five minutes
from Kyoto. Hakone, Nikko and Kamakura are inside two hours of Tokyo.  Those spokes run on
local and rapid lines at a fraction of bullet-train prices, which means the pass does not help
with them either.

Once the corridor is the mental model rather than the country, most of the remaining
decisions in this book get easier, and several of them stop being decisions at all.

# The four things with deadlines


Japan asks very little of an American traveller in advance — no visa, no
vaccination paperwork, no entry fee.  What it does have is a small set of tasks whose cost
rises sharply the later you do them, and which are easy to leave until the week before because
nothing forces them earlier.

    Rooms, late November foliage 2–3 months

The transit card is the one that looks trivial and is not. Creating a Suica in Apple Wallet
takes four minutes at home and can fail in a way that takes an afternoon to solve in Tokyo,
because a US-issued Visa is refused for top-ups while Mastercard and American Express are
accepted.  The failure produces an unhelpful error rather than an explanation, and the
place people discover it is a ticket gate with a queue behind them. Do it on the sofa.

# What does not need doing

You do not need cash in advance at an airport exchange desk, you do not need a pocket
wi-fi device booked from abroad in most cases, and you do not need a rail pass ordered before
you have counted your legs. Each of these is sold hard to people planning a first trip and
each is, for the corridor-shaped trip most people take, either unnecessary or actively worse
than the alternative available on arrival.

# The first six hours


You land after eleven hours in the air, somewhere between mid-afternoon and
late evening, and what happens next is decided almost entirely by two things you chose months
earlier: which airport you fly into, and what time the wheels touch. Neither feels like an
itinerary decision at the time of booking. Both are.

Haneda sits close to central Tokyo and is reached in roughly thirty minutes.  Narita
is a genuine distance out, and the transfer runs sixty to ninety minutes depending on which
service you take.  On a normal day that difference is an hour. On the day you have not
slept properly, are carrying everything you brought, and are trying to work out a ticket
machine in a language you do not read, it is the difference between an evening and a
lie-down.

# The order that works

Clear immigration, ignore the exchange desks, and go straight to the train — assuming the
transit card on your phone already works, which is why it is the one task on the pre-departure
list with a hard deadline. If it does not, buy a physical card from a station machine and feed
it cash: the machines take notes, carry an English interface, and sidestep the entire
card-network problem in one step. 

Then do nothing ambitious. The first evening is for a short walk, a convenience-store meal
that will be better than it has any right to be, and an early night against the jet lag.
Japan is thirteen or fourteen hours ahead of the American east coast, and the trip is long
enough that one surrendered evening costs nothing while one badly-handled first night costs
two days.

# How a week in Japan runs


Japanese sights open early and empty out late, and the gap between eight in
the morning and ten in the morning is the difference between a temple you will remember and a
queue you will photograph. This is the most reliably useful habit a first-timer can adopt, and
it costs nothing but a decision made the night before. 

The reason is structural rather than cultural. Tour groups, school trips and day-trippers
from the other city all arrive on the same rough schedule, which means the famous sites load
from mid-morning and stay loaded until late afternoon. Arrive at opening and you have perhaps
ninety minutes of something close to solitude at places that are unusable by eleven.

The New Year window is the one Americans walk into unaware, because late December reads as
an ordinary travel period from the United States and nothing on the calendar warns you. 
Shrines are extraordinarily busy with the first-visit tradition, department stores shut on the
first, and a great deal of the small, good, independent layer of the country simply stops for
several days.

# The two hours worth protecting

Build the day around one early anchor and let the rest be loose. A first-timer’s
instinct is to schedule four sights and move between them efficiently; the better structure is
one thing at opening, a long unhurried middle, and an evening that is allowed to be
unremarkable. Japan rewards the loose afternoon more than most countries, because the density
of small good things (a shop, a shrine, a counter with six seats) means walking
produces finds that planning cannot.

# The three operations everyone runs


A first trip converges on a small number of unavoidable set pieces. They are
unavoidable for good reasons, being genuinely extraordinary, but each one has
a way of being done that works and a way that produces the photograph everyone else has and
very little else.

# The shrine at Fushimi

The vermillion gate tunnels above Fushimi Inari are the most photographed thing in Kyoto
and, for most of the day, the most crowded. The operation that works is to go at or before
opening, since the grounds are open continuously rather than gated by hours,  and
to keep climbing. Crowd density falls off sharply with altitude, and the intersection partway
up is where most visitors turn back.  Above it the tunnels are quiet, and the inscriptions
on the gates, which are donations with dates and names on them, become legible as a record
rather than a backdrop.

# The bullet train itself

It is worth saying what the departure is like, because it is the first thing that tells
an American visitor they are somewhere run differently. The train is not there, and then it
is, and the doors open opposite marks painted on the platform where you are already standing.
Cleaners go through the carriage in the time it takes you to find your seat. At the appointed
second — not the minute, the second — the platform begins to move. Nothing about
it is dramatic, which is the point: an entire country has agreed to be this precise, and it
is on display at the price of an ordinary ticket. 

The Shinkansen is a set piece rather than merely transport, and it is worth treating as
one on the leg that matters. Reserve a seat rather than taking the non-reserved car; on the
Tokyo–Kyoto run, a right-hand seat gives you the Mount Fuji side on a clear day, and the
ride is rated by a great many travellers as a highlight in its own right.  It is also the
one part of the trip where being early is pointless: the train leaves at the second it says it
will, and the platform is organised so that queueing for ten minutes buys you nothing.

# The bath

An onsen is the set piece most Americans approach with the most apprehension and describe
afterwards with the most enthusiasm. The procedure is fixed and simple: wash thoroughly, at a
seated station, before entering the water; bathe with no swimwear; keep the small towel out of
the bath.  The only genuine obstacle is tattoos, which are still commonly refused at
public baths. The practical answer is a tattoo-friendly establishment or a private
bath, arranged before you arrive rather than discovered at the door. 

# Four kinds of room, scored the same way


The room categories here do not map onto American expectations. A business
hotel is not a budget compromise, a capsule is not a hostel, and a ryokan is not a boutique
hotel with tatami. Judged on the criteria that matter on a trip (what the
location buys you, what is included, what it costs in daily friction), they sort
differently than the nightly rate suggests.


  
    CategoryWhat you are buying
    Business hotel a small, spotless, well-located room
    Western chain hotel space and familiarity, further out
    Ryokan, half board the whole evening, not the room
    Capsule a night, cheaply, in the centre
  


The business hotel is the workhorse and the right default for most of a first trip. The
rooms are small by American standards and immaculately maintained, they sit next to stations
because that is who they were built for, and for a traveller who is out from eight until nine
they are excellent value.  Choosing a larger room further out is the most common way a
first-timer spends more money to have a worse trip.

# The decision, in order

Pick the station first, the category second, the room last. In Tokyo that means somewhere
on or one stop from the Yamanote loop; in Kyoto it means a major bus corridor rather than a
subway stop, because the subway does not go where the temples are.  Then choose the
category from the list above, and only then compare rooms. Doing it in the other order, by
finding an appealing room and then discovering what it is connected to, is how people
end up with three transfers before breakfast.

# The days, as operations


An itinerary tells you where to be at eleven o’clock. An operation tells
you what kind of day you are running, which is more useful, because the specific temple
matters far less than whether the day has one anchor or four. These are the four shapes that
work, and the failure each one avoids.


  
    ShapeWhat it is for
    One anchor, loose afternoon the default; most days should be this
    Neighbourhood day, no trains jet lag, rain, the day after a long leg
    Day trip, committed only on trips of ten days or more
    Transfer day the long leg, plus one easy thing at the far end
  


The transfer day is the one most often mis-planned. A Tokyo–Kyoto leg is a little
over two hours on the train and most of a morning once you count the station, the luggage and
the check-in at the other end. Treating it as a half-day with one gentle thing scheduled after
it produces a good day; treating it as a travel morning followed by a full afternoon of sights
produces a bad one, and it is the same number of hours either way.

# Jet lag, honestly

Japan is thirteen or fourteen hours ahead of the American east coast, which is close to
maximal, and the first two days are compromised whatever you do. Plan them as
neighbourhood days with nothing that cannot be abandoned, get outside in daylight, and do not
schedule the thing you most want to see on day one.  A first trip that front-loads its
highlights spends them on a version of itself that is not fully present.

# How far ahead each thing must be decided


Almost nothing about Japan needs to be booked a year out, and a few things
genuinely do. The trouble is that the two sets are not intuitive, and the items with the
longest horizons are the ones that look most like details.

The asymmetry is the point. Rooms in the two peak windows are the only scarce

A trip that books rooms early and leaves the rest open is running the horizons correctly.
A trip that books eleven experiences from abroad four months out has converted a fortnight into
a schedule, and the schedule will be wrong within a day of landing.

# The rule that outlives the list


Every guide to Japan opens its food chapter with names. The names are the part
that expires: the chef moves, the queue arrives, the lease ends, and a list assembled in one
spring is quietly wrong by the next. What does not expire is the structure of eating here
— which kinds of places exist, what each is for, and how you get into them — and
that structure is unusually legible once someone draws it for you.

# The four layers

Japanese eating stacks into four layers that operate on completely different rules, and
most first-timers only know about the top one.


  
    LayerHow you get in
    The destination restaurant booked, often months, sometimes impossible
    The counter — ramen, sushi, tempura walk in, often a ticket machine
    The izakaya walk in, small plates, drinking
    Depachika and konbini no booking, no language, no wait
  


The counter layer is where a first trip’s best meals almost always happen, and it is
the one the planning instinct skips because it cannot be booked from Ohio in February. A ramen
shop with a vending machine at the door is not a downgrade from a restaurant — it is the
form the cuisine takes at its most serious, and the machine exists because the cook would
rather be cooking than taking your order. 

The bottom layer deserves a sentence of its own, because nobody believes it until they
have stood in one. It is eleven at night, you are jet-lagged and hungry in a country where
you cannot read the signs, and the brightly-lit shop on the corner sells you an egg sandwich
on bread with the crusts cut off, a rice triangle wrapped so the seaweed stays dry until you
open it, and a hot coffee, for about the price of a bag of crisps at home — and all of
it is good. Not good-for-a-convenience-store. Good. 

# Ordering when you cannot read the menu

This is the anxiety that keeps people in hotel restaurants, and it dissolves faster than
anyone expects. Ticket machines have photographs, and increasingly an English button. Plastic
food models in the window are a genuine ordering system, not a curiosity — you may point
at one. Many counters serve one thing, so sitting down is most of the order. And the reliable
fallback everywhere is to look at what the person beside you is eating and indicate that you
would like the same. 

Slurping noodles is normal and faintly encouraged. Tipping is not done anywhere in the
country, and leaving money on a table causes confusion rather than pleasure.  Payment
usually goes onto a small tray at the register rather than into a hand.

# What to book

Two meals for a fortnight is the right order of magnitude — the one or two evenings
you would be genuinely sorry to miss, and nothing else. A kaiseki dinner, a specific sushi
counter, the ryokan meal that comes with the room: those are worth the calendar. Everything
else is better found than scheduled, because the upside sits in exactly the places
that do not take a reservation from abroad. A restaurant that eagerly accepts foreign
bookings months out is telling you something about who its customers are. 

The failure mode here is not hunger. It is spending an extraordinary food country executing
a spreadsheet, and arriving home having eaten well but never once walked into somewhere
because it smelled good.

# Where it leaks


It is worth saying plainly, because it contradicts the reflex an American
brings from other famous destinations: nobody in Japan is trying to take your money. There is
no tipping, no haggling, no dual-pricing, no menu with a different number for you, and the
scams that organise a trip to certain other capitals essentially do not feature.  The
corpus behind this book contains almost no warnings of that kind, which is itself the finding.

What exists instead is leakage: money that goes nowhere useful because of a decision made
early and never revisited.

# Cash, still

Cards are widely accepted in the places a first trip spends most of its money, and the
country has moved a long way toward contactless. The exceptions are specific and worth
knowing: smaller and older restaurants, market stalls, temple and shrine entry fees and
charms, and rural businesses are still commonly cash-only.  Carrying a modest amount of
cash removes the problem entirely, and the stored balance on a transit card covers a
surprising share of the small purchases besides.

# One administrative note

Tax-free shopping registration through the government’s pre-arrival web service has
been changing, and the separate registration step it used to offer is being retired. 
This is exactly the class of fact that belongs on the Field Sheet rather than in a book: check
the current process close to departure rather than trusting any printed description of it,
including this one.

# Costs you the thing you came for

The quietest failure has no line item. It is arriving in a country you will probably visit
once, with a plan assembled from forty open tabs, and spending the fortnight executing it
rather than being there. Every over-planned meal, every day trip added because it was on a
list, every hour spent extracting value from a pass is a small subtraction from the trip you
imagined when you booked. The book is mostly arithmetic because the arithmetic is what buys
back the attention.

# What this book will not tell you


Four things this book deliberately does not do. Each is a thing most guides
lead with, and each is a thing we would have to get wrong in order to include.


It does not name hotels. We have not slept in them. An assessment of a room we have
not been in is a repackaged booking-site listing with a confident voice on top, and you can
read those for free. What the book does instead is tell you which station to sleep near and
why, which is the part that survives a change of ownership.

It does not name restaurants. A list of restaurants is wrong within a season — the
chef moves, the queue finds it, the lease ends. The rule that generated the list is still
right in five years, so the book gives you the rule. Decision 07 is that argument in full.

It does not print current prices. Prices belong in the Field Sheet, which is dated
and re-issued. A price set in a book is a claim that stops being true quietly, and the reader
has no way to tell when it did.

It does not republish anyone's ratings. Aggregate scores from the booking platforms
are contractually and editorially radioactive, and reprinting them would add nothing you
cannot see for yourself.


There is a fifth refusal that belongs here too, because it shaped this edition: where the
corpus behind this book was thin on a subject, we left the subject out rather than writing
around the gap. A chapter assembled to fill a page is detectable, and it devalues the chapters
that were earned.

# The country, not the plan


There is a moment, usually on the second or third morning, when the planning
stops mattering. It tends to arrive somewhere ordinary — a station platform at seven in the
morning, everyone moving in the same direction without hurry, a vending machine humming, the
train arriving at the second it said it would. Nothing about it is a sight. It is just a
country working, at a level of consideration that is difficult to explain to someone who has
not stood in it.

That is the thing the arithmetic is protecting. Every decision in this book exists to
subtract a small anxiety from the fortnight so that more of it is available for the parts
that are not decidable in advance: the walk you took because the light was good, the counter
with six seats you went into because it was raining, the temple you had entirely to yourself
at eight in the morning because you were awake and nobody else was.

Work the sums out before you go. Then put the book down and stop optimising — the country
is better than any plan you can make of it.

# What does a day trip cost you?

Nobody plans a trip to Japan by deciding how many days not to spend in Tokyo. Yet that is the decision a day trip makes, and it is made by accretion — Hakone because of the Fuji view, Nara because of the deer, Nikko because it was on a list — until a five-day trip built around two cities has barely two days left standing in either of them.
The substitution is easiest to see in hours rather than days. A day trip to Hakone or
Nikko is roughly two hours of travel each way once you count the walk to the station, the
transfer and the wait, which removes most of a working day before you have looked at anything.
What remains is an afternoon in a place you do not know, followed by the same journey back in
the dark. {EST}

Against that, the marginal day in a city you have already learned is worth more than it
sounds. By day three in Tokyo you know which exit of which station you want, you have found a
coffee place, and you can navigate an evening without a map. That fluency is precisely what a
day trip spends, and it is the reason returning travellers cut day trips first.
Under a week, take none. At ten days, take two. A day trip is not an addition to the trip, it is a substitution: the Shinkansen to Hakone and back is three hours of daylight before you have looked at anything. The test is not whether the destination is worth seeing — they all are — but whether you would rather spend that day somewhere new or spend it getting better at the city you already chose. First-timers reliably answer that question wrong in the planning and right in hindsight.
# Does the national rail pass pay for itself?

For twenty years the Japan Rail Pass was the first thing an American bought and the advice was sound. In October 2023 the seven-day ordinary pass went from about ¥29,650 to ¥50,000 — a rise of roughly seventy per cent in one step — and a great deal of the English-language internet has never been revised. The pass did not merely get more expensive. It stopped being the default answer.
Two details sit on the credit side and are worth naming, because the arithmetic above
is deliberately unkind to the pass. It also covers local JR lines inside the cities and the
airport express from Narita, which is real value that is easy to forget when you are counting
only the long legs. {EST} And it converts every journey into a decision you no longer have to
make, which has a convenience worth something even when the money does not work.

The honest counter is that neither of those closes a ¥22,000 gap. If your route makes two
long journeys, you would need the incidental local travel to be worth more than most first
trips generate before the pass draws level, and the trips where it does draw level are
precisely the ones that were already going to reach four legs.
Count your long journeys before you buy, not after. Divide the pass price by a Tokyo–Kyoto fare and it needs roughly four legs of that distance to break even — about two round trips inside seven days. The classic first trip makes two. The deeper cost is not the ¥50,000: it is that a pass already paid for quietly argues for more legs, and the route bends to justify the purchase instead of the purchase following the route.
# Which card will load on an American phone?

Japanese city transit offers three ways to pay and most visitors default to whichever one they read about first. That is a survivable mistake. The unsurvivable one is arriving with a phone that cannot top itself up, discovering it at a Shinjuku gate at nine in the morning, and losing the first afternoon to a problem that takes four minutes to solve on your own sofa.
The reason this fails so specifically is that the refusal happens inside Apple Wallet
rather than at the card network, so the error you see describes nothing useful and your bank
shows no declined transaction to investigate. {SUR} People conclude the card is broken, or that
their phone is, and try the same card repeatedly at a gate.

There is a second failure that looks identical and is not your card at all: a nightly
maintenance window during which top-ups fail for everyone, running until roughly four in the
morning Japan time. {SUR} A failure at two in the morning is a clock problem, not a payment
problem, and the fix is to wait rather than to start deleting and re-adding cards.
Create the card at home, load it with the card you intend to use, and watch the top-up complete before you fly. A US Visa is refused inside Apple Wallet while Mastercard and Amex go through, which is not documented anywhere official and produces a meaningless error rather than an explanation. If every card you own is Visa, the answer is not to keep retrying it at the gate: it is a physical card from a station machine, fed cash, which sidesteps the card networks entirely.
# When should you come, and what does the date cost?

Cherry blossom is the reason most people first think about Japan and the worst single week to build a plan around. The bloom holds for roughly a week in any one city, the forecast that predicts it does not exist until February, and the rooms you would need were gone in October. You are asked to commit six months before anyone can tell you whether the thing you are committing to will be there.
What makes the spring window genuinely hard is not the crowds but the order the
decisions arrive in. The rooms must be committed roughly six months out; the forecast that
would tell you whether to commit does not exist until February. {EST} You are not choosing
between a good bet and a bad one, you are choosing whether to bet at all, months before the
information exists.

Autumn inverts every term of that. The colour holds for weeks rather than a week, the
weather is settled rather than volatile, and a two-to-three month booking lead is short enough
that you are deciding with most of the information already in hand. The spectacle is quieter;
the planning is a different activity altogether.
If the dates are yours to choose, take late November. Autumn colour lasts weeks rather than days, the weather is settled, the booking lead is a third of spring's, and you are not paying peak prices for a probability. Cherry blossom is worth the gamble on a second trip, when the cities themselves are no longer new and losing the flowers costs you an image rather than the trip. Choose the season deliberately; it decides the booking deadline, which decides everything downstream of it.
# Which station should you sleep next to?

In a compact European capital the question is which neighbourhood you want to walk out into. Tokyo does not work like that. It is a transit graph before it is a set of neighbourhoods, and the practical question is not which district charms you on a map but which line you will be standing on at eight in the morning and eleven at night.
The Yamanote rule works because of what a first trip does rather than because of
anything about the neighbourhoods. Almost every destination on a standard Tokyo list sits on
or one stop from that loop, so a room near any of its stations turns most journeys into a
single ride with no transfer. {EST} A cheaper room three transfers away charges the difference
back twice a day, in the currency of a trip that is short.

Kyoto has to be reasoned about separately, and travellers who apply Tokyo logic there get
it wrong in a specific way. Kyoto has two subway lines and its temples are scattered around
the edges of the city, which is why the buses do the real work. {EST} A hotel chosen for
subway access can be further from the day's sights than one chosen for a bus corridor.
Sleep on the Yamanote loop, and in Kyoto ignore the subway entirely. Almost everything a first trip does in Tokyo touches the Yamanote line, so a room within a few minutes of any of its stations removes a daily tax you would otherwise pay in transfers. Kyoto inverts the logic: it is a bus city with two subway lines that do not go where the temples are, so proximity to a major bus corridor beats proximity to a station.
# Is the ryokan worth it, and where should it be?

The ryokan is the one night of a Japan trip people describe years later, and it is also the one most often booked in the wrong city, on the wrong night, at a price the traveller never quite compares to anything. It is not a hotel with tatami. It is dinner, breakfast, a bath and a room sold as a single evening, which makes the nightly rate the wrong number to judge it by.
The comparison people make is rate against rate, and it is the wrong one. A ryokan
at half board is a room, a multi-course dinner, breakfast and the bath, sold as one evening;
a business hotel at half the price is a room. {EST} Priced against a hotel night plus two
restaurant meals, the premium narrows to something a traveller can actually judge.

Where it goes wrong is placement rather than price. The format asks you to arrive by
mid-afternoon, change, bathe, eat at the hour the kitchen serves, and stay in. That is a
poor fit for a Kyoto night, where the city itself is the evening, and an excellent one in a
hot-spring town where there is nowhere else to be.
One night, in an onsen town, not in Kyoto. A ryokan works when the building and the bath are the evening — which means arriving by mid-afternoon and going nowhere afterwards. That is a poor fit for Kyoto, where the evening is the city itself, and an excellent fit for Hakone, Kinosaki or the Japan Sea side. Price it against a hotel room plus two restaurant meals and the premium shrinks to something defensible.
# How do you eat well without a reservation system you can use?

First-timers over-plan meals the way they over-plan sights, researching a specific restaurant for every slot on the itinerary — and in Japan that effort converts badly. The best places are small, often unlisted, frequently unbookable from abroad, and the ones that are bookable from abroad are disproportionately the ones that market to foreigners. Planning harder makes the food worse.
The reason effort converts badly here is structural. The places a first-timer would
most enjoy are small, frequently unlisted in English, and often unbookable from abroad — while
the ones that are easily bookable from abroad are disproportionately the ones that
market to foreigners. {EST} Sorting restaurants by how reachable they are from Ohio in February
selects against the thing you came for.

The layer that rewards walking in is also the layer that is hardest to research: counters
with six seats, a ticket machine at the door, one dish done for thirty years. {EST} Leaving
slots open is not a lack of planning. It is the plan that matches how the good places
actually work.
Book two meals for the whole trip and let the rest happen. Reserve the one or two dinners you would be sad to miss and leave every other slot open, because the upside in Japan sits at counters you cannot book and department-store basements no guide sends you to. The failure mode here is not eating badly — it is hard to eat badly — it is spending the trip executing a spreadsheet through a country that rewards walking in.
# Which airport, and what happens in the first six hours?

The airport looks like a price decision and behaves like an itinerary decision. Narita and Haneda are both fine airports with good transfers; the difference is what each one does to the evening you land, and a first evening lost to a ninety-minute train after eleven hours in the air is a real cost that never appears in the fare comparison.
The reason landing time outranks the airport is that the transfer is fixed and your
capacity is not. An hour saved arriving at Haneda is a real hour, but it is an hour spent by
someone who has been awake for twenty. {EST} After a late landing, the difference between a
thirty-minute transfer and a ninety-minute one is the difference between going to bed and
going to bed later.

The variable worth optimising is therefore the schedule, not the airport code. An early
afternoon arrival gives you a usable evening from either airport: a walk, a first meal, and
enough daylight to start resetting the clock. That evening is also the cheapest day of the
trip to lose, which is why nothing worth seeing should be scheduled into it.
Pay a modest premium for Haneda, and pay more attention to the landing time. Haneda is meaningfully closer and worth a small fare difference, but the variable that decides your first day is when the wheels touch — an early afternoon landing at Narita beats a late-evening landing at Haneda every time. Set the transit card up before you fly and the first hour on the ground is a train, not a queue at a machine you are too tired to read.
# How many temples before they stop working?

A first trip arrives with a list of famous names and the assumption that the constraint is time: fit in as many as the days allow. The constraint is not time. It is attention, and it runs out earlier than anyone plans for — which is why the classic Kyoto mistake is not missing something, it is seeing six things and remembering two.
The corpus word for this is temple fatigue, and it is not a joke about
tired feet. Japanese religious architecture is deliberately repetitive: the same forms, the
same materials, the same spatial logic, refined across centuries. That is precisely what
makes it extraordinary and precisely why the fifth one in a day reads as the fourth. The
distinctions that reward attention are the ones attention has stopped making. {EST}

The counter-move is not fewer days of sightseeing. It is contrast inside the day — a
market, an ordinary neighbourhood, a long lunch, a walk along a canal — because the next
temple lands properly only after something that is not a temple. Two major sites with
something unlike them in between returns more than four in sequence, and it is the same
number of hours. {EST}
Two major temples a day, three if they are spread, and never four. Put something that is not a temple between them, and take the most famous one at opening when it is still a building rather than a queue. The instinct to maximise is the thing to resist: a Kyoto day that does less is a Kyoto day you can still describe a year later, and that is the only measure of a sight that matters once you are home.
